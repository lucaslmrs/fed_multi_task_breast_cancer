## To Transfer or Not To Transfer (2005)

- **Autores:** Michael T. Rosenstein, Zvika Marx, Leslie Pack Kaelbling, Thomas G. Dietterich
- **Venue:** NIPS 2005 Workshop on Inductive Transfer: 10 Years Later (4-page workshop paper)
- **URL:** https://people.csail.mit.edu/mtr/papers/RosensteinM05c.pdf
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "negative transfer" (also "harmful transfer", transfer that "hurt[s]" / "hinder[s] performance"); its opposite is "positive transfer"

**Definição (literal).** Abstract (p.1): "With transfer learning, one set of tasks is used to bias learning and improve performance on another task. However, transfer learning may actually hinder performance if the tasks are too dissimilar. As described in this paper, one challenge for transfer learning research is to develop approaches that detect and avoid negative transfer using very little data from the target task." Section 1 (p.1-2): "We are particularly interested in the situation where an algorithm must detect, perhaps implicitly, that the inductive bias learned from the auxiliary tasks will actually hurt performance on the target task." Figure 2 caption (p.4): "Shown are the fraction of tested A-B pairs with a statistically significant transfer effect (p<0.05). Black and gray respectively denote positive and negative transfer, and white indicates no statistically significant difference."

**Local.** Abstract (p.1); Section 1 Introduction (end of p.1 to top of p.2); Figure 2 caption (p.4)

**Formulação.** There is no formal or mathematical definition. The paper defines the term by what it does: negative transfer happens for a target task B when the model trained with auxiliary data A scores significantly worse on B's held-out test set than a model trained only on B ("B-only"). The test is a significance test at p<0.05, on the log odds of a correct prediction. Written as an informal formula: NT(A->B, n_B) holds iff Perf_B(transfer(A, B_{n_B})) < Perf_B(B-only(B_{n_B})) significantly, where n_B is the amount of target training data. Here "Perf_B(...)" means B's performance; the formula is not in the paper. The effect depends on n_B and on how similar A and B are.

**Métrica.** Target-task (B) accuracy on a 100-example stratified holdout set, compared with a B-only baseline that ignores the auxiliary data. For every A-B pair of people, the paper reports whether the difference is significant (p<0.05), using the log odds of a correct prediction. This sorts the pairs into positive transfer, no significant difference and negative transfer (Fig. 2b). A second comparison sets the hierarchical model against flat naive Bayes, which pools all data (Fig. 2a). Both comparisons are repeated with 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 target training examples.

**Método.** The paper adds transfer to naive Bayes through a hierarchical Bayesian model. Tasks A and B each get their own naive Bayes model. Each pair of matching parameters (theta_i^A, theta_i^B) is drawn from a shared hyperprior P_i with unknown mean and small variance. Because the posterior can make that variance larger, the model can decouple parameters that differ between tasks. The posteriors are computed with an extension of Neal's slice sampling. The baselines are flat naive Bayes, which pools all data without distinction, and B-only.

**Resultados.** The task is predicting whether a person will accept a meeting invitation. There are 21 people (8 from a military exercise, 13 from an academic setting), 3,966 examples and 15 features. Each person in turn is the target B, and other people are the auxiliary A. (1) In Fig. 1, transfer from similar people beats B-only by about 8 accuracy points with 2 target examples (about 63% vs about 55%). Transfer from dissimilar people falls below B-only (about 48% vs about 55%). (2) In Fig. 2a, the hierarchical model is significantly better than flat pooling in most pairs (about 70-88% of them) and rarely worse. (3) In Fig. 2b, about 40% of A-B pairs show significant negative transfer against B-only when there are 2 target examples. About half show positive transfer. As target data grows to 32 examples, both the negative and the positive effects shrink, and most pairs end up with no significant difference. (4) A transfer-aware logistic regression gave qualitatively similar results.

**Limitações.** This is a 4-page workshop paper. It uses one small tabular dataset with 15 features, simple models (naive Bayes) and a single target task in each experiment. Task similarity is never measured: "similar" and "dissimilar" people are chosen after the fact from the results. There is no formal definition of negative transfer and no method for predicting it in advance; detection is left as an open challenge. It runs many pairwise significance tests with no correction for multiple comparisons. It does not cover neural networks, gradient conflict or federated aggregation. The hierarchical model reduces negative transfer compared with pooling, but it does not remove it compared with B-only.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is the historical origin of the term, and it gives the project its baseline-relative definition: negative transfer means the target task does worse than when it is trained without the other sources. In this project, "federated still underperforms local-only" is negative transfer in exactly Rosenstein's sense, with local-only as the B-only baseline. Flat FedAvg also mirrors their "flat" pooling, and the FedPer personalized parameters mirror their hierarchical decoupling. The paper defines negative transfer by outcome only. It does not attribute the cause to conflicting updates, so the project's low cancellation ratio does not contradict the existence of negative transfer. A performance deficit can come from another mechanism, such as step dilution (a smaller effective step), without any update cancellation. That supports keeping the outcome-level term ("negative transfer", federated vs local-only) separate from the mechanism-level measure (cancellation, cosine conflict). The finding that negative transfer shrinks as target data grows also predicts that small clients like BUSI are where the cost or benefit of federation is largest. Their per-pair significance labelling (positive / none / negative) is a template for per-client reporting. The project's own Wilcoxon p-values, however, must stay labelled exploratory.

## A theory of learning from different domains (2010)

- **Autores:** Shai Ben-David, John Blitzer, Koby Crammer, Alex Kulesza, Fernando Pereira, Jennifer Wortman Vaughan
- **Venue:** Machine Learning 79:151-175 (Springer, open access). Extended from NIPS 2006 (Ben-David et al.) and NIPS 2007 (Blitzer et al.)
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10994-009-5152-4 (full text read from the author PDF at https://www.alexkulesza.com/pubs/adapt_mlj10.pdf)
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** The paper never uses the phrase "negative transfer". It treats the phenomenon as domain adaptation and writes about "adaptability", the "ideal joint hypothesis", the "H-divergence" / "H Delta H-divergence", and the "phase transition" past which source data "will yield suboptimal performance". The keywords list "Domain adaptation · Transfer learning · Learning theory · Sample-selection bias".

**Definição (literal).** Sect. 6 "Optimal mixing value", p. 161: "First, if there are enough target data (specifically, if mT ≥ D^2 = d/A^2) then no source data are needed, and in fact using any source data will yield suboptimal performance. This is because the possible reduction in error due to additional source data is always less than the increase in error caused by the source data being too far from the target data. Second, even if there are few target examples, it might be the case that we do not have enough source data to justify using it, and this small amount of source data should be ignored."  Sect. 4.2, p. 157 (on lambda): "The ideal joint hypothesis explicitly embodies our notion of adaptability. When this hypothesis performs poorly, we cannot expect to learn a good target classifier by minimizing source error." Sect. 7.2, p. 165: "When enough labeled target data exists, we always prefer to use only the target data, no matter how much source data is available. Intuitively this is because any biased source domain cannot help to reduce error beyond some positive constant."

**Local.** Sect. 6, p. 161, Eq. (2) and Fig. 1 (phase transition); Sect. 4.2, p. 157 (Definition 2 and the paragraph after it); Sect. 7.2, p. 165 (Fig. 3, column c)

**Formulação.** A domain is a pair <D, f>: D is a distribution over inputs and f: X -> [0,1] is a labeling function. eps_S(h) = E_{x~D_S}|h(x) - f_S(x)|.
Def. 1 (H-divergence): d_H(D,D') = 2 sup_{h in H} |Pr_D[I(h)] - Pr_D'[I(h)]|. Lemma 2: the empirical d_H = 2(1 - min_h [error of a domain classifier that separates source (label 0) from target (label 1)]).
Def. 2: h* = argmin_h eps_S(h) + eps_T(h), and lambda = eps_S(h*) + eps_T(h*).
Def. 3: H Delta H = {h XOR h' : h, h' in H}. Lemma 3: |eps_S(h,h') - eps_T(h,h')| <= 1/2 d_{H Delta H}(D_S,D_T).
Thm 2: eps_T(h) <= eps_S(h) + 1/2 d^_{H Delta H}(U_S,U_T) + 4 sqrt((2d log(2m') + log(2/delta))/m') + lambda.
Thm 3 (alpha-error, eps^_alpha = alpha eps^_T + (1-alpha) eps^_S, with a beta fraction of target samples): eps_T(h^) <= eps_T(h*_T) + 4 sqrt(alpha^2/beta + (1-alpha)^2/(1-beta)) sqrt((2d log(2(m+1)) + 2 log(8/delta))/m) + 2(1-alpha)(1/2 d^_{H Delta H} + 4 sqrt((2d log(2m') + log(8/delta))/m') + lambda).
Eq. (1): f(alpha) = 2B sqrt(alpha^2/beta + (1-alpha)^2/(1-beta)) + 2(1-alpha)A, where A is the total divergence and B is about sqrt(d/m).
Eq. (2): alpha* = 1 if m_T >= D^2, and min{1, nu} otherwise, with D = sqrt(d)/A and nu = m_T/(m_T+m_S) * (1 + m_S / sqrt(D^2(m_S+m_T) - m_S m_T)).
Multi-source case: Thm 4 gives eps_T(h^) <= eps_T(h*_T) + 2 sqrt(sum_j alpha_j^2/beta_j * (d log(2m) - log delta)/(2m)) + sum_j alpha_j (2 lambda_j + d_{H Delta H}(D_j, D_T)). Thm 5 gives eps_T(h^) <= eps_T(h*_T) + 4 sqrt(...) + 2 gamma_alpha + d_{H Delta H}(D_alpha, D_T), with gamma_alpha = min_h {eps_T(h) + sum_j alpha_j eps_j(h)}.

**Métrica.** Nothing here measures "negative transfer" directly. The relevant quantities are: (1) domain divergence, as the empirical H-divergence from a domain classifier (Lemma 2). In experiments it is approximated by zeta(U_S,U_T), the score of a linear classifier trained to tell unlabeled source from target (a lower bound on d_{H Delta H}). (2) lambda, the joint error of the best single hypothesis on both domains. It cannot be estimated without target labels and is assumed negligible in the experiments. (3) Target test error as a function of the source/target weight alpha. Harm shows up when the alpha that minimizes the bound or the empirical error is 1, which means any source weight increases target error.

**Método.** Pure learning theory with an illustrative experiment. (i) Theorem 2 bounds target error by source error + 1/2 H Delta H-divergence (which can be estimated from unlabeled samples) + lambda. (ii) A uniform-convergence bound (Thm 3) covers ERM on a convex combination alpha of empirical target and source errors. Minimizing that bound over alpha gives the optimal mixing weight in closed form (Eq. 2) as a function of m_T, m_S, VC dimension d and divergence A. (iii) Extension to N sources with domain weights alpha_j (Thms 4-5), using either pairwise divergences or the divergence between the target and the alpha-weighted source mixture. (iv) Check on Amazon sentiment data (apparel as target) with a weighted Huber/hinge loss on linear classifiers: compare the shape of the approximated bound (Eq. 3) with the empirical error-vs-alpha curves.

**Resultados.** - The target error of a source-trained hypothesis depends on source error, the classifier-induced divergence between the marginals, and lambda. If lambda is large, no amount of source data helps.
- The optimal alpha bound is always at least as tight as using only the source (alpha=0), only the target (alpha=1), or uniform instance weighting (alpha=beta). If the domains are identical (A=0), alpha* = beta (uniform weighting).
- Two phase transitions (Sect. 6, Fig. 1). (a) If m_T >= d/A^2, alpha* = 1, so any source data hurts. In their example (d=1601, A=0.715) the threshold is about 3,130 target samples. (b) With few target samples, a small amount of source data should still be ignored until there is enough of it.
- Empirically (Fig. 3): error-vs-alpha curves are roughly convex, and the bound-minimizing alpha also gives low test error. More distant sources give higher target error. More target data raises the best alpha. With enough target data, distant source data stops helping no matter how much there is.
- Multi-source: uniform convergence is fastest when alpha_j = beta_j (equal per-sample weighting). This trades off against down-weighting distant sources, and a non-uniform alpha helps when a mixture of sources approximates the target better than any single source.

**Limitações.** - Binary classification only, with 0-1-style loss, VC-dimension complexity and a single hypothesis class. It does not cover segmentation, deep nets or representation learning (the bounds are vacuous for modern network sizes). The authors say even their small linear setup is "much too small for the bound to yield meaningful numerical results".
- lambda needs target labels and is ignored in the experiments. The estimated divergence zeta is a lower bound, while the theorem needs an upper bound (and computing it exactly is intractable).
- It analyses ERM on a fixed weighted data mixture with data pooled centrally. Optimization dynamics, gradient conflict, parameter averaging, local steps and personalized heads are not modelled.
- There is no explicit definition or measurement of "negative transfer". The harm is implicit, as the regime where alpha*=1.
- It assumes a single target and fixed hypotheses. It says nothing about multi-task settings where each client is its own target.
- Open problems the authors list: data-dependent (e.g. Rademacher) bounds, better divergence measures, and algorithms that choose the multi-source alpha.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is the canonical theory for when another domain's data helps or hurts. It is the formal backbone for the "negative transfer" section, even though the paper never uses the term. In the FedPer setting each client or dataset is a target, and the other datasets are weighted sources through the aggregation weights. Thms 4-5 match hierarchical FedAvg with dataset_weights as alpha_j. The bound's three ingredients map to the project as follows:
(1) d_{H Delta H} between BUSI, ISIC, TCGA-LGG and SIIM-ACR is large, since the modalities differ (and Lemma 2 gives a cheap proxy: a domain classifier on trunk features).
(2) lambda / gamma_alpha asks whether one shared trunk can serve all domains, which FedPer eases by personalizing stems and heads.
(3) The phase-transition result (if m_T >= d/A^2, any source weight hurts) offers a principled explanation for why federated can underperform local-only on datasets with enough local data (ISIC, SIIM-ACR, TCGA-LGG). Small BUSI is the regime where source data can still help.
The theory is about data and hypothesis mismatch, not about update geometry. So it predicts harm even without cancellation, which fits the finding of little inter-dataset cancellation. The paper says nothing about the cancellation ratio 1 - ||sum w_i Delta_i|| / sum w_i ||Delta_i|| or about step dilution. Those are optimization effects that the ERM-based bound does not model. The student should present step dilution as a separate mechanism (an optimization or effective-learning-rate effect), distinct from Ben-David-style distribution mismatch, and not label it "negative transfer" in this paper's sense. Practical uses: weigh the aggregation weights against this paper's alpha* logic, and estimate an empirical H-divergence between datasets' trunk features to describe domain distance.

## Characterizing and Avoiding Negative Transfer (2019)

- **Autores:** Zirui Wang, Zihang Dai, Barnabás Póczos, Jaime Carbonell (Carnegie Mellon University)
- **Venue:** CVPR 2019 (arXiv:1811.09751v4, 05 Oct 2019)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09751
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "negative transfer"; "negative transfer condition (NTC)"; "negative transfer gap (NTG)"

**Definição (literal).** Section 3 ("Rethink Negative Transfer", paragraph "Negative Transfer"), item 1: "Therefore, to study negative transfer, one should focus on a specific algorithm at a time and compare its performance with and without the source-domain data. Hence, we define the negative transfer condition (NTC) for any algorithm A as R_{P_T}(A(S,T)) > R_{P_T}(A(∅,T)). (3) For convenience, we also define the negative transfer gap (NTG) as a quantifiable measure of negative transfer: R_{P_T}(A(S,T)) − R_{P_T}(A(∅,T)), (4) and we say that negative transfer occurs if the negative transfer gap is positive and vice versa." The informal definition the paper starts from (Sec. 3) is: "A widely accepted description of negative transfer [20, 35] is stated as 'transferring knowledge from the source can have a negative impact on the target learner'." The abstract says: "when transferring knowledge from a less related source, it may inversely hurt the target performance, a phenomenon known as negative transfer."

**Local.** Section 3, Eq. (3) for NTC and Eq. (4) for NTG; the expected target risk is Eq. (1); the rejected algorithm-agnostic definitions are Eq. (2) and Appendix A, Eqs. (12)-(14); the formal Definition 1 is Appendix A, Eq. (15).

**Formulação.** Expected target risk (Eq. 1): R_{P_T}(h) := E_{x,y~P_T}[ℓ(h(x),y)]. NTC (Eq. 3): R_{P_T}(A(S,T)) > R_{P_T}(A(∅,T)). NTG (Eq. 4): NTG = R_{P_T}(A(S,T)) − R_{P_T}(A(∅,T)); NTG > 0 means negative transfer occurred. Definition 1 (Appendix A, Eq. 15): R_{P_T}(A(S,T)) > R_{P_T}(A(∅,T)) ≥ min_{A'} R_{P_T}(A'(∅,T)). The paper rejects two alternatives. (a) The algorithm-agnostic version min_A R(A(S,T)) > min_{A'} R(A'(∅,T)) (Eq. 12) can never hold, because an algorithm that ignores S matches the RHS (Eq. 13). (b) Comparing against the best target-only algorithm, R(A(S,T)) > min_{A'} R(A'(∅,T)) (Eqs. 2 and 14), cannot be measured, and it flags as negative transfer an algorithm that never uses the source. In the experiments NTG is computed in accuracy: accuracy of the target-only baseline A_T minus accuracy of the transfer method.

**Métrica.** NTG is measured on target test data as accuracy(target-only version of the same algorithm) − accuracy(the algorithm trained with source + target). For example, for DANN the baseline is DANN_T, which "treats labeled target data as 'source' data and uses unlabeled data as usual" (Sec. 5.2). Positive NTG means negative transfer. Accuracies are averaged over five random repeats. The authors control the divergence factor with synthetic source perturbations: ε_x applies input noise to source samples (rotation, salt-and-pepper, flipping) and ε_y assigns random labels to source samples. They control the target factor through L%, the share of target training data that is labeled.

**Método.** The paper first analyses negative transfer through three factors. (1) The algorithm: negative transfer must be defined relative to a specific algorithm. (2) Divergence between the joint distributions P_S(X,Y) and P_T(X,Y): "regardless of the algorithm choice, the distribution shift is the actual root to negative transfer". (3) The amount of labeled target data, which has a mixed effect: it strengthens the target-only baseline, which makes negative transfer "relative", but it also helps find the shared structure. The authors then propose the "discriminator gate". It applies importance reweighting with the density ratio P_T(x,y)/P_S(x,y), which a joint (x,y) GAN discriminator estimates as ω = SG(D/(1−D)) (Eqs. 8-9). On top of this they use an augmented adversarial loss that matches both the marginal and the joint distributions (Eq. 10), with the overall objective argmin_{F,C} argmax_D L^gate_CLF − μ L^aug_ADV (Eq. 11). The gate is added to six deep domain-adaptation methods: DAN, DCORAL, DANN, ADDA, PADA and GTA.

**Resultados.** Divergence factor: on Office-31 (Table 1), NTG grows as the perturbation ε grows, and at L%=10% its sign flips. Under pure covariate shift (ε_y=0) on W→D, no negative transfer occurs even at high ε_x (Table 3). Target factor: at a fixed ε, NTG grows with L%. With L%=0 (unsupervised domain adaptation), NTG stays negative even at ε=0.9, so "negative transfer is relative and it depends on target labeled data". Algorithm factor (Table 2: ε=0.7, L%=10%, at most 3 labels per class): KMM has a smaller NTG than TCA, GTA is the most robust, and MMD-based methods (DAN, NTG avg 0.1) have a smaller NTG than adversarial ones (DANN 3.0, ADDA larger). The authors suggest that stronger domain matching leads to more severe negative transfer. The gate improves every base method on every task: DANN goes from 47.0% average with NTG +3.0 to 57.4% with NTG −7.4 (+10.4 points); DAN gains +4.8 and DCORAL +5.1. Gains are largest on Office-Home and VisDA. With a degenerate source (ε=1.0), the gated model matches DANN_T. In the ablation on A→D (Table 4), the gate and the joint matching each help, and their combination is best. At high ε the gated model beats an oracle that removes the perturbed source samples.

**Limitações.** The setting is single-source to single-target domain adaptation for classification only. There are no multiple sources, no multi-task learning, no federated training and no segmentation. The divergence is mostly synthetic (noise injection and random label corruption on standard benchmarks), so real-world shifts such as medical cross-modality shift are not studied. The definition is in terms of expected risk, but in practice it is estimated as a single accuracy difference on a test split, with no statistical treatment beyond ±std over five repeats. The definition attributes the gap to "using the source data" under a fixed algorithm. It assumes the target-only run A(∅,T) is a fair matched counterfactual, with the same architecture, budget and hyperparameters. The paper does not decompose the gap into optimization effects (for example, step size or budget) versus distribution-conflict effects. The gate needs a joint discriminator and some labeled target data. The authors admit only that "Understanding negative transfer in more complex transfer tasks and settings should be addressed in a future research" (Sec. 6). There is no dedicated limitations section.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is the canonical formal definition to adopt. Per client and per task, the project's "federated vs local-only" comparison is exactly the NTG of Eq. 4. The target is the client's own data T, the source is the other clients' updates S, the algorithm A is FedPer with the same model, seeds, budget and evaluation, and A(∅,T) is the local-only arm (federated.standalone: True), which the repository already pairs with matched seeds and budget. Federated underperforming local-only therefore means NTG > 0 for those clients, which is negative transfer in the Wang et al. sense. Three caveats follow from the paper.
(1) Algorithm dependence: NTG is defined for a fixed A. Comparisons are only valid when both arms share the local-training budget mode, which AGENTS.md already requires. Changing aggregation or budget changes A and hence the NTG.
(2) Root cause: the paper attributes negative transfer to joint-distribution divergence and does not consider an optimization mechanism. The project's cancellation ratio 1 − ||Σ wᵢΔᵢ|| / Σ wᵢ||Δᵢ|| and the pairwise cosines are a gradient- or update-level, mechanistic proxy, a different object from NTG. Low inter-dataset cancellation combined with positive NTG suggests that the observed negative transfer is not driven by conflicting update directions. The project's step-dilution explanation (an aggregated step about 10x smaller) is an optimization-budget effect that Wang et al.'s framework would simply fold into "the algorithm factor". The thesis should state explicitly that NTG measures the outcome, while cancellation and step dilution are candidate mechanisms.
(3) Target-data factor: clients with more labeled data (ISIC, SIIM-ACR) have stronger local-only baselines and are predicted to suffer larger NTG. The data-scarce BUSI clients are where positive transfer is most plausible. Reporting NTG per client against client size would test this.
The discriminator-gate idea (importance-weighting sources by relatedness) maps loosely onto similarity-weighted or personalized aggregation in FL. The paper's numbers are single-source domain-adaptation benchmarks, so they are not a state-of-the-art reference for FL performance. Per project rules, any per-client NTG differences should be reported as exploratory and not called statistically significant.

## Clustered Federated Learning: Model-Agnostic Distributed Multi-Task Optimization under Privacy Constraints (2019)

- **Autores:** Felix Sattler, Klaus-Robert Mueller, Wojciech Samek
- **Venue:** arXiv:1910.01991v1 (4 Oct 2019). Later published in IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks and Learning Systems 32(8), 2021. The journal venue comes from background knowledge; the arXiv page does not state it.
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "congruent" / "incongruent" clients (data-generating distributions); "data distributions diverge"; "Federated Learning yields suboptimal results". The words "negative transfer" and "interference" do not appear in the paper.

**Definição (literal).** Assumption 1 ("Federated Learning"): "There exists a parameter configuration θ∗∈Θ, that (locally) minimizes the risk on all clients' data generating distributions at the same time" [R_i(θ*) ≤ R_i(θ) ∀θ∈B_ε(θ*), i=1..m]. "In the following we will call a set of clients and their data generating distributions φ congruent (with respect to f and l) if they satisfy Assumption 1 and incongruent if they don't." (Section I, Introduction). Abstract: "Albeit it's popularity, it has been observed that Federated Learning yields suboptimal results if the local clients' data distributions diverge."

**Local.** Section I (Introduction), Assumption 1 and the definition that follows it; the Abstract for the "yields suboptimal results" sentence. The criterion is in Section II (eq. 13, Theorem 1 eqs. 15-21, Corollary 1 eqs. 24-25, Definition 2), Section II-A (eqs. 32-33) and Section III (eqs. 34-35, 39; Algorithm 3).

**Formulação.** Congruence (Assumption 1): ∃θ* such that R_i(θ*) ≤ R_i(θ) for all θ ∈ B_ε(θ*) and all i=1..m. Incongruent = Assumption 1 is violated. Assumption 2 (CFL): there is a partition C={c_1..c_k} of the clients such that each cluster satisfies Assumption 1.

Key geometric fact (eq. 13): at a stationary point of the FL objective, cos(∇r_i, ∇r_j) = +1 when i and j share a distribution and −1 otherwise (case of two distributions).

Theorem 1 (Separation): let γ_i = ||∇R_I(i)(θ*) − ∇r_i(θ*)|| / ||∇R_I(i)(θ*)|| and H_ij = −γ_iγ_j + sqrt(1−γ_i²)·sqrt(1−γ_j²). Then α_intra^min ≥ min_{I(i)=I(j)} H_ij, and α_cross^max ≤ cos(π/(k−1))·H + sin(π/(k−1))·sqrt(1−H²) when H ≥ cos(π/(k−1)), else 1.

Definition 2 (separation gap): g(α) = α_intra^min − α_cross^max. If g > 0, the bi-partition that minimizes the maximum cross-cluster cosine is correct (Corollary 1).

When to split (eqs. 32-33): split only if ||Σ_i (|D_i|/|D_c|) ∇r_i(θ*)|| < ε1 (the aggregate is near stationary) AND max_i ||∇r_i(θ*)|| > ε2 > 0 (some clients are individually far from stationary). Split condition eq. 39: γ_max < sqrt((1 − α_cross^max)/2). Recurse on each cluster at most k−1 times.

**Métrica.** Pairwise cosine similarity between client gradients, in practice between client weight updates Δθ_i after 3 local epochs. The paper derives max cross-cluster cosine α_cross^max, min intra-cluster cosine α_intra^min and the separation gap g(α) from them. The diagnostic for incongruence is the norm of the weighted mean update (small, so the federation has converged) against the max individual update norm (large, so clients still disagree). This pair of quantities is a norm-based way of detecting that client updates cancel in the aggregate.

**Método.** CFL is a post-processing step on top of FedAvg. Standard FL runs until the weighted mean update is near zero (||mean Δ|| < ε1). If some clients still have large updates (max ||Δ_i|| > ε2), the server computes the pairwise cosine similarity matrix of client weight updates and bi-partitions the clients so that the maximum cross-cluster cosine is minimized. Each cluster then continues FL separately, and splitting recurses until no cluster meets the criteria. The method is model-agnostic and needs no change to the FL protocol. Because cosine similarity and norms are invariant to orthonormal transforms, it also works with transformed (encrypted) updates.

**Resultados.** Setups: 20 clients in 4 clusters, with label swaps on MNIST, label permutations on CIFAR-10 with a 5-layer CNN, and topic sub-corpora on Ag-News with a 2-layer LSTM. All clients train 3 local epochs with batch size 100.
- Data needed per client: about 20 points per client on MNIST give a correct split with weight updates; CIFAR needs about 500.
- Weight updates vs. gradients: weight updates separate clusters better than full gradients, with less data and earlier in training (about 10 rounds).
- CIFAR-10 permuted labels: FL plateaus near 20% accuracy. After three splits (rounds 50, 100, 150), accuracy roughly doubles to close to 60% for all clients.
- Ag-News: FL stalls at a perplexity of about 42-43. After splits at rounds 30, 60 and 90, CFL reaches below 36 and recovers the four true topic clusters.
- Congruent groups: CFL does not split them.

**Limitações.** - The guarantees assume FL has converged to a stationary point of the joint objective and only "mild assumptions"; approximate convergence works in practice. Clustering is therefore delayed until FedAvg stalls, which wastes rounds.
- Incongruence is binary: a group of clients is either congruent or not. The paper does not quantify partial or graded harm and does not use the term "negative transfer".
- The experiments use synthetic label-swap and permutation conflicts, where conditional distributions are directly contradictory and cross-cluster cosines approach −1. This is far more adversarial than real heterogeneous medical domains.
- The output is hard clusters, each with a full separate model. There is no partial sharing such as FedPer heads, so the shared-trunk-plus-personalized-head setting is not analyzed.
- Separation needs enough data per client (about 500 points on CIFAR).
- There is no explicit limitations section. Compressed updates are left to future work.
- Cosine on whole-model weight updates conflates layers; there is no per-block analysis.

**Relevância para o projeto.** Strong conceptual reference, but it is foundational, not the current state of the art.

1. Definition: CFL frames client conflict as incongruence, meaning no single θ is simultaneously a local optimum for all clients (Assumption 1). This is the federated analogue of negative transfer, defined at the level of the optimization objective rather than measured as a performance drop. The project can cite it as the formal "one shared model cannot serve all clients" definition. The term "negative transfer" itself must come from other literature.

2. Measurement: CFL's two diagnostics map directly onto the project's logs.
   - Pairwise cosine of client weight updates is the project's trunk-delta cosine.
   - The criterion ||weighted mean Δ|| small while max ||Δ_i|| large is a norm-based statement of cancellation. It is closely related to the project's ratio 1 − ||Σ w_i Δ_i|| / Σ w_i ||Δ_i||: high cancellation at a stationary point is exactly the CFL split signal. In CFL the cancellation comes from antiparallel updates, with cross-cluster cosines near −1.

3. Interpretation for the finding: the project sees little inter-dataset cancellation, with cosines near the orthogonal reference rather than strongly negative. Under CFL's theory that is not evidence of incongruence of the CFL kind. Near-orthogonal updates still shrink the aggregated step, roughly by 1/sqrt(n) for equal norms. That matches the observed step dilution (about 10x smaller aggregated step) rather than conflict.
   - CFL's separation theorem says a correct split needs a positive separation gap between intra- and inter-cluster cosines. The project can test this directly: compare intra-dataset and inter-dataset cosines to see whether a dataset-wise clustering would be justified.
   - Caveat: CFL's guarantees apply only near a stationary point of the joint objective. Cosines measured early in training, or under step dilution, may not satisfy its premises.

4. Method relevance: CFL suggests a dataset-wise or cosine-driven clustered-FedPer baseline, in which the trunk is federated only within clusters. That could separate "conflict" from "dilution" explanations for why federated underperforms local-only.

## Which Tasks Should Be Learned Together in Multi-task Learning? (2020)

- **Autores:** Trevor Standley, Amir Zamir, Dawn Chen, Leonidas Guibas, Jitendra Malik, Silvio Savarese
- **Venue:** ICML 2020 (PMLR 119); arXiv:1905.07553v4
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07553
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "negative transfer"; "task cooperation and competition"; "task compatibility"; "multi-task (learning) affinity" / "between-task affinity", contrasted with "transfer learning affinity" (Taskonomy)

**Definição (literal).** "Unfortunately, the quality of predictions are often observed to suffer when a network is tasked with making multiple predictions due to a phenomenon called negative transfer. In fact, multi-task performance can suffer so much that smaller independent networks are often superior. This may be because the tasks must be learned at different rates. Or because one task may dominate the learning leading to poor performance on other tasks. Furthermore, task gradients may interfere and multiple summed losses may make the optimization landscape more difficult." (Sec. 1 Introduction, p.1). Affinity: "In order to determine the between-task affinity for multi-task learning, we took the average of our first-order relationships matrix (Table 2) and its transpose." (Sec. 4, Setting 1, p.4); Table 3 caption: "These values show the average change in the performance of two tasks when trained as a pair, relative to when they are trained separately."

**Local.** Sec. 1 Introduction (p.1) for negative transfer; Sec. 4 'Setting 1', Tables 2-3 (p.4) for multi-task affinity; Sec. 5 'Formal Problem Definition' (p.6) for the grouping objective

**Formulação.** No formal definition of negative transfer itself; it is operationalized empirically. Pairwise relationship (Table 2): for task j trained with task i, relative performance = relative change in task j's loss of a 1-SNT two-task network vs task j alone on a 1/2-SNT network (equal total compute). Affinity A_ij = (R_ij + R_ji)/2 (average of the matrix and its transpose); negative values = competition/negative transfer. Grouping problem (Sec. 5): tasks T={t1..tk}, budget b; candidate network n has cost c_n and loss L(n,t_i) (= inf if n does not solve t_i); solution S: cost(S)=sum_{n in S} c_n; L(S,t_i)=min_{n in S} L(n,t_i); L(S)=sum_{t_i in T} L(S,t_i); S_b = argmin_{S subset C0 : cost(S) <= b} L(S). NP-hard (reduction from SET-COVER), solved by branch-and-bound. HOA: per-task loss of a higher-order group estimated as the average of that task's loss across the pairwise networks containing it.

**Métrica.** Task-level outcome, not gradient/parameter geometry: relative change (%) in a task's validation/test loss when co-trained with another task (or subset) versus trained alone at matched compute (Standard Network Time, SNT = multiply-adds of the reference encoder). Symmetrized pairwise affinity; total loss of a grouping under an inference budget. Agreement between affinity matrices measured with Pearson r. Candidate set = all 2^|T|-1 task subsets (31) + 5 half-size single-task nets = 36 networks per setting.

**Método.** Exhaustive empirical study on Taskonomy (~4M images, 600 buildings, building-disjoint splits) with two 5-task sets (Set 1: semantic segmentation, depth, surface normals, SURF keypoints, Canny edges; Set 2: autoencoder, normals, occlusion edges, reshading, principal curvature) under four settings varying network capacity (Xception17 vs Xception), dataset size (3.9M vs 200k) and task set. Hard parameter sharing (shared Xception encoder + per-task lightweight decoders), unweighted mean of task losses. They train one network per task subset, then select the set of networks (each task served by its best network) minimizing total loss under an inference-time budget; two cheaper approximations: Early Stopping Approximation (ESA, ~20x cheaper) and Higher Order Approximation (HOA, predicts >=3-task groups from pairs, ~45% saving).

**Resultados.** (1) Setting 1 (small net): MTL vs equal-per-task-compute single-task nets is worse: -7.56% (2 tasks), -7.23% (3), -10.69% (4), -19.00% (5); at equal total compute 3-5-task nets are better (+4.23%, +4.86%, +0.34%) but 2-task nets still worse (-1.15%). (2) Task relationships are setting-dependent: Setting 1 vs Setting 2 pairwise relationships uncorrelated (Pearson r=0.08); capacity makes cooperation more likely; small-data setting correlates with both (r=+0.375, p=0.10; r=+0.558, p=0.01). Contrary to the common assumption, with only 200k examples 'most tasks suffer when trained with another task'. (3) No correlation between multi-task affinity and Taskonomy transfer affinity in any setting (r=-0.12, p=0.74; -0.14; -0.235, p=0.51; -0.153, p=0.674); depth and normals (most transfer-affine) are not multi-task affine. (4) 'more similar tasks don't necessarily train better together'; an auxiliary task (Normals) improved its partner in 15 of 16 models while itself performing worse — asymmetric transfer. (5) Optimal grouping beats single all-task net, independent nets, Random, Pessimal, Sener&Koltun MGDA and GradNorm in all four settings at every budget; e.g., 2.5 SNT optimal grouping beats five independent nets at 5 SNT; HOA nearly matches optimal, ESA usually competitive but worse (r=0.49 between 20%-data and final loss).

**Limitações.** Negative transfer is only characterized informally (causes listed as hypotheses: differing learning rates, task dominance, gradient interference, harder landscape) and measured as an end-performance difference, so it cannot separate mechanisms. Exponential candidate set (2^|T|-1 trained networks), costly even with ESA/HOA; HOA still trains over half the candidates with 5 tasks. Single dataset (Taskonomy, same images/domain, all tasks labelled on every image), single architecture family, single seed without error bars (justified by large test set), no hyperparameter search, uniform loss weights; ESA could not be tested at other data amounts. Centralized setting only — no data heterogeneity across silos, no federated aggregation. Loss totals not comparable across settings.

**Relevância para o projeto.** Supplies the canonical operational definition of negative transfer in MTL: co-training a task with others yields worse task performance than training it alone at matched compute — exactly the project's federated-vs-local-only comparison (federated underperforming local-only is negative transfer in this outcome sense). Crucially, the paper defines and measures it by OUTCOMES (loss change), not by update geometry; the project's cancellation ratio 1-||sum w_i Delta_i||/sum w_i||Delta_i|| and pairwise cosine are mechanism-level proxies for one hypothesized cause ('task gradients may interfere'). The paper's own list of causes includes 'one task may dominate' and tasks needing 'different rates', which is compatible with the project finding that low inter-dataset cancellation coexists with harm: the cost can be step dilution/rate mismatch rather than conflicting directions, so low cancellation does not rule out negative transfer. Its findings that affinity depends on capacity and data size (small data -> more competition), that domain/task similarity does not predict MTL affinity, and that transfer is asymmetric (a helper task can itself lose) argue for measuring per-client/per-dataset pairwise affinity directly (e.g., federating pairs/subsets of datasets BUSI/ISIC/TCGA-LGG/SIIM-ACR and computing each client's loss change vs local-only) and for a grouping/clustered-sharing design (which datasets should share a trunk) instead of one trunk for all. Caveat: their setting is centralized, single-domain, and all tasks on the same images, unlike cross-domain FedPer.

## Gradient Surgery for Multi-Task Learning (PCGrad) (2020)

- **Autores:** Tianhe Yu, Saurabh Kumar, Abhishek Gupta, Sergey Levine, Karol Hausman, Chelsea Finn
- **Venue:** NeurIPS 2020 (arXiv:2001.06782)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.06782
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "conflicting gradients", "gradient interference" / "destructive gradient interference", "tragic triad" (conflicting gradients, dominating gradients, high curvature). The full text never uses the phrase "negative transfer". "Positive transfer" appears once, in the CosReg ablation.

**Definição (literal).** Sec. 2.2, Definition 1: "We define ϕ_ij as the angle between two task gradients g_i and g_j. We define the gradients as conflicting when cos ϕ_ij < 0." Introduction: "We define two gradients to be conflicting if they point away from one another, i.e., have a negative cosine similarity. We hypothesize that such conflict is detrimental when a) conflicting gradients coincide with b) high positive curvature and c) a large difference in gradient magnitudes." Sec. 2.2: "conflicting gradients are not detrimental on their own. Indeed, simply averaging task gradients should provide the correct solution to descend the multi-task objective."

**Local.** Introduction (Sec. 1); Sec. 2.2 "The Tragic Triad", Definitions 1-3; Sec. 2.3 (PCGrad rule); Sec. 2.4, Definitions 4-5 and Theorems 1-2

**Formulação.** Def 1 (conflict): cos φ_ij < 0, where φ_ij is the angle between task gradients g_i and g_j. Def 2 (gradient magnitude similarity): Φ(g_i,g_j) = 2||g_i||_2||g_j||_2 / (||g_i||_2^2 + ||g_j||_2^2). It equals 1 for equal magnitudes and goes to 0 as they diverge. Def 3 (multi-task curvature): H(L;θ,θ') = ∫_0^1 ∇L(θ)^T ∇²L(θ + a(θ'-θ)) ∇L(θ) da. The paper calls it "high curvature" if H > C for some large constant C. PCGrad rule: if cos(g_i,g_j) < 0, set g_i ← g_i − (g_i·g_j / ||g_j||²) g_j. Tasks are processed in random order and the projected gradients are summed. Def 5: ξ(g1,g2) = (1 − cos²φ12) ||g1−g2||² / ||g1+g2||². Theorem 2 (non-convex, L-Lipschitz gradient, H(L;θ,θ^MT) ≥ ℓ||g||²): L(θ^PCGrad) ≤ L(θ^MT) if (a) cos φ12 ≤ −Φ(g1,g2), (b) ℓ ≥ ξ(g1,g2)L, and (c) t ≥ 2/(ℓ − ξ(g1,g2)L). Theorem 1 (convex case): PCGrad converges either to the optimum or to a point where cos φ12 = −1. Empirical curvature: H(L;θ_t,θ_{t+1}) = 2[L(θ_{t+1}) − L(θ_t) − ∇L(θ_t)^T(θ_{t+1} − θ_t)].

**Métrica.** Pairwise cosine similarity between per-task gradients on the shared parameters, computed per batch. A negative value counts as conflict. The paper reports the percentage of iterations with positive cosine. It also measures gradient magnitude similarity Φ and estimates multi-task curvature from consecutive iterates with a Taylor formula (Sec. 5.3, Fig. 4), tracking how often Theorem 2 conditions (a) and (b) hold. Interference is measured only indirectly through task performance (success rate, mIoU, error). There is no aggregate cancellation ratio.

**Método.** PCGrad ("gradient surgery") targets one leg of the tragic triad, gradient conflict. For each task gradient, in random task order, it checks every other task gradient. When the cosine is negative, it projects the gradient onto the normal plane of the other one. The modified gradients are summed and passed to any optimizer (SGD, Adam), with no extra hyperparameters. Non-conflicting gradients are left unchanged so positive interactions survive. The method is model-agnostic; it was applied to the shared parameters only (e.g., with routing networks and MTAN).

**Resultados.** Meta-World MT10/MT50 with SAC: PCGrad solves all 10 MT10 tasks and about 70% of MT50 tasks. A single SAC policy and a multi-head policy each fail to learn half the skills. Independent SAC agents reach the same level but need about 2M (MT10) and 15M (MT50) more samples. CIFAR-100 (20 tasks): PCGrad alone gets 71% accuracy, beating cross-stitch and independent training; PCGrad plus routing networks adds +2.8% absolute. NYUv2: MTAN+PCGrad is best in 8 of 9 metrics across segmentation, depth and normals. CelebA (40 tasks): lower average error than Sener and Koltun (MGDA-UB). Ablations: changing only the direction or only the magnitude, and GradNorm, all do worse than full PCGrad; the magnitude-only variant is much worse. PCGrad beats CosReg, which pushes the cosine toward 0. Sec. 5.3: multi-task curvature stays positive and grows during training, and conflict (condition a) holds most of the time until Task 2 starts being learned.

**Limitações.** The authors admit conflict alone is not harmful; their theory only gives sufficient conditions for a one-step improvement in the two-task case. Convergence can stall or slow where cos φ = −1 (the authors argue SGD noise makes this unlikely). The method needs one gradient per task per step, with O(T²) projections and memory cost. The projection result depends on task order (randomised to be symmetric only in expectation). The tragic-triad evidence is mostly a 2-D toy landscape plus one two-task RL pair. The analysis is centralised and per-step, using exact task gradients on one model: it has no clients, no multi-step local updates and no aggregation weights. It never formally defines negative transfer, and it does not separate magnitude dilution from directional cancellation in the averaged update. The authors list extensions (meta-learning, continual learning, NLP) as future work.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This paper is the source of the cosine-based interference measurement the project uses, but its claim is narrower than "negative cosine = negative transfer". PCGrad says negative cosine (conflict) hurts only together with large magnitude differences and high curvature, and that "simply averaging task gradients should provide the correct solution" otherwise. That supports the project finding: low inter-dataset cancellation plus near-orthogonal trunk deltas does not by itself predict harm.

It maps onto the project's two measures as follows. The pairwise cosine between client trunk deltas is a federated, multi-step counterpart of Def. 1 (client pseudo-gradients instead of per-batch task gradients). The cancellation ratio 1 − ||Σ w_i Δ_i|| / Σ w_i ||Δ_i|| is not in this paper; it combines the cosine leg with the magnitude leg (Φ) of the triad.

The step-dilution observation (an aggregated step about 10x smaller) matches PCGrad's own ablation that magnitude matters as well as direction. It also matches the Theorem 1 failure mode: opposing updates shrink the combined step. To follow PCGrad, the project could also report Φ (magnitude similarity) between client deltas and per-pair conflict rates, not just mean cosine.

Caveats when citing it: (i) the paper never uses the term "negative transfer", so cite it for "gradient conflict/interference", not as a definition of negative transfer; (ii) the project's orthogonal reference matters because Def. 1 uses a sign threshold of 0, which in high dimensions gives near-zero cosines that Def. 1 cannot separate from noise; (iii) PCGrad-style projection could be applied server-side to client deltas (as in later FL work such as FedGH and FedMGDA), but this paper does not test that.

## Salvaging Federated Learning by Local Adaptation (2020)

- **Autores:** Tao Yu, Eugene Bagdasaryan, Vitaly Shmatikov (Cornell University)
- **Venue:** arXiv preprint (cs.LG). v1 Feb 2020; v3 (read here) 3 Mar 2022. No conference or journal is named in the paper. Code: github.com/ebagdasa/federated_adaptation
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.04758
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** The paper never uses the words "negative transfer". It describes the phenomenon as a lack of "incentive to participate": participants "gain no benefit from FL" because "the federated model is less accurate on their data than the models they can train locally on their own". Related phrases it uses: "disincentivized participants", "destroy individual accuracy", "accuracy for individual participants", "tail" vs "head" participants. The fix is called "local adaptation".

**Definição (literal).** Section 1 (Introduction), paragraph 3: "Users have an incentive to participate in federated learning only if federated models are more accurate than the models they can train independently on their own data." Abstract: "many participants gain no benefit from FL because the federated model is less accurate on their data than the models they can train locally on their own." Section 5: "rational users need an incentive to participate. For example, they benefit if the federated model is more accurate than the models they can train locally on their own data." Also Section 5: "an aggregated model may perform well on the global test data but poorly on an individual participant's test data, thus removing their main incentive to participate."

**Local.** Abstract; Section 1 (Introduction, paragraph 3); Section 5 ("Privacy and robustness destroy individual accuracy", paragraphs 1 and 3). The paper defines no equation for the phenomenon. The only equations are FedAvg (Eq. 1), the DP guarantee and DP aggregation (Eq. 2-3), median aggregation (Eq. 4), the EWC adaptation loss (Eq. 5) and the KD adaptation loss (Eq. 6).

**Formulação.** The paper gives no formal definition; it is an operational comparison done per participant. Implied criterion, for participant i with local test set D_i^test: participant i has no incentive (is harmed) iff Acc(G^T; D_i^test) < Acc(L_i; D_i^test), where G^T is the final federated model and L_i is a model trained from scratch on D_i only. The reported quantity is Acc(G^T) - Acc(L_i) per participant (Fig. 1), and after adaptation Acc(A_i) - Acc(L_i) (Fig. 2) and Acc(A_i) - Acc(G^T) (Fig. 4). Aggregation follows McMahan 2018: G^t = G^{t-1} + (eta/m) * sum_i (P_i^t - G^{t-1}) (Eq. 1); DP variant: G^t = G^{t-1} + (eta/m) * sum_i Clip(P_i^t - G^{t-1}, S) + N(0, sigma) (Eq. 3); robust variant: G^t = G^{t-1} + eta (P~^t - G^{t-1}), where P~ is the coordinate-wise median (Eq. 4). Adaptation losses: MTL/EWC, l(A,x) = L_cross(A,x) + sum_i (lambda/2) F_i (A_i - G^T_i)^2 with lambda = 5000 and the Fisher information F computed on a public auxiliary dataset (Eq. 5); KD, l(A,x) = alpha K^2 L_cross(A,x) + (1-alpha) KL(softmax(G^T(x)/K), softmax(A(x)/K)) with alpha = 0.95 and K = 6 (Eq. 6).

**Métrica.** Per-participant test accuracy on each participant's own held-out data, against two baselines (Section 7.2): "(1) the participant's local model and (2) the unadapted federated model, both measured on that participant's test data." Headline statistic: the count and share of participants for whom the federated (or adapted) model is less accurate than their local trained-from-scratch model. Also reported: mean accuracy gains from adaptation, and gains binned by local-model accuracy, vocabulary size and total words. Reddit uses a chronological 9:1 train/test split per user. CIFAR-10 has no per-participant test set, so a participant's accuracy is the global per-class test accuracy weighted by that participant's training class proportions.

**Método.** The paper looks at FL from the viewpoint of a single participant. It measures, per participant, whether three federated models beat a local model trained from scratch: plain FedAvg (BASIC-FED), DP FedAvg with clipping S=15 and noise sigma=0.01 (DP-FED), and coordinate-wise median aggregation (ROBUST-FED). It then evaluates purely local post-hoc adaptation of the final global model G^T, which needs no change to the FL protocol and no cooperation from other participants. The four techniques are full fine-tuning (FT), freeze-base (FB: only the top layer is fine-tuned), multi-task learning via EWC regularization toward G^T (MTL), and knowledge distillation with G^T as teacher (KD). Tasks: Reddit next-word prediction (80,000 users, 2-layer LSTM, 5,000 rounds, 100 users per round) and CIFAR-10 split over 100 clients with Dirichlet alpha=0.9 (ResNet-18, 1,000 rounds, 10 clients per round).

**Resultados.** Before adaptation (Section 5, Fig. 1): BASIC-FED is worse than the local model for 9.22% of Reddit users (7,377) and for 1 of 100 CIFAR clients. DP-FED is worse for 21.16% (16,931) on Reddit and 11% on CIFAR. ROBUST-FED is worse for the majority on Reddit, 52.15% (41,720), and for 34% on CIFAR. After adaptation (Section 7.1): mean gains on Reddit are 2.32 / 2.12 / 2.12 points (BASIC / DP / ROBUST), which offset the DP loss of -1.42 and the robust-aggregation loss of -2.81. On CIFAR the gains are 2.98 / 6.83 / 6.34, offsetting DP -7.83 and robust -11.89. The share of Reddit users for whom the adapted model still underperforms the local model drops to 0.04% (28) for BASIC, 1.85% (1,465) for DP and 18.72% (14,809) for ROBUST. On CIFAR the adapted model beats every local model. The largest gains go to "tail" participants with poor local models. "Head" participants, whose idiosyncratic data made the local model beat the global one, gain less but enough to make participation pay. FB is best for BASIC-FED; MTL is best for tail participants under DP and ROBUST. Some participants never recover under median aggregation, which the authors conjecture shuts out their contribution entirely. Appendix A.2: retraining without the 7,377 disincentivized users barely changes the global model (mean 20.008% vs 20.021%). Appendix A.1: averaging the fine-tuned models again can raise global accuracy (90.44% to 92.64% with 50 fine-tuning epochs) but can also lower it (89.22% with 100 epochs).

**Limitações.** The paper never names or formalizes negative transfer, and has no gradient- or update-level measure of conflict. Harm is only inferred from end accuracy against a local baseline. It is single-task and single-domain: next-word prediction and CIFAR-10 with mild Dirichlet skew, no cross-domain clients, no multi-task heads. Adaptation happens only once, after training; personalization is not built into training. On CIFAR, per-client accuracy is synthetic (a class-weighted global test score), so the CIFAR "harm" numbers mainly reflect label skew. Local baselines use fixed epochs and learning rates, so the comparison depends on how well the local model is tuned. Single runs: no seeds, variance or statistical testing. Hyperparameters are taken from earlier work (lr=40 for the LSTM, lambda=5000 from Kirkpatrick). The EWC Fisher matrix needs a public auxiliary dataset. The explanation of why head and tail participants differ is conjecture backed by a few example sentences.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This paper is the source of the evaluation criterion the project already uses: compare federated with local-only per client, on each client's own test data. Its definition is operational and outcome-based (federated accuracy below local accuracy means no incentive to participate). It is not a definition of negative transfer in the strict sense of conflicting updates. So in the thesis, "federated underperforms local-only" should be called a participation-incentive / personalization gap (Yu et al. 2020), and kept apart from mechanism-level terms such as gradient conflict or cancellation. Three points apply to the step-dilution finding. (1) The paper's harmful mechanisms (DP clipping plus noise, median aggregation) work by restricting or shrinking each participant's contribution to the global step, not by participants cancelling each other. That is close to the project's observation that cancellation between datasets is low but the aggregated step is about 10x smaller. The paper describes this as the tradeoff where "aggregation must limit the influence of these contributions, producing an inaccurate model". (2) Its "head" participants are clients whose data is very different from the rest. Their local model wins even under plain FedAvg, which is how the small BUSI clients sit next to large ISIC/SIIM clients in this project. (3) Its remedy, local adaptation of a global model, overlaps with FedPer. The project already personalizes stems and heads during training. Yu et al. suggest adding a post-hoc fine-tune or freeze-base step on the final global trunk as a cheap extra arm. If FedPer plus fine-tuning still loses to local-only, the gap cannot be blamed on missing personalization. The paper offers no measurement tool for cancellation; for definitions of that, other papers on gradient conflict and update cancellation are needed. Caveat: the paper's results come from single-domain, single-task data, so they do not show what happens cross-domain.

**Local.** Section 4.1 "Inter-Task Affinity", Eq. (1) and the paragraph after it. The training-level average is an unnumbered display equation right after Eq. (1). The phenomenon is characterized in Section 1 (Introduction).

**Formulação.** Setup: shared parameters θ_s and task-specific parameters θ_i. Total loss L_total(X, θ_s, {θ_i}) = Σ_{i∈T} L_i(X, θ_s, θ_i), with unit weights; the paper says the construction generalizes to arbitrary weights.
Lookahead step on the shared parameters only, using task i's gradient: θ^{t+1}_{s|i} := θ^t_s − η ∇_{θ^t_s} L_i(X^t, θ^t_s, θ^t_i).
Eq. (1), per-step affinity: Z^t_{i→j} = 1 − L_j(X^t, θ^{t+1}_{s|i}, θ^t_j) / L_j(X^t, θ^t_s, θ^t_j). The batch X^t and task j's head θ^t_j stay fixed. Z > 0 means i helps j; Z < 0 means i's update is antagonistic to j. The measure is asymmetric.
Training-level score: Ẑ_{i→j} = (1/T) Σ_{t=1}^{T} Z^t_{i→j}. It can also be averaged every n steps or over a contiguous window.
Groups of three or more tasks: the affinity onto a is approximated by the mean of the pairwise affinities, e.g. (Ẑ_{b→a} + Ẑ_{c→a})/2 for the group {a,b,c}.
Selection: choose at most k groups (the inference budget) that maximize the total affinity onto each served task. This is NP-hard and is solved by branch-and-bound or a binary integer program.
Theory (Sec. 4.3): L_a is α-strongly convex and β-smooth, η ≤ 1/β, and the gradient norms are equal. If b has higher affinity than c on a, and cos(g_a, g_c) ≤ (η/4)·(β/α)/((β/α) − 1) − 1, then a step on g_a+g_b lowers L_a more than a step on g_a+g_c (Proposition 1; Lemma with Eq. (2)). Appendix A.3 gives a quadratic counterexample in which the assumption fails.

**Métrica.** Affinity is the relative change in task j's training loss on the same batch after a one-step SGD lookahead on the shared parameters with task i's gradient (Eq. 1). It is scale-invariant and its sign tells helpful from harmful. Per-step values are averaged over training. The ablations show that computing it every 10 steps loses no quality and runs 319% faster. Affinities computed on validation loss correlate with train-loss affinities at Pearson 0.9804. Downstream, the chosen groupings are judged by total test loss (Taskonomy) or total test error (CelebA) under a fixed inference budget, against MTL, STL, random grouping (RG), gradient-cosine grouping (CS), HOA, Uncertainty Weights, GradNorm and PCGrad.

**Método.** TAG trains all tasks together once in a hard-parameter-sharing network. During training it periodically computes the pairwise lookahead affinities Z_{i→j}: save the state, take one step on the shared parameters with task i's gradient, measure task j's loss ratio, then restore. It then picks task groups that maximize the total affinity onto each task within a budget, solved by branch-and-bound or integer programming. Finally it trains the chosen groups from scratch. The goal is to replace the exhaustive or higher-order search over 2^|T|−1 networks (HOA) with a single run.

**Resultados.** The abstract on Taskonomy reports 10.0% lower test loss than training all tasks together, while running 11.6x faster than a state-of-the-art grouping method (HOA). Taskonomy (5 tasks): TAG beats MTL by 10.0%, GradNorm by 7.7%, STL by 1.5% and random grouping by 9.5%. Its 2-split grouping beats HOA by 2.5%, but HOA's 3-split grouping is better. With the latency constraint removed, the paper says TAG "surpasses that of the HOA, RG, and CS task grouping methods, while operating 22 times faster than HOA". The 11.6x and 22x speedups are two different reported settings. CelebA (9-task subset, 2-split budget): total error 49.66 for TAG, 49.73 for HOA, 49.99 for STL and 50.00 for MTL. UW, GradNorm and PCGrad all do worse than the groups found by HOA or TAG. The paper concludes: "the improvement from identifying tasks which train well together cannot be replaced with current multi-task training augmentations". Grouping by gradient cosine similarity (CS) is weaker than TAG and, on Taskonomy, costs 140 more GPU hours. The conclusion says affinity "can find close to optimal auxiliary tasks" and "implicitly measure generalization capability among tasks".

**Limitações.** 1. The authors flag task a8 in CelebA: "This case represents a limitation of TAG, and it will struggle to find the best auxiliary task for objectives like a8". There the affinity differences are tiny (0.04), and TAG picked the worst partner.
2. Groupings are sensitive to hyperparameters. Halving the batch size or doubling the learning rate changes the ground-truth optimal groupings (Table 3, checked by training all 511 CelebA combinations).
3. Computing affinity only in the first, middle or last 25% of training degrades grouping quality, so the signal changes over training.
4. The theory holds only in the strongly convex and smooth setting, with equal gradient norms and a cosine bound. Appendix A.3 gives a counterexample in which it fails.
5. The lookahead is one step of plain SGD on a single batch, and the head of the target task stays fixed.
6. Groups of three or more tasks use only the mean of pairwise affinities, so higher-order interactions are not measured.
7. Taskonomy uses the "medium" split rather than Full+.
8. The setting is centralized, single-domain MTL with shared inputs. There is no federated setting, no multi-step local training, and no aggregation.
9. The paper names no "negative transfer" construct; harm is the sign of Z.

**Relevância para o projeto.** TAG offers a loss-based, sign-carrying, scale-invariant measure of transfer that complements the project's geometric metrics. The cancellation ratio 1 − ||Σ wΔ|| / Σ w||Δ|| and pairwise cosine only describe the geometry of the update vectors. TAG instead asks whether a step from source i lowers or raises target j's loss. The paper reports that cosine-based grouping (CS) does worse than the lookahead affinity, which suggests cosine alone is an imperfect proxy for transfer.
A client-level version can be computed directly in FedPer. Apply client i's trunk delta Δ_i (or the aggregated Σ wΔ) to the global trunk. Keep client j's personalized stem and head fixed, and evaluate L_j on a held-out batch: Z_{i→j} = 1 − L_j(θ_s + Δ_i, θ_j) / L_j(θ_s, θ_j). This is a multi-step, federated analogue of Eq. (1), not the paper's exact one-step SGD form; it inherits the paper's caveat that affinity depends on learning rate and step size.
Two diagnostics follow. (a) Compare Z_{j→j} (own delta) with Z_{agg→j} (the delta after FedAvg). This splits the loss lost to "step dilution" (a ~10x smaller aggregated step) from directional harm (Z_{i→j} < 0 for i ≠ j), which the cancellation ratio cannot tell apart. Low cancellation with Z_{agg→j} > 0 but much smaller than Z_{j→j} would confirm dilution rather than conflict.
(b) An inter- versus intra-dataset affinity matrix (BUSI, ISIC, TCGA-LGG, SIIM) could motivate clustered or grouped federation, the federated analogue of TAG's task grouping.
Caveats: affinity is asymmetric and depends on hyperparameters (the paper's Table 3), and on non-convex networks it is only a proxy.

## Do Current Multi-Task Optimization Methods in Deep Learning Even Help? (2022)

- **Autores:** Derrick Xin, Behrooz Ghorbani, Justin Gilmer, Ankush Garg, Orhan Firat
- **Venue:** NeurIPS 2022 (arXiv:2209.11379)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11379
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "task interference problem" / "loss interference" / "negative interactions between the gradients" / "conflicting gradients". The exact phrase "negative transfer" never appears in the paper.

**Definição (literal).** "Different tasks often compete with each other for model capacity, leading to the task interference problem." (Section 1, Introduction). Section 3 (Prior Work) attributes the gradient-conflict view to earlier work: [prior work] "hypothesize that negative interactions between the gradients of different tasks is a significant contributor to the interference problem."

**Local.** Section 1 (Introduction) for task interference; Section 3 (Prior Work) for the gradient-conflict hypothesis; Section 2 (Setting), Eq. 1, for scalarization and the Pareto front

**Formulação.** The paper gives no formal definition of interference or negative transfer. It formalizes the baseline (Eq. 1): theta_hat(w) = argmin_theta L(theta; w), where L(theta; w) = sum_i w_i L_i(theta), w > 0, sum_i w_i = 1 ("w is a fixed vector of task weights determined by the practitioner beforehand"). Pareto dominance: theta dominates theta' if it is no worse on every task loss and strictly better on at least one. "theta is Pareto optimal if it is not dominated by any other point. The collection of the Pareto optimal points is denoted as Pareto front." In practice, interference is the gap between a method's per-task (train or test) losses and the Pareto front obtained by sweeping w.

**Métrica.** Interference and any MTO benefit are judged by Pareto-front comparison. The authors trace the scalarization Pareto front by grid-searching task weights w (and the sampling ratio in NMT), then plot each MTO method's per-task train loss (optimization) and test metric (generalization) against that front. A method helps only if it lands strictly beyond the front. Metrics: per-language cross-entropy and BLEU for NMT; segmentation/depth test errors for CityScapes; attribute errors for CelebA. The paper also reports hyper-parameter sensitivity and wall-clock cost (training steps per second). It does not use a gradient-cosine or cancellation metric.

**Método.** A large-scale empirical audit, not a new algorithm. Well-tuned scalarization (fixed task weights, grid-searched) is compared with popular multi-task optimization (MTO) methods: MGDA, GradNorm, PCGrad, IMTL and Random Loss Weighting (RLW). The benchmarks are multilingual NMT (WMT En->Fr/Zh/De/Ro, Transformers from a small 3+3-layer base upward, kept small on purpose to exacerbate interference), CityScapes (ResNet-50 shared encoder) and CelebA. The paper then analyses evaluation pitfalls and proposes evaluation practice.

**Resultados.** (1) "all of the MTO algorithms in our study simply yield performance trade-off points on the scalarization Pareto front", so "their performance can be fully replicated by simply optimizing a weighted average of the losses." (2) In NMT, "Larger models achieve Pareto fronts strictly to the lower-left of the base model": spending compute on capacity beats spending it on MTO, and per-task gradients cost "a significant reduction in the number of training steps per second". (3) On the low-resource Romanian task, generalization "is primarily driven by the amount of regularization", and the results "cast doubt on the ability of MTO algorithms to effectively regularize the model". (4) CityScapes: "MTO solutions significantly under-perform scalarization", and "appropriately balancing the different losses is crucial". CelebA: comparable. (5) "the effect of tuning hyper-parameters is much more significant than the effect of the MTO algorithm choice." (6) Evaluation pitfalls: high sensitivity to hyper-parameters; "rerunning multiple seeds is insufficient" when hyper-parameters come from a sparse grid; "the current practice to consider just one (arbitrary) weighting scheme will bias the evaluation"; "different MTO algorithms find different points on the same Pareto front". The authors recommend a Common Task Framework and say MTO methods should be "robust to changes in the utility function". Conclusion: "scalarization, with appropriate weights, can match both the optimization and the generalization behaviors of MTO algorithms."

**Limitações.** - The authors note that grid-searching the scalarization front "is computationally prohibitive" as the number of tasks grows. It is practical here with 2-4 tasks.
- Only supervised learning is studied (no RL or self-supervised).
- The paper gives no formal definition of interference or negative transfer and no direct measurement of gradient conflict, so it argues from outcomes (Pareto position), not mechanism.
- The setting is centralized MTL on a single machine and dataset family. There is no federated, non-IID or cross-domain-client setting, and no partial supervision.
- Seed counts are not stated; the authors argue seeds are less important than hyper-parameter variance.
- It does not show that interference is absent, only that MTO methods do not beat a tuned static weighting.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is a counterweight before attributing federated < local-only to gradient conflict.
(a) Terminology: the paper speaks of "task interference" and capacity competition, not "negative transfer". It never defines conflict quantitatively, so the project's cancellation ratio 1 - ||sum w_i Delta_i|| / sum w_i ||Delta_i|| and the pairwise cosines are a mechanistic measure this paper deliberately avoids. Cite the paper for the outcome-based (Pareto) view, not for a definition.
(b) The paper's main lesson matches the project's finding. Once hyper-parameters (task weights, budget) are tuned, conflict-resolution machinery rarely matters, and hyper-parameter or budget effects dominate the algorithm choice. That supports reporting step dilution and local budget (steps vs epochs) as primary causes rather than conflict, given low inter-dataset cancellation. It also cautions against adopting PCGrad-style aggregation fixes without a tuned baseline.
(c) Evaluation implications for the study:
  - compare arms as per-task trade-offs (seg vs cls; task_weights / task_lambdas sweeps) rather than single points;
  - tune the federated arm (server step size / learning rate, local budget) before claiming negative transfer;
  - the warning that seed reruns do not replace hyper-parameter variance fits the project's exploratory, single-seed Wilcoxon caveat.
(d) The finding that capacity and regularization drive low-resource generalization is relevant to small BUSI clients versus large ISIC/SIIM clients.
(e) Gap: nothing here covers aggregation-level cancellation or federated client drift, so the project's metric is complementary.

## In Defense of the Unitary Scalarization for Deep Multi-Task Learning (2022)

- **Autores:** Vitaly Kurin, Alessandro De Palma, Ilya Kostrikov, Shimon Whiteson, M. Pawan Kumar
- **Venue:** NeurIPS 2022 (arXiv v4, 8 Mar 2023)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.04122
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** The paper never uses the phrase "negative transfer" or "interference" (checked by searching the full text). Its terms are: "conflicting per-task gradient directions" / "conflicting gradients assumption" / "gradient 'tug-of-wars'" (PCGrad/GradDrop line), "loss imbalances", "inter-task competition" / "tasks compete for model capacity" (MGDA line), PCGrad's "tragic triad", and "the reported weakness of unitary scalarization". "Unitary scalarization" means minimizing the plain sum of task losses. "SMTO" means specialized multi-task optimizer.

**Definição (literal).** §2 Related Work: "One line of research hypothesizes that conflicting per-task gradient directions lead to suboptimal models, and focuses on explicitly removing such conflicts [66, 11, 42, 62, 28, 41]. Some authors postulate that loss imbalances across tasks hinder learning, proposing loss reweighting methods [30, 10, 40]. Sener and Koltun [54] and Navon et al. [48] propose that tasks compete for model capacity and interpret MTL as multi-objective optimization in order to cope with inter-task competition."

§1 Introduction: "While some work shows that multi-task networks trained via unitary scalarization exhibit superior performance to independent per-task models [35, 29], others suggest the opposite [58, 30, 54]. As a result, many explanations for the difficulty of MTL have been proposed, each motivating a new Specialized Multi-Task Optimizer (SMTO)... We instead postulate that the reported weakness of unitary scalarization is linked to experimental variability or to a lack of regularization."

§3 PCGrad: "Yu et al. [66] postulate that multi-task convergence is severely slowed down if the following three conditions (named the tragic triad) hold at once: (i) conflicting gradient directions: [cos(g_i, g_j) < 0] for some i, j; (ii) differing gradient magnitudes...; and (iii) the unitary scalarization has high curvature along [the average gradient]."

§5.1: "This suggests that the main difficulty of MTL is not associated with the optimization of its training objective, but rather to incorporating adequate regularization."

**Local.** §1 Introduction (p.1); §2 Related Work (p.2); §3 "PCGrad" paragraph (tragic triad, with Eq. 5 for the projection); §5 intro ("Let us define a regularizer as a technique to reduce overfitting [15]"); §5.1 Ablation Study. Eq. (1) defines the unitary-scalarization objective.

**Formulação.** Eq. (1): min_θ L^MT(θ) := Σ_{i∈T} L_i(f(θ, X, i), Y), where θ = θ_shared ∪ {θ_i^task}. Unitary scalarization runs gradient descent on (1), so the step is -Σ_i ∇_θ L_i, computed with a single backward pass. PCGrad's notion of conflict (§3): tasks i and j conflict when cos(∇L_i, ∇L_j) < 0. PCGrad then projects each g_i onto the normal plane of every g_j it conflicts with: g_i ← g_i − (g_i·g_j/||g_j||²) g_j (Eq. 5). GradDrop's notion (§3) is entry-wise sign conflict, measured by the "positive sign purity" P = ½(1 + Σ_i g_i / Σ_i |g_i|) per coordinate. The MGDA convergence set (Prop. 1) is any θ with 0 ∈ conv{∇_{θs} L_i}, i.e. Pareto stationarity, which is a superset of the stationary points of (1).

The paper gives no formal definition of negative transfer. Instead, the phenomenon is operationalized as "SMTO beats unitary scalarization" or "unitary scalarization is worse than per-task models". The paper argues that such gaps vanish once regularization is matched.

**Métrica.** The paper never measures gradient conflict directly as an outcome. It evaluates benchmark performance with a mean and 95% CI over repeated runs, with models selected on validation and reported on test:
- Multi-MNIST: average task accuracy, 10 runs.
- CelebA (40 tasks): average task accuracy, 3 runs.
- Cityscapes: depth absolute and relative error, segmentation mIoU and pixel accuracy, 3 runs, per-metric early stopping.
- Meta-World MT10/MT50: best average success rate, 10 runs.
- Training time per epoch for every benchmark.

For the regularization analysis it uses:
- validation-accuracy curves over epochs, with and without dropout or ℓ2, to show when overfitting starts;
- training loss per task;
- an under-optimization diagnostic: the norm of the unitary-scalarization update on the shared parameters, ||Σ_i ∇_{θs} L_i||, tracked during training (§5.3). A large norm near convergence means the optimizer stopped away from stationary points of the summed loss;
- Sign-Agnostic GradDrop (App. D.3), a control that tests whether sign conflicts matter.

**Método.** The paper is a critical re-evaluation rather than a new method. It re-implements MGDA, IMTL, PCGrad, GradDrop and RLW (Dirichlet/Normal) in one PyTorch pipeline and compares them with unitary scalarization. All methods get the same tuned single-task regularization: dropout, an ℓ2/weight-decay grid, early stopping by validation selection, and, for RL, a larger replay buffer, actor ℓ2 and reward normalization at the minibatch level. The second part (§5) gives technical results showing that each SMTO acts partly as a regularizer:
- MGDA, IMTL and two-task PCGrad converge to supersets of the stationary points of the summed loss (Prop. 1, Cor. 1, Cor. 2), so they under-optimize, much like early stopping.
- PCGrad is equivalent to a dynamic, stochastic loss rescaling (Prop. 3), similar to RLW.
- GradDrop's "joint minima" property also holds for a sign-agnostic random gradient drop (Prop. 4).

**Resultados.** - No SMTO consistently beats unitary scalarization on Multi-MNIST, CelebA, Cityscapes, MT10 or MT50. The 95% CIs overlap everywhere, and unitary scalarization is the cheapest method.
- Runtime: PCGrad is the slowest on CelebA. On MT50 a unitary-scalarization run takes about 15 hours, while PCGrad, MGDA and GradDrop take more than a week.
- On Cityscapes, the parity holds even though the depth loss is roughly an order of magnitude smaller than the segmentation loss. The authors present this as evidence against the loss-imbalance hypothesis.
- Earlier reports that PCGrad beats unitary scalarization on Meta-World are attributed to the baseline lacking reward normalization.
- With regularization removed on CelebA (§5.1), the SMTOs behave like ℓ2-penalized unitary scalarization and delay overfitting. Early stopping alone lets unitary scalarization match them. Unregularized unitary scalarization drives each task's training loss close to its optimum, so the bottleneck is generalization, not optimization.
- MGDA and IMTL keep a large summed-loss update norm near convergence, which is empirical under-optimization (§5.3).
- Sign-agnostic GradDrop matches GradDrop on CelebA, which "partly undermin[es] the conflicting gradients assumption" (§5.2). App. B.4 adds: "this seriously undermines the conflicting gradient hypothesis that motivated GradDrop."
- IMTL crashed from numerical overflow in RL.

**Limitações.** The authors state these limitations in §6:
- "we do not exclude the existence of settings where unitary scalarization underperforms."
- Grid searches ran under limited compute, so some methods might gain from more tuning.
- The regularization hypothesis is "only a partial explanation."

Additional limitations:
- Few seeds: CelebA and Cityscapes have 3 runs, so the CIs are wide and "no significant difference" is partly a power issue.
- The benchmarks are single-domain and have closely related tasks (the same images labelled for several tasks), apart from Meta-World.
- No cross-domain or heterogeneous-input setting and nothing federated.
- Nash-MTL, CAGrad and similar recent SMTOs were not benchmarked, only discussed analytically.
- The authors explicitly exclude settings where an update does not use all tasks.
- Conflict, transfer and interference are never measured directly. The paper only shows that removing conflicts does not help.
- The paper never defines negative transfer, so it cannot rule out real interference in settings with less related tasks.

**Relevância para o projeto.** 1. Terminology. This paper is a key reference for arguing that "negative transfer" is often claimed without being isolated. It frames the gap between multi-task and per-task models (which mirrors federated vs local-only here) as possibly caused by experimental variability, lack of regularization or tuning differences rather than real interference. It never defines negative transfer, so the definition must come from other papers (e.g. Wang et al. 2019 for transfer learning; Standley et al. 2020 or Fifty et al. 2021 for MTL). Cite it for the critique, not as a definition source.

2. Fit with the cancellation-ratio finding. The project found little inter-dataset cancellation (1 − ||Σ wΔ|| / Σ w||Δ||, close to the orthogonal reference), yet federated still underperforms local-only. That matches the paper's thesis: the gap may not come from conflicting update directions. Its PCGrad and GradDrop analyses show that methods built to remove conflicts work through rescaling, stochasticity and under-optimization. Two predictions follow for the student: conflict-surgery methods applied to the trunk aggregate (PCGrad/CAGrad-style server aggregation) are unlikely to close the gap, and the explanation should be sought in effective step size and regularization.

3. Step dilution. The paper's §5.3 diagnostic, the norm of the summed-loss update on the shared parameters, is conceptually close to the project's ~10x smaller aggregated step. Both treat a change in update magnitude, not direction, as what matters. In the paper, aggregators with enlarged convergence sets (MGDA/IMTL) under-optimize. In this project, averaging near-orthogonal client deltas shrinks the step, a geometric effect close to the orthogonal reference that acts like an implicit lower learning rate or early stopping on the shared trunk. The paper suggests reading this as under-optimization or regularization of the trunk rather than as interference, which supports comparing against local-only with matched effective steps or LR rescaling (e.g. server learning rate / FedAvgM-style correction).

4. Methodological lessons for the comparison:
- tune regularization and early stopping fairly in both arms;
- report variability across seeds (the project uses a single seed and non-independent client×fold pairs, so p-values stay exploratory);
- pick models on validation and report on test;
- keep the budget mode matched.

5. Caveat. The paper's benchmarks are same-input, single-domain MTL with joint updates. The project is cross-domain (ultrasound, dermoscopy, MRI, CXR), uses different inputs per client, applies FedPer personalization and averages multi-step local updates. These differ structurally, so the paper's conclusion ("unitary scalarization suffices") does not carry over automatically. It explicitly leaves open "settings where unitary scalarization underperforms", and cross-domain federated MTL could be one.

## A Survey on Negative Transfer (2023)

- **Autores:** Wen Zhang, Lingfei Deng, Lei Zhang, Dongrui Wu
- **Venue:** arXiv:2009.00909 (v4 read, dated 09 Aug 2021). The journal version is IEEE/CAA Journal of Automatica Sinica, 2023. The arXiv page does not give the venue; it comes from my recall and has not been checked.
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.00909
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** True

**Termo usado.** "negative transfer" (NT); "negative transfer gap" (NTG); in multi-task learning, also "negative interference"

**Definição (literal).** Abstract and Section I (Introduction): NT is when "introducing source domain data/knowledge undesirably decreases the learning performance in the target domain" (the abstract says "leveraging source domain data/knowledge undesirably reduces the learning performance in the target domain"). Section II-C, Definition 1: "Let ϵ𝒯 be the test error in the target domain, θ(𝒮,𝒯) a TL algorithm between 𝒮 and 𝒯, ... Then, NT happens when ϵ𝒯(θ(𝒮,𝒯)) > ϵ𝒯(θ(∅,𝒯))"; "the degree of NT can be evaluated by the NTG"; "Obviously, NT occurs if the NTG is positive." Section VIII-A: "conflicting gradients among different tasks may induce NT (also known as negative interference)."

**Local.** Abstract; Section I (Introduction); Section II-C, Definition 1 (Negative Transfer Gap); Section II-D (factors of NT); Section VIII-A (Multi-Task Learning)

**Formulação.** NT happens when ε_T(θ(S,T)) > ε_T(θ(∅,T)). Here ε_T is the test error on the target domain, θ(S,T) is the model a transfer-learning algorithm trains on source S plus target T, and θ(∅,T) is the same algorithm trained on target data only. The degree of NT is NTG = ε_T(θ(S,T)) − ε_T(θ(∅,T)), and NT occurs iff NTG > 0. The paper notes that NTG "may not always be computable": in unsupervised domain adaptation there is no labelled target data, so the no-transfer error cannot be measured.

**Métrica.** The Negative Transfer Gap: target test error with transfer minus target test error without the source, at the performance level, judged against a no-transfer baseline. The paper gives no gradient- or parameter-level measure (no gradient cosine, no update-cancellation metric). It mentions conflicting gradients in multi-task learning only as a possible cause of NT, not as a way to measure it.

**Método.** This is a survey with no new method or experiments. It formalises NT through NTG and names four factors behind it (Section II-D): domain divergence ("Arguably, the divergence between the source and target domains is the root of NT."), the transfer algorithm, source data quality and target data quality. It sorts about fifty representative approaches into a 'reliable TL' scheme: secure transfer ("explicitly avoids NT in its objective function"), domain similarity / transferability estimation, distant (transitive) transfer through intermediate domains, and NT mitigation (data-level, model-level and target-prediction-level). Section VIII links NT to multi-task learning, lifelong learning and adversarial attacks.

**Resultados.** The paper reports no new experiments or benchmark numbers; cited methods are summarised only qualitatively. The main conceptual results are three. First, the operational definition of NT is relative to a no-transfer baseline on the same target (NTG). Second, domain divergence is treated as the root cause. Third, the recommended pipeline is to estimate source-target similarity, then use secure transfer when similarity is high and distant transfer or mitigation when it is low. For multi-task learning (VIII-A) it cites gradient surgery and gradient vaccine, GradNorm and uncertainty weighting, task-relatedness learning, routing networks, Pareto search, and multilingual 'negative interference'. Future directions: NT for regression (most methods are for classification); positive transfer in open environments (streams, heterogeneous features, unknown classes); theoretical tests or empirical procedures to find the exact factors behind NT; secure versions of unsupervised domain adaptation, few-shot and adversarial transfer learning.

**Limitações.** Scope is limited to "homogeneous and closed-set classification problems in TL". It leaves out work that does not frame itself in terms of NT. It never mentions federated learning; the nearest point is a passing remark that source data can be turned into pretrained models for privacy. NTG is defined only for a single source and a single target, and only at the level of final error. It gives no definition for symmetric multi-task or multi-client settings, and none for segmentation. It gives no way to tell apart the mechanisms behind a positive NTG (for example, gradient conflict vs. optimisation dilution). It has no dedicated limitations section, and since it is a survey it adds no empirical evidence.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is the main source for the definition. In the paper's terms, NT in the project means federated (with transfer from other clients and datasets) does worse than local-only (no transfer) on the same client's test split. Per client, NTG = error_federated − error_local-only. By this definition, the finding that federated underperforms local-only IS negative transfer, whatever its mechanism. So the project's cancellation ratio 1 − ||Σ wᵢΔᵢ|| / Σ wᵢ||Δᵢ|| and its pairwise trunk-delta cosines are not measures of NT under this survey. They are mechanism diagnostics, in the same family as the 'conflicting gradients' the survey names as one possible cause in multi-task learning (VIII-A, gradient surgery and vaccine). Low inter-dataset cancellation together with a positive NTG suggests the cause is not gradient conflict. That fits the step-dilution explanation, which the survey's four factors do not directly cover: the closest are 'transfer algorithm' and domain divergence. The survey supports framing the thesis as 'NT (NTG > 0) observed; conflict-driven cause tested and largely ruled out; optimisation/dilution cause proposed'. It also points to transferability estimation and secure transfer as possible mitigation families. Caveat: the survey has no federated or segmentation coverage, so FL-specific sources are needed for the cancellation/'weight cancellation' concept.

## Optimizing the Collaboration Structure in Cross-Silo Federated Learning (FedCollab) (2023)

- **Autores:** Wenxuan Bao, Haohan Wang, Jun Wu, Jingrui He (UIUC)
- **Venue:** ICML 2023 (PMLR 202), to my knowledge; the arXiv v1 HTML I read (submitted 10 Jun 2023) names no venue, so treat the venue as unverified.
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06508
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** True

**Termo usado.** "negative transfer problem" (in global FL, GFL, and also in personalized FL, PFL)

**Definição (literal).** Abstract: "Through utilizing more training data, FL suffers from the potential negative transfer problem: the global FL model may even perform worse than the models trained with local data only." Section 1: "However, when clients have non-IID data, GFL may suffer from the negative transfer problem: the global model performs even worse than the local models" (citing Zhang et al., 2021). Section 3.1, GFL paragraph: "However, when clients have different distributions, the global model significantly degrades, and even performs worse than local training [...] which we refer to as the negative transfer problem." (The middle of this sentence, with the Zhang et al. 2021 citation, was not reproduced word for word by the fetch tool.)

**Local.** Abstract; Section 1 (Introduction); Section 3.1 (Problem setup, GFL paragraph). The operational measure is IPR, Eq. 11 in Section 5.

**Formulação.** The paper gives no closed-form definition of negative transfer. It is defined operationally per client: client i suffers it when eps_i(h_FL) > eps_i(h_local), meaning its expected risk under the FL model is worse than under its own locally trained model.

Setup (Section 3.1): N clients; client i holds m_i samples from distribution D_i; beta_i = m_i/m.
- Local training: minimize eps_hat_i(h).
- GFL: minimize sum_i beta_i eps_hat_i(h).
- Collaboration structure (Eq. 1): client i minimizes sum_j alpha_ij eps_hat_j(h), with alpha_ij = beta_j * 1{j in C} / sum_{k in C} beta_k, over non-overlapping coalitions C. This gives the collaboration matrix A (Eq. 9).

Theorem 3.3 (Eq. 6), with probability at least 1-2delta:
eps_i(h_alpha) <= eps_i(h*_i) + 2 phi(alpha_i, beta, m, delta) + 2 sum_{j!=i} alpha_ij D(D_i, D_j)
The three terms are irreducible error, a quantity-driven generalization term, and a distribution-shift term.
- Empirical quantity term (Eq. 7): phi_hat = (C/sqrt(m)) * sqrt(sum_j alpha_ij^2 / beta_j).
- Distance D (Def. 3.2): the C-divergence max_h |eps_i(h) - eps_j(h)|, estimated as 2*BalAcc - 1 of a federatedly trained pairwise client discriminator.
- FedCollab objective (Eq. 10): min over A of sum_i [phi_hat_i + sum_j alpha_ij D_hat_ij].

Corollaries 3.4-3.5: with no distribution shift, GFL is optimal. Above a threshold D_thr, which shrinks as m_i grows, local training is optimal. So small clients gain the most from collaborating, and large clients collaborate only with very close distributions.

**Métrica.** Relative to the local-only baseline, per client:
- IPR (Incentivized Participation Rate, Eq. 11): the fraction of clients whose FL model beats their local model. 100% IPR means no client suffers negative transfer.
- RSD (Reward Standard Deviation, Eq. 12): the standard deviation of per-client accuracy gains over local training.
- Mean accuracy over 5 runs.

Negative transfer is measured purely at the outcome level, as performance against local-only. It is not measured at the level of gradients or updates.

**Método.** FedCollab first estimates pairwise distribution distances between clients without sharing data, using a lightweight 2-layer MLP discriminator trained federatedly (on ResNet-18 features for CIFAR). It also uses client sample counts. It then solves the bound-derived objective (Eq. 10) with a greedy coalition-reassignment optimizer that starts from local training (Algorithm 2). This needs no model training and reaches only a local optimum. Any GFL or PFL algorithm (FedAvg, FedProx, SCAFFOLD, pFedMe, Ditto, etc.) is then run inside each coalition. The collaboration structure is the lever for avoiding negative transfer.

**Resultados.** Setup: 20 clients (10 large, 10 small), three synthetic non-IID scenarios.
- Label shift (FashionMNIST, MLP): FedAvg Acc/IPR 46.64/46.00 → 92.45/100.00 with FedCollab.
- Feature shift by rotation (CIFAR-10, CNN): 44.31/86.00 → 52.61/100.00.
- Concept shift by label permutation (CIFAR-100 coarse, ResNet-18): 26.62/50.00 → 40.94/100.00.

Other findings:
- FedCollab brings all 7 base GFL/PFL algorithms to about 100% IPR.
- It beats clustered FL on CIFAR-100: 40.94 vs IFCA 30.74 and KMeans 34.24.
- Ablation on FashionMNIST: ignoring distances drops accuracy to 67.79; ignoring quantities drops it to 90.31.
- Section 5.2 notes that under plain FL "more than half of the clients (mostly the small clients) receive a FL model worse than local model."

**Limitações.** The paper has no limitations section. The points below are inferred.
- The bound is VC-dimension / 0-1-loss based, so it is loose for deep networks.
- The capacity constant C is a tuned hyperparameter, and the structure found depends on it.
- The greedy optimizer only finds a local optimum.
- Coalitions are hard and non-overlapping. Within a coalition, the whole model is shared at uniform quantity weights. There is no partial-parameter (FedPer-style) sharing, so it cannot express "share the trunk, keep the heads".
- Experiments are small synthetic simulations (20 clients; rotations or label permutations of FashionMNIST/CIFAR) with a single task per client. There is no multi-task, segmentation or medical cross-domain data.
- Negative transfer is diagnosed only by outcome against local-only. There is no mechanism-level analysis such as update conflict or cancellation.
- The appendix past about 100k characters (B.2-B.5) was not read.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is a direct federated counterpart to the project's operational definition. The project's finding that federated underperforms local-only is exactly the FedCollab "negative transfer problem", and IPR (the share of client × task pairs where federated beats local-only) and RSD could be reported directly from the existing *_test_results.csv deltas.

The bound also gives a theory for when local-only should win. Distribution distance D is large across BUSI, ISIC, TCGA-LGG and SIIM-ACR. Large clients (ISIC, SIIM) have a small D_thr, so collaborating with distant domains is predicted to hurt them. Small clients (BUSI) should benefit most.

The paper does NOT tie negative transfer to gradient or update conflict. In its framing, harm comes from a distribution-shift term plus quantity weighting. That is compatible with the project's observation of low inter-dataset cancellation: negative transfer can arise without updates cancelling. It also supports treating step dilution and the averaging weights (alpha_ij) as separate mechanisms rather than as "conflict".

Practical ideas it suggests:
(a) A coalition or intra-dataset-only federation arm as an ablation, analogous to FedCollab's structure search.
(b) A discriminator-based D_hat between datasets as a measured covariate.

Caveat: its whole-model sharing and single-task setting differ from FedPer's partial sharing in a multi-task setup. The bound applies to the shared hypothesis only loosely.

## Federated Multi-Task Learning on Non-IID Data Silos: An Experimental Study (FMTL-Bench) (2024)

- **Venue:** arXiv preprint (v2, 16 Apr 2024, cs.LG). The HTML still has the ACM template placeholder ("Make sure to enter the correct conference title"), so the paper does not confirm a venue.
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12876
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** True

**Termo usado.** The paper never uses the term "negative transfer": a full-text search found no occurrence of "negative". It describes the phenomenon in other words: "gradient conflict" (Sec. 3.4.1), "optimization direction conflicts from the MTL process" (Sec. 3.4.4), and "task heterogeneity" / "model task heterogeneity" (Sec. 1, 2). In practice it shows negative transfer as a negative Δ% against the local-training baseline.

**Definição (literal).** The paper gives no definition of negative transfer. The closest verbatim statements are these. Sec. 3.4.1: "In fact, the PCGard algorithm, designed to resolve gradient conflict issues, generally underperforms." Sec. 3.4.4: "Simultaneously, it can resist optimization direction conflicts from the MTL process and reduce model performance losses." Sec. 1: "with MTL optimization methods managing task heterogeneity arising from diverse client data." Metric (Sec. 3.2.4): "we compute weighted average per-task performance improvement" ... "relative to the target local training baseline without any aggregation." On l_i: "The variable l_i equals 1 when a lower metric value is desirable for task i, and 0 otherwise." ... "By default, all tasks are assigned equal weight."

**Local.** Sec. 3.2.4, Eq. 5 (metric); Sec. 3.4.1 and Sec. 3.4.4 (conflict wording); Sec. 1 and Sec. 2 (heterogeneity wording). Source: arXiv HTML v2.

**Formulação.** Eq. 5: \Delta\% = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (-1)^{l_i} w_i \frac{M_{Fed,i} - M_{Target,i}}{M_{Target,i}} \times 100\%. Here M_Target is the local-training baseline with no aggregation (or another target baseline), l_i = 1 when lower is better for task i, and w_i = 1/N by default. Some tables also report a global-evaluation variant, ΔG. Negative transfer from federation then shows up as Δ% < 0. The paper never states this as a definition; it is implied.

**Métrica.** The paper measures outcomes only. Δ% is the weighted mean, across tasks, of the relative per-task metric change of each FL/FMTL method versus the Local baseline, with the sign flipped for lower-is-better metrics. It is reported per scenario, per algorithm and per architecture (SD single-task models, MD multi-decoder, TC task-conditioned decoder), with local and global evaluation (Δ and ΔG). It does not measure conflict directly at the gradient or update level: there is no cosine, cancellation or conflict statistic. Communication, time and energy costs are also reported.

**Método.** The paper is a benchmark, not a new algorithm. FMTL-Bench covers three levels:
- Data: NYUD-v2 dense prediction tasks, with PASCAL-Context added for cross-domain scenarios. Seven scenarios: IID-1 (4 clients, same domain, same tasks); NIID-2 SDST (each client a distinct task); NIID-3 SDHT (a mix of multi-task and single-task clients); NIID-4/5 unbalanced versions of 1 and 2; NIID-6 UBCDMT and NIID-7 UBCDST (cross-domain, PASCAL-Context clients with human-parts segmentation and surface normals).
- Model: SD vs MD vs TC architectures, and the backbone size.
- Optimization: nine baselines (Local, FedAvg, FedProx, FedAMP, FedRep, PCGrad, CAGrad, MaT-FL, FedMTL).
Under parameter decoupling (FedRep, MaT-FL) only the encoder is transmitted. The "-E" variants send only encoder parameters or accumulated gradients. Training settings: AdamW, batch size 8, at most 100 rounds, 4 local epochs on NYUD-v2 and 1 on PASCAL-Context. The paper also includes a case study on communication, time and energy costs.

**Resultados.** - Sec. 3.4.1: "no single 'algorithm-model' baseline consistently outperforms others across all experiments."
- PCGrad, designed against gradient conflict, "generally underperforms" (Sec. 3.4.1).
- In IID-1, "FedAvg and FedProx significantly outperform others"; under imbalance (NIID-4) "these two algorithms are relatively robust."
- When client data shrinks and client count grows, "the performance of all algorithms, excluding FedAvg and FedProx, declines significantly" (Sec. 3.4.5).
- With balanced labels per task, "multiple single-task learning models (each model only learns one task) can achieve better performance" than one multi-task model (Sec. 3.4.2). This is MTL-level negative transfer; the paper gives no explanation.
- Cross-domain: "cross-domain tasks pose a challenge for FMTL, thereby necessitating design of additional optimization strategies" (Sec. 3.4.2). In NIID-7 (cross-domain, single-task clients) FedAvg-SD scores -27.21 Δ% and FedAvg-TC -4.07 Δ% against Local (0.00). In NIID-6 with global evaluation, FedAvg-TC reaches +6.61 ΔG against Local-TC -0.61.
- A larger backbone helps in every experiment. TC uses fewer parameters but more compute.

**Limitações.** - No definition or direct measurement of negative transfer or of conflict: the paper only reports the end-performance Δ% against Local, and it never attributes a loss to conflict versus other causes such as reduced effective step size, drift, or budget.
- Single seed, with no variance or significance reporting visible.
- Only computer-vision dense prediction on natural images (NYUD-v2, PASCAL-Context); no medical or classification+segmentation pairs.
- Few clients (4 to 5).
- No FedPer, Ditto or FedBABU among the baselines (FedRep and MaT-FL are the closest partial-sharing methods).
- The authors admit missing detail: "Due to paper length constraints, we have not listed all conclusions from other experiments" and "we will not discuss in detail the nine algorithms."
- No explanation of why gradient-surgery methods fail in FL.
- No venue confirmed.

**Relevância para o projeto.** High relevance as the reference protocol, low relevance as a definition source.

1. It confirms that the FMTL literature puts negative transfer into practice as an outcome: Δ% of a federated method versus the Local baseline, averaged over tasks with sign correction. That is exactly the project's federated-vs-local-only comparison, so the project can report a Δ% per dataset to align with the field. Federated underperforming local-only would be read there as negative transfer from federation.
2. Its cross-domain, single-task-client scenario (NIID-7) is the closest analogue to this project's single_task topology across BUSI/ISIC/TCGA/SIIM. There FedAvg loses heavily against Local (-27% Δ%), while encoder-only sharing (FedRep/MaT-FL-style, akin to FedPer) and the TC architecture soften the loss. That matches the project's observation.
3. It offers no update-level measure. The project's cancellation ratio, its intra/inter split with an orthogonal reference, and the pairwise cosine go beyond FMTL-Bench: they let the project separate conflict from other mechanisms. The paper cannot tell a conflict-driven loss from one caused by step dilution. The project's finding (little inter-dataset cancellation, a ~10x smaller aggregated step, federated still below local) is a mechanism-level account that FMTL-Bench lacks. It is also consistent with FMTL-Bench's surprising result that PCGrad/CAGrad do not help: if conflict is not the dominant cost, gradient surgery should not fix it.
4. Caution: the paper never uses the term "negative transfer", so the thesis should not cite it as the source of a definition of the term. Cite it for the Δ% protocol and the cross-domain FMTL difficulty, and take the definition from MTL/transfer sources (e.g., Wang et al. 2019; Standley et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2020 for gradient conflict).

## FedHCA2: Towards Hetero-Client Federated Multi-Task Learning (2024)

- **Autores:** Yuxiang Lu, Suizhi Huang, Yuwen Yang, Shalayiding Sirejiding, Yue Ding, Hongtao Lu
- **Venue:** CVPR 2024 (arXiv 2311.13250)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13250
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** medium · **candidato a SOTA:** True

**Termo usado.** "model incongruity", "conflicting parameter updates" / "update conflicts among clients", "conflict of gradients"; data heterogeneity and task heterogeneity. The paper never uses the term "negative transfer" (and not "interference" either in the Introduction).

**Definição (literal).** Abstract: "The main challenge of HC-FMTL is the model incongruity issue that invalidates conventional aggregation methods." §1: "HC-FMTL introduces an additional challenge of model incongruity, which exacerbates client heterogeneity." §1: "Data heterogeneity is a consequence of clients encountering distinct data domains" ... "task heterogeneity, which assigns different objectives for each task, could impede joint optimization". §1: the method "mitigates the negative effects of conflicting parameter updates caused by data and task heterogeneity". §3.3: "As the inner product of gradients is a measurement of accordance, maximizing the inner product is equal to reducing the conflict of gradients." Eq. 6 discussion: it "measures the maximum conflict between client updates and the target update, which is an approximation to the conflict between gradients." (These quotes were pulled through a summarizing fetch tool, so check the wording against the PDF before citing.)

**Local.** Abstract; §1 Introduction (para. 3-4 and contributions); §3.3 Hyper Conflict-Averse Aggregation (Theorem 1, Eq. 4-9)

**Formulação.** There is no formal definition of negative transfer. Conflict is defined implicitly as a low (or negative) inner product between client updates, and between client updates and the aggregate. Theorem 1 says gradient descent on a shared MTL encoder is equivalent to FL averaging aggregation plus "an extra term that maximizes the inner product of gradients between all pairs of tasks" (proof in Appendix A). HCA, Eq. 6: max_{U~} min_i <Δθ_i^E, U~> s.t. ||U~ − Δθ̄^E|| ≤ c·||Δθ̄^E||, with c in [0,1). Here Δθ̄^E is the average encoder update. Dual (Eq. 7-8): min_w F(w) = U_w^T Δθ̄^E + sqrt(φ)·||U_w||, with U_w = (1/N) Σ_i w_i Δθ_i^E, φ = c²||Δθ̄^E||², Σw_i = 1, w_i ≥ 0. Update (Eq. 9): U~ = Δθ̄^E + (sqrt(φ)/||U_w||)·U_w. This is CAGrad applied to client parameter deltas. Decoder (Eq. 12-13): V_l = [Δθ^D_{1,l},...,Δθ^D_{K,l}]^T, Ã_{i,l} = Softmax(Δθ^D_{i,l} V_l^T/√d)·V_l. Personalized update (Eq. 14): θ_i^(r) = θ_i^(r−1) + Δθ_i^(r) + ψ_i·θ~_i, where ψ = α (encoder) or β (decoder). Learnable weights (Eq. 16-17): Δα_i = (U~^(r))^T Δθ_i^{E,(r)}, Δβ_{i,l} = (Ã_{i,l}^(r))^T Δθ_{i,l}^{D,(r)}.

**Métrica.** The paper does not measure conflict empirically. It has no cosine-similarity or conflict plots, and the inner product is invoked only in theory. Effects are judged indirectly by Δm, the average per-task relative performance drop against the local-training baseline, sign-adjusted for lower-is-better metrics. This is effectively a negative-transfer score relative to local-only, with Δm > 0 meaning federation helps. Per-task metrics include mIoU, odsF, mErr, RMSE and others. Figs. 6-7 show how the aggregation weights evolve.

**Método.** FedHCA2 targets hetero-client federated MTL, where clients hold different tasks or task sets: single-task clients and a multi-task client, each with its own decoders. The method has three parts. (1) Hyper Conflict-Averse Aggregation on the shared encoder: a CAGrad-style min-max problem over the client encoder deltas, justified by Theorem 1, which says that MTL gradient descent on a shared encoder equals FL averaging plus a gradient-agreement term. (2) Hyper Cross-Attention Aggregation for decoders: layer-wise attention over all clients' decoder updates, giving each client a personalized mixture. (3) Learnable Hyper Aggregation Weights α_i and β_{i,l}, which scale how much of the aggregated update each client absorbs. Prediction heads stay local and are not aggregated.

**Resultados.** Benchmarks: PASCAL-Context (4,998 train / 5,105 test images, 5 tasks) and NYUD-v2 (795 / 654 images, 4 tasks). Setting 1 has five single-task PASCAL clients plus one multi-task NYUD client. Backbone Swin-T, 100 rounds, batch size 8, AdamW at lr 1e-4. Table 1 Δm: FedHCA2 +2.18%, FedAMP +1.47%, MaT-FL −0.46%, FedAvg −11.76%. FedAvg loses badly to local training. Table 2 Δm: FedHCA2 +0.75%, FedAMP +0.27%, FedPer −0.16%. FedPer is slightly worse than local-only. Ablation (Table 3): encoder HCA alone +1.89%, decoder aggregation alone +1.80%, both +2.18%. Table 5 (multi-task clients only): Δm +0.53%, but PASCAL SemSeg mIoU falls below local (64.17 vs 64.87). The gains over local-only are small, in the order of 1-2% Δm.

**Limitações.** The paper never defines or measures conflict or negative transfer. Its conflict claims rest on theory (Theorem 1) plus downstream Δm, with no evidence that client deltas actually conflict. Parameter deltas taken after several local epochs are used as a stand-in for gradients, and the paper admits this is an approximation. Eq. 5 assumes the optimizer auto-scales the learning rate. The only stated limitation (Conclusion) is that heterogeneous architectures are left for future work. Evaluation is on natural-image dense prediction (PASCAL, NYUD) with few clients and one seed. There is no classification task and no medical or cross-modality domain. Improvements over local-only are marginal, and some tasks still lose to local training. The CAGrad constraint keeps the update within c·||mean update|| of the mean, so it cannot restore magnitude lost to averaging.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is the closest architectural analogue to the project: a shared encoder aggregated across clients with different tasks, personalized heads, mixed single- and multi-task clients, and a FedPer baseline. For terminology, it frames the problem as "model incongruity" plus "conflicting parameter updates" (a low inner product between client deltas) and never says "negative transfer". Its Δm against local training is in effect how the project's federated-vs-local-only gap is measured. FedAvg (−11.76%) and FedPer (−0.16%) underperforming local-only matches the project's finding. ## Features are fate: a theory of transfer learning in high-dimensional regression (2024)

- **Autores:** Javan Tahir, Surya Ganguli, Grant M. Rotskoff
- **Venue:** arXiv preprint 2410.08194 (v2)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08194
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "negative transfer"; "transferability" (symbol 𝒯); related: "feature sparsification", "anomalous positive transfer"

**Definição (literal).** "We refer to the situation 𝒯<0 as negative transfer, since pretraining leads to degradation of the generalization error." (Section 2). Also, Section 3.3: the "condition for negative transfer is satisfied when there is more target data than there is power in the pretrained subspace."

**Local.** Section 2 (Eqs. 5-6) for the definition; Section 3.3 (Eq. 11) for the condition on target data vs. pretrained subspace

**Formulação.** Transferability 𝒯 = E_D[ R_sc − R_tx ] (Eq. 5), where R = E_{p(x)}[(f(x,Θ) − f*(x))^2] (Eq. 6) is generalization error, R_sc is the error of a model trained from scratch on n target samples, and R_tx is the error of the transferred model (linear transfer: frozen pretrained features, train only the readout; or fine-tuning: all parameters from the pretrained init). Transfer succeeds iff 𝒯 > 0; negative transfer iff 𝒯 < 0. Deep linear model f(x) = x^T W1...WL, Gaussian inputs, source/target linear teachers with β_s^T β_t = cos θ. Linear transfer: E R_lt = sin^2 θ + (σ^2 + sin^2 θ)/(n − 2) (Eq. 10). With γ = n/d: underparameterized (γ>1) negative transfer iff γ − 1 > σ^2 / sin^2 θ; noiseless case negative for cos^2 θ < γ < 1 (Eq. 11). Fine-tuning: 𝒯_ft = (γ − 1)(1 − 2 cos θ) for γ ≤ 1, 0 for γ > 1, i.e. negative iff cos θ < 1/2 (Eq. 15, Thm 3.9).

**Métrica.** Expected difference in test MSE (generalization error) between from-scratch and transferred models on the target task, 𝒯 = E[R_sc − R_tx]; analysed as a phase diagram over target sample ratio γ = n/d, label noise σ and source-target feature angle θ. Checked in simulation for deep linear and ReLU networks.

**Método.** Uses a feature-centric theory of transfer: a model is pretrained on a source task, then either its features are frozen and only the readout is trained (linear transfer) or the whole model is fine-tuned on n target samples. Closed-form generalization errors are derived for deep linear networks in the rich (feature-learning, small-init) regime. Pretraining causes "feature sparsification", meaning the learned features collapse onto the source direction. The paper derives exact transferability phase diagrams and extends the results heuristically to wide ReLU networks, where linear transfer behaves like kernel interpolation. A power-law fit locates the boundary there.

**Resultados.** (1) Transfer beats training from scratch when the target function is well represented in the pretrained feature space (small angle θ). (2) Negative transfer appears as the target dataset grows: it happens once the target data carries more information than the pretrained subspace holds. Frozen features cap the error at sin^2 θ, while scratch training keeps improving with n. (3) Ridge regularization does not cure negative transfer and makes the negative region larger. (4) Fine-tuning a deep linear net is negative iff cos θ < 1/2 (for γ ≤ 1) and has no effect for γ > 1. (5) "Anomalous positive transfer" can occur even at θ = π/2 when target data is scarce. (6) Theorem A.2 (Appendix A): Dudley, KL and Wasserstein-type distances between source and target can be made arbitrarily large while transfer stays positive. In the paper's words, "distributional measures of source-target distance are insufficient for predicting the success of transfer learning" (Appendix G, Fig. 4, gives empirical support). (7) A finite source dataset moves the phase boundary but leaves the qualitative picture unchanged (Appendix C).

**Limitações.** The analytic results are for deep linear networks only, whose "representation capacity ... is limited to affine transformations", so depth separation and nonlinear effects are not captured. Data are Gaussian and teachers are linear and single-direction. The scratch baseline is unregularized for tractability; regularized baselines appear only in simulation. The ReLU results are heuristic (an empirical power law, ν≈1.18), since the authors are "not aware of a theory of generalization error for infinite width nonlinear networks" in the mean-field regime. Transfer here is sequential and single-source, from pretraining to a target task: there is no multi-task or federated setting, no simultaneous training and no gradient interaction. Preprint, so not peer-reviewed as far as the page shows.

**Relevância para o projeto.** The paper gives the cleanest formal definition of negative transfer to cite. It is defined as worse target generalization than a from-scratch baseline (𝒯 < 0), which matches the project's comparison of FedPer against the local-only baseline. Negative transfer is therefore defined by outcome (performance against a baseline), not by a mechanism. The project's cancellation ratio 1 − ||Σ wΔ|| / Σ w||Δ|| measures gradient or update conflict. That is a candidate mechanism, not negative transfer itself, and this paper does not define or measure update cancellation at all. Its central result fits the project's finding: a shared, pretrained-like subspace hurts once a client has enough local data relative to the information that subspace holds. So the large clients (ISIC ~3k, SIIM ~12k, TCGA ~3.9k images) are the ones most likely to see negative transfer from a shared trunk that fits their target poorly, even with little cancellation between datasets. Federated underperformance can then come from feature misalignment and from capacity or budget effects such as step dilution, not only from update conflict. The result that KL/Wasserstein distances do not predict transferability warns against arguing from "domain gap" alone (ultrasound vs dermoscopy vs MRI vs X-ray) and supports measuring feature-subspace alignment, for example the overlap between the subspaces each client's head uses. Caveats: the setting is sequential pretrain-then-transfer in linear regression, not simultaneous federated multi-task learning with personalized heads. Use it as conceptual and definitional support, not as direct evidence.

The primary historical source is Thorndike & Woodworth (1901), "The influence of improvement in one mental function upon the efficiency of other functions", Psychological Review 8:247-261. Its full text was not read here; only Perkins & Salomon's summary of it was.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This gives the project a citable origin and baseline definition for the term. This maps onto the project's main comparison. Federated (shared trunk) underperforming local-only on a client is negative transfer in this original sense. Weight cancellation, 1 - N/E, is a different thing: a possible mechanism measured on updates, not the phenomenon itself. Three things follow. (a) The thesis should define negative transfer by the federated vs. local-only performance gap, per client, task and dataset, and treat cancellation, cosine conflict and step dilution as candidate mechanisms. ## Multi-Task Learning Using Uncertainty to Weigh Losses for Scene Geometry and Semantics (2018)

- **Autores:** Alex Kendall, Yarin Gal, Roberto Cipolla (University of Cambridge)
- **Venue:** CVPR 2018
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07115

**Termo usado.** The paper never uses the terms "negative transfer", "interference", "gradient conflict" or "cancellation". It frames the problem as sensitivity to loss weighting: with a poor weighting (uniform, or near the optimum but off it), the joint network ends up worse than single-task models on one or more tasks. That is negative transfer in practice, but the paper attributes it to loss scale and task noise, not to tasks conflicting.

**Definição (literal).** Abstract: "In this paper we make the observation that the performance of such systems is strongly dependent on the relative weighting between each task's loss." Sec. 3, after Eq. (1), L_total = sum_i w_i L_i: "However, there are a number of issues with this method. Namely, model performance is extremely sensitive to weight selection, w_i, as illustrated in Figure 2." Sec. 3: "We observe that at some optimal weighting, the joint network performs better than separate networks trained on each task individually (performance of the model in individual tasks is seen at both edges of the plot ...). At near-by values to the optimal weight the network performs worse on one of the tasks." Sec. 1: "We observe that the optimal weighting of each task is dependent on the measurement scale (e.g. meters, centimetres or millimetres) and ultimately the magnitude of the task's noise." Sec. 5.1: "Using a uniform weighting results in poor performance, in some cases not even improving on the results from the single task model."

**Local.** Abstract; Sec. 1 Introduction; Sec. 3 around Eq. (1) and the Figure 2 discussion; Sec. 5.1 Model Analysis; Sec. 6 Conclusions; Appendix B

**Formulação.** There is no formal definition of negative transfer. Its operational reading is that a multi-task model scores worse on a task than the single-task model, and the paper shows this by sweeping the task weight w (Figure 2 tables, Table 1). The method comes from a Gaussian likelihood with a per-task homoscedastic noise sigma_t. For two regression tasks, Eq. (7) is L(W, s1, s2) ~ 1/(2 s1^2) L1(W) + 1/(2 s2^2) L2(W) + log s1 + log s2. For regression plus classification, with a softmax whose logits are scaled by 1/s^2, Eq. (10) is L ~ 1/(2 s1^2) L1 + 1/s2^2 L2 + log s1 + log s2. In practice the network learns s = log sigma^2 for numerical stability. The log terms act as a regulariser that stops sigma growing without bound.

**Métrica.** Task metrics only. There is no measure of interference, gradient conflict or update cancellation. Results are semantic segmentation IoU, instance segmentation mean error (px) and inverse-depth mean error on Tiny CityScapes (128x256) and full CityScapes. Single-task models are the reference, and a joint model below that reference reads as harmful. The paper also reports how the learned weights evolve; the final ratio is 43 : 1 : 0.16 for semantic, depth and instance.

**Método.** The model is a shared encoder (ResNet-101 + ASPP, dilated) with a separate decoder per task (semantic, instance, depth). Losses are weighted by learned per-task homoscedastic (task-dependent aleatoric) uncertainty from a maximum-likelihood derivation: each task loss is scaled by 1/sigma_t^2 (or 1/(2 sigma_t^2)), plus a log sigma_t penalty, and the sigma_t are learned jointly with the weights by SGD. The weights therefore change during training and adapt to the scale and noise of each task, so a hand-tuned grid search is no longer needed.

**Resultados.** Table 1, Tiny CityScapes validation:
- Single-task models: segmentation 59.4% IoU, instance 4.61 px, depth 0.640.
- Unweighted sum (1/3 each): 50.1% IoU, 3.79 px, 0.592. Segmentation drops 9.3 IoU points below its single-task model, a clear case of negative transfer caused by the weighting.
- Approximately optimal grid weights (0.89 / 0.01 / 0.1): 62.8%, 3.61, 0.549.
- Uncertainty weighting with 3 tasks: 63.4%, 3.50, 0.522, better than both single-task models and grid search.

Figure 2 sweep (semantic + depth): segmentation IoU goes 59.4 at w=1.0, 60.4 at 0.85, 56.3 at 0.5, 47.2 at 0.2 and 42.7 at 0.1, against 62.7 for learned weights. Performance is non-monotonic and very sensitive to w.

The learned sigmas converge in a few hundred iterations whatever their initialisation (Appendix B). Uncertainty decreases during training, which raises the effective learning rate, and the authors compensate with power-law LR annealing. On full CityScapes, one model handles all three tasks competitively (Table 2).

**Limitações.** - Only three related, complementary tasks on one domain (CityScapes road scenes), all labelled on every image. There are no heterogeneous domains, no partial supervision, no federated setting and no direct test of tasks that really conflict.
- Task interaction is not measured at all (no gradient cosine or conflict metric). A good weighting is assumed to resolve everything, which fixes scale imbalance but not directional conflict.
- The authors raise open questions: "there is usually not a single optimal weighting for all tasks", whether MTL is "an ill-posed optimisation problem", where to split the shared encoder, and the wish "to quantify the relationship between tasks".
- Learned weights rise as uncertainty falls, which inflates the effective learning rate and needs LR annealing (App. B).
- Errors are highly correlated across tasks (App. B).
- The benchmark comparison is "not entirely fair" (Table 2).
- Single seed, no variance reported.
- Later work (e.g. GradNorm, PCGrad, Xin et al. 2022 "Do current MTO methods help?") found that uncertainty weighting often does not beat tuned scalarisation.

**Relevância para o projeto.** 1) Definitions. Kendall et al. do not define negative transfer. They characterise it implicitly as "the joint model performs worse than single-task on some task" and attribute it to loss-scale and noise imbalance, not to directional conflict. The project's cancellation metric (1 - N/E) and pairwise trunk-delta cosine measure a different mechanism, direction conflict at aggregation, which this paper neither discusses nor measures. Cite it as the canonical "scale and weighting" view of negative transfer, as opposed to the "gradient and update conflict" view (Yu et al. PCGrad; Nagura et al.).

2) It fits the project's finding. Little inter-dataset cancellation plus a much smaller aggregated step points to a magnitude and scale problem, the kind Kendall's framework addresses, rather than conflict. Within one client the paper's lesson holds: the seg/cls balance (lambda) can by itself push a multi-task model below single-task, and its uniform-weight result (-9.3 IoU) is a cautionary example.

3) The repository already implements it: `loss.task_weighting: uncertainty` gives Sum lambda_t e^{-s_t} L_t + s_t/2 on clients with 2 or more tasks, with personalized s_t (`task_log_vars`). That makes it the direct reference for that option. Caveats:
- (a) In FedPer, s_t is personalized, so it rebalances only the local objective and does nothing about step dilution at server aggregation.
- (b) The paper's warning that falling uncertainty raises the effective learning rate matters for the local budget: larger effective local steps change the magnitude of client deltas and so the cancellation/dilution measurements. Arms with and without uncertainty weighting should be compared knowing this confound.
- (c) The paper's setting (one domain, fully labelled, centralized) differs from the project's (cross-domain, federated, partially supervised), so its gains should not be assumed to carry over.

## On Learning Invariant Representations for Domain Adaptation (arXiv v2 title: "On Learning Invariant Representation for Domain Adaptation") (2019)

- **Autores:** Han Zhao, Remi Tachet des Combes, Kun Zhang, Geoffrey J. Gordon (CMU, Microsoft Research Montreal)
- **Venue:** ICML 2019 (PMLR vol. 97, zhao19a); arXiv:1901.09453 (v2, 30 May 2019, the version read)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.09453
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** The string "negative transfer" never appears in the full text (searched). The paper calls the phenomenon "hurtful" invariant representations: aligning features while lowering source error raises target error ("the smaller the source error, the larger the target error"; "will only increase the target error"; "may hurt generalization on the target domain"). It names two causes: "conditional shift", meaning the labeling functions or class-conditionals differ, and a gap between the marginal label distributions. The paper does not use the words interference, conflict or cancellation.

**Definição (literal).** Sec. 1 (p.2): "our example shows that the objective of learning invariant representations while minimizing the source error can actually be hurtful, in the sense that the better the objective, the larger the target error. At a colloquial level, this happens because learning invariant representations can break the originally favorable underlying problem structure, i.e., close labeling functions and conditional distributions." Sec. 4.1 (p.6): "∀h : R↦{0,1}, εS(h∘g) + εT(h∘g) = 1. In other words, under the above invariant transformation g, the smaller the source error, the larger the target error." Remark after Theorem 4.3 (Sec. 4.3, p.11): "if the marginal label distributions are significantly different between source and target domains, then minimizing dJS(D^Z_S, D^Z_T) and the source error εS(h∘g) will only increase the target error."

**Local.** Sec. 1 Introduction (p.2); Sec. 4.1 counterexample with Fig. 1 (pp.6-7); Theorem 4.1 and its Remark (Sec. 4.2, pp.7-8); Lemma 4.8, Theorem 4.3 and Corollary 4.1 (Sec. 4.3, pp.10-11); Sec. 5 Experiments (pp.11-12); Appendix C

**Formulação.** The paper defines no negative-transfer quantity. It characterizes the failure with bounds instead.

(1) Upper bound, Theorem 4.1: εT(h) ≤ εS(h) + d_H~(DS, DT) + min{E_DS[|fS − fT|], E_DT[|fS − fT|]}. The terms are source error, marginal (feature) discrepancy, and labeling-function or conditional shift. The last term equals the cross-domain error εS(fT) or εT(fS). It replaces the λ* (optimal joint risk) term of Ben-David et al.'s Theorem 2.1, eq. (1). Under covariate shift it vanishes.

(2) Lower bound, Theorem 4.3: under the Markov chain X →g Z →h Ŷ, and if dJS(D^Y_S, D^Y_T) ≥ dJS(D^Z_S, D^Z_T), then εS(h∘g) + εT(h∘g) ≥ ½ (dJS(D^Y_S, D^Y_T) − dJS(D^Z_S, D^Z_T))². Here dJS = sqrt(JS divergence) is the JS distance and D^Y is the marginal label distribution. This follows from Lemma 4.8: dJS(D^Y_S, D^Y_T) ≤ dJS(D^Z_S, D^Z_T) + sqrt(εS) + sqrt(εT). Consequence: a perfectly invariant representation (dJS on Z = 0) forces a joint error of at least ½ dJS(D^Y_S, D^Y_T)².

(3) Corollary 4.1: the same bound holds with domain-specific encoders gS and gT. Separate (personalized) feature extractors therefore do not escape it when the label marginals differ.

(4) Counterexample, Sec. 4.1: DS = U(−1,0) and DT = U(1,2). The function g(x) = x+1 for x ≤ 0 and x−1 for x > 0 aligns both domains to U(0,1) exactly. Before g, λ* = 0. After g, λ*_g = 1 and ||f'S − f'T||_1 = 1.

**Métrica.** Target-domain test accuracy of DANN over training iterations, compared with a "No Adaptation" baseline (source-only model, horizontal line). A least-squares fit of the post-peak trajectory exposes the negative slope. In theory the quantities are the joint error εS + εT, the H~-divergence, the JS distance between label marginals, and the labeling-function shift E[|fS − fT|]. The paper never measures parameter-space quantities such as gradient or update cosine, or norm cancellation.

**Método.** Theoretical analysis of unsupervised domain adaptation through invariant representations (DANN-style: a shared encoder g, a shared hypothesis h, and an adversarial discriminator). Three parts:
(i) A constructive 1-D counterexample showing that invariance plus small source error is not sufficient.
(ii) A λ*-free upper bound containing an explicit conditional-shift term, made data-dependent with Rademacher complexity (Theorem 4.2).
(iii) An information-theoretic lower bound on joint error from the data-processing inequality on JS distance (Theorem 4.3, Corollary 4.1).
Empirical check: original DANN with gradient reversal on 16×16 grayscale digits (MNIST, USPS, SVHN), using a small CNN (2 conv layers with 10 and 20 channels, FC 1280→100→10). Appendix C repeats it on MNIST→USPS with artificially unbalanced source labels, averaged over 5 seeds.

**Resultados.** - Invariant features plus low source error do not guarantee target generalization. In the counterexample every hypothesis on the aligned representation has joint error 1, while the raw input admits a hypothesis with zero error on both domains.
- Sufficient condition (Theorem 4.1): small source error, close marginals, and close labeling functions. The new term can be tighter than λ*: in the Fig. 1 example it is 1/2 against λ* = 1.
- Necessary condition (Theorem 4.3): when the label marginals differ, there is a fundamental tradeoff between invariance and joint error. Pushing invariance and source error further only raises target error. Separate per-domain encoders do not help (Corollary 4.1).
- Experiments (Fig. 3): on USPS→MNIST, USPS→SVHN, SVHN→MNIST and SVHN→USPS, DANN's target accuracy peaks within about 10 iterations and then declines while source training accuracy keeps rising (negative least-squares slopes). The authors attribute this to the differing label distributions in Fig. 2. The effect is stronger with artificially unbalanced labels (Appendix C, Fig. 4; std over 5 seeds is small relative to the trend).
- Prescription: align label distributions or conditionals, not just feature marginals.

**Limitações.** - Setting: unsupervised single-source DA with a deterministic labeling function and binary classification in the theory. Target labels are assumed unavailable, which is unlike federated MTL, where every client has labels.
- The authors admit the lower bound is not tight. In the Sec. 4.1 example it gives a trivial 0 while the true joint error is 1, because the label marginals there are equal.
- The experiments are small: digits at 16×16, a tiny CNN, a single method (DANN). Accuracy changes are a few points. The main-text figures report no seeds or error bars, which appear only in Appendix C.
- No parameter- or gradient-space measurement of interference. The phenomenon is defined in terms of risk, not update geometry.
- The paper offers no algorithm to fix the problem; aligning label distributions or conditionals is left as future work.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is a risk-level (function-space) account of why a shared representation can hurt, the counterpart of the project's parameter-space cancellation metric (1 − N/E) and pairwise delta cosines.

(1) It explains how harm can occur with no visible update conflict. Forcing a shared trunk to serve domains whose label marginals or labeling functions differ creates a joint-error floor, ½ (dJS(label marginals) − dJS(feature marginals))². Low inter-dataset cancellation therefore does not imply a harmless shared trunk. This matches the finding that federated underperforms local-only despite little inter-dataset cancellation.

(2) Corollary 4.1 bears directly on FedPer, with one caveat. Domain-specific encoders (here, personalized stems) do not escape the bound when the representation reaching the shared hypothesis is aligned. In FedPer the heads are personalized, not shared, so the bound applies only loosely: it binds when the shared trunk drives representations toward invariance, which FedAvg does not explicitly enforce. That is a key difference.

(3) BUSI (3 classes), ISIC (7 classes, 66.9% NV), TCGA-LGG (mask-derived tumour/no-tumour) and SIIM-ACR have very different, non-overlapping label spaces and marginals. Even if the theory carried over, its lower bound is defined only for a shared label space Y.

(4) The DANN accuracy-vs-iteration curve (early peak, then decline) suggests a cheap diagnostic: plot per-client federated validation metrics against rounds versus local-only. Separately, the bound's "conditional shift" term is a useful name for what step dilution does not explain.

Cite it as foundational theory for "invariance can hurt" (negative transfer in DA). It is not a federated or multi-task method, not a definition of negative transfer, and not SOTA.

Sec. 1: "One such challenge is that the effective data distribution at different clients can vary greatly across the (potentially millions) of participating devices. Such statistical heterogeneity can be detrimental to the performance of ML training algorithms for several applications like personalization, recommendation, fraud detection, etc." Sec. 1 (bullet): "same input data can receive different labels from different users implying that the personalized models must differ across users to be able to predict different labels for similar test data. This is already outside the scope of standard federated learning which learns one global model and effectively replicates it locally on every client." Sec. 2: "It is known that the original FedAvg algorithm is possibly non-convergent when training on pathological and/or highly non-identical data partitions." Sec. 2 (MTL framing): "Intuitively, the base layers can act as the shared layers in a multi-task learning model and capture the wisdom of the crowd, while the personalization layers can act as the task specific layers of the same multi-task learning model to capture user specific aspects."

**Local.** Abstract; Sec. 1 Introduction (paragraph 2 and the first challenge bullet); Sec. 2 Related Work ("Federated Learning" and "Multi-task and Transfer Learning" paragraphs). Formal objective in Sec. 3.1, Eq. (2)-(3); aggregation rule in Sec. 3.2 and in the Server algorithm.

**Formulação.** The phenomenon itself has no formal definition. The paper defines "statistical heterogeneity" only informally: client distributions P_j differ. In the experiments it sets heterogeneity with k, the maximum number of classes per client. The formal content is the objective. Eq. (1): forward pass y_hat = f(x; W_B, W_Pj), where the base layers W_B (K_B layers, shared) are followed by the personalization layers W_Pj (K_P layers, private to client j). Eq. (2): average personalized population risk L_PR(W_B, W_P1..W_PN) = (1/N) sum_j E_{(x,y)~P_j} l(y, f(x; W_B, W_Pj)). Eq. (3): per-client empirical risk L_j^ER = (1/n_j) sum_i l(y_ji, f(x_ji; W_B, W_P)). Algorithm: each client runs SGD on (W_B, W_Pj) for e epochs, starting from the server's W_B^(k-1) and its own W_Pj^(k-1). The server sets W_B^(k) = sum_j gamma_j W_B,j^(k) with gamma_j = n_j / sum n_j. Personalization weights are never sent. FedPer reduces to FedAvg when K_P = 0.

**Métrica.** There is no interference, conflict or cancellation metric. The only measures are client-averaged test accuracy over global rounds (convergence speed and steady state) and the spread of test accuracy across clients (Fig. 5, shown as shaded bands and framed as fairness). Heterogeneity is controlled through k, the classes allowed per client. Results appear only as curves in figures; the text has no numeric tables.

**Método.** FedPer splits a deep network into shared base layers, trained with FedAvg (aggregation weighted by n_j), and personalization layers that are trained only on local data and never aggregated. The personalization layers are the LAST layers: the classifier plus the last few basic blocks, so K_P=1 is the classifier only and K_P=4 is the classifier plus the last 3 blocks. Setup: ResNet-34 (16 blocks) and MobileNet-v1 (11 blocks); 100 global rounds; e=4 local epochs; constant lr 0.01. CIFAR-10/100 use N=10 balanced clients, non-IID through k in {4,8,10} (or {40,80,100} for CIFAR-100), batch 128. FLICKR-AES uses 30 naturally unbalanced users and batch 4. All clients participate in every round. The motivation comes from MTL: base layers play the role of the shared trunk, and personalization layers play the role of task-specific heads, citing Smith et al. 2017 (MOCHA) and Ruder 2017.

**Resultados.** Sec. 4.2: at k=4 (highly non-IID), FedPer beats FedAvg on CIFAR-10 in both convergence speed and steady-state client-averaged accuracy. In the paper's words: "as the data partition becomes more identical (parameterized by k moving from 4 to 10), FedPer's performance comes closer to that of FedAvg". The same holds on CIFAR-100 (appendix). FedPer also gives lower variation in accuracy across clients (Fig. 5). Sec. 4.3: there is "not ... a clear correlation between K_P and the client averaged test accuracy at steady state, except that having atleast one personalization layer helps". K_P=2 was best on CIFAR-10 and K_P=1 on CIFAR-100. Sec. 4.4: replacing the base layers with one linear layer lowers accuracy on CIFAR-100 and FLICKR-AES, so the shared base layers learn something. The paper adds: "the performance of purely local training confirms that the number of training samples per client is not large enough to learn individual client models to high degrees of accuracy". Sec. 4.5: on FLICKR-AES, FedAvg with MobileNet is "similar to random guessing" because one global model cannot represent conflicting per-user labels, while FedPer models user preferences.

**Limitações.** (1) It never defines or measures negative transfer, interference or update conflict. "Ill-effects of statistical heterogeneity" is inferred only from the accuracy gap with FedAvg. (2) The main baseline is FedAvg, not local-only. Local training appears only indirectly in Sec. 4.4/Figs. 9-10, and the claim that base layers help depends on clients being too small to train alone. (3) Small scale: 10 balanced clients from a single domain, created with synthetic label skew; there is no cross-domain or cross-modality heterogeneity, and every client does the same task. (4) Results are curves only, with no numeric tables, no repeated seeds and no variance across runs. (5) The choice of K_P shows no clear trend. (6) The posted manuscript still contains an author "To Include" comment block (fine-tuning, block counts, train/test split), so it looks like an unfinished preprint. (7) Simplifying assumptions: fixed datasets, the same e and b on every client, and full participation. (8) The linear-replacement ablation mixes up model capacity with the value of federation.

**Relevância para o projeto.** FedPer is the scheme the project implements, so this paper is the foundational citation for the method, not a source for terminology. It gives NO definition of negative transfer, interference or cancellation. The student should cite FedPer for the base/personalization split and the MTL analogy (Sec. 2, verbatim quote above), and should take definitions of negative transfer and conflict from other sources (for example Nagura et al. for N/E, and the MTL and FL conflict papers). Points of contact and divergence: (a) FedPer personalizes the TOP layers (classifier plus last blocks) and shares the bottom. The project instead personalizes the input stem (encoder1) as well as the heads, and shares the middle trunk (encoder2..5 + bottleneck). That is an extension FedPer did not test, motivated by mixed 1-channel/3-channel modalities, and the thesis should say so. (b) FedPer's evidence that base layers help (Sec. 4.4) depends on clients being too data-poor to train alone. Several project clients (ISIC, SIIM, TCGA-LGG with thousands of samples) do not meet that premise. This is consistent with the observation that federated underperforms local-only, and FedPer never compares against strong local-only models. (c) FedPer's aggregation is plain n_j-weighted FedAvg of weights. All clients start each round from the same W_B, so this equals weighted averaging of deltas. The project's step-dilution finding (aggregated step about 10x smaller than individual steps) is therefore a property of the exact rule FedPer prescribes, which FedPer never analysed. (d) FedPer's heterogeneity is single-domain label skew. The project's cross-domain, multi-task setting lies outside its experiments, so FedPer offers no evidence either way about inter-dataset conflict. (e) The finding that FedPer converges to FedAvg as data become IID (Sec. 4.2) supports reading personalization as a remedy for distribution mismatch, not for gradient cancellation.

## Conflict-Averse Gradient Descent for Multi-task Learning (CAGrad) (2021)

- **Autores:** Bo Liu, Xingchao Liu, Xiaojie Jin, Peter Stone, Qiang Liu (UT Austin, Sony AI, Bytedance Research)
- **Venue:** NeurIPS 2021 (read from arXiv HTML, v2, 21 Feb 2024)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14048
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "conflicting gradients" / "conflict among objectives". The paper never uses the phrases "negative transfer" or "interference" (I searched the full HTML text for both). Its stand-in for negative transfer is the outcome-level symptom: MTL "much worse ... than learning them independently".

**Definição (literal).** Abstract: "A standard multi-task learning objective is to minimize the average loss across all tasks. While straightforward, using this objective often results in much worse final performance for each task than learning them independently. A major challenge in optimizing a multi-task model is the conflicting gradients, where gradients of different task objectives are not well aligned so that following the average gradient direction can be detrimental to specific tasks' performance." Sec. 1: "A known cause of this phenomenon is the conflicting gradients [41]: gradients from different tasks 1) may have varying scales with the largest gradient dominating the update, and 2) may point in different directions so that directly optimizing the average loss can be quite detrimental to a specific task's performance." Sec. 2.1 ("Optimization Challenge: Conflicting Gradients"): "However, $g_{0}$ may conflict with individual gradients, i.e. $\exists~~i,~\langle g_{i},g_{0}\rangle<0$. When this conflict is large, following $g_{0}$ will decrease the performance on task $i$." Sec. 3.1, after Eq. (2): "If $R(\theta,d)<0$, it means that all losses are decreased with the update given a sufficiently small $\alpha$. Therefore, $R(\theta,d)$ can be regarded as a measurement of conflict among objectives."

**Local.** Abstract; Sec. 1 (Introduction); Sec. 2.1, Eq. (1) and the "Conflicting Gradients" paragraph; Sec. 3.1, Eqs. (2)-(3); Sec. 5.2 (Delta-m definition)

**Formulação.** Setup (Eq. 1): K tasks with losses L_i(theta) on shared parameters theta. The main objective is the average L_0 = (1/K) sum_i L_i, with g_i = grad L_i and g_0 = (1/K) sum_i g_i. Conflict (Sec. 2.1): there exists i with <g_i, g_0> < 0, i.e. the averaged step increases some task's loss to first order. Two causes are named: scale imbalance, where one gradient dominates, and directional misalignment. Conflict measure (Eq. 2): R(theta,d) = max_i (1/alpha)[L_i(theta - alpha d) - L_i(theta)] ≈ -min_i <g_i, d>. This is the worst-case first-order change across tasks for update direction d, and R < 0 means no task is hurt. CAGrad (Eq. 3): max_d min_i <g_i, d> s.t. ||d - g_0|| <= c||g_0||, with c in [0,1). Dual: min over w in the simplex of F(w) = g_w^T g_0 + sqrt(phi)||g_w||, with g_w = sum_i w_i g_i and phi = c^2||g_0||^2. The update is d* = g_0 + (sqrt(phi)/||g_w*||) g_w*. c = 0 gives plain GD; c -> infinity approaches MGDA. Outcome metric (Sec. 5.2, after Maninis et al.): Delta_m = (1/K) sum_i (-1)^{l_i}(M_{m,i} - M_{b,i})/M_{b,i}. This is the average relative per-task drop against independently trained single-task models, i.e. the operational measure of negative transfer.

**Métrica.** Two levels. (a) Optimization-level conflict: the worst-case first-order loss change R(theta,d) ≈ -min_i <g_i, d> (Eq. 2), plus the sign test <g_i, g_0> < 0. The paper reviews PCGrad's pairwise test g_i · g_j < 0 as the baseline. CAGrad does not report conflict statistics over training; R is used inside the optimizer, not logged as a diagnostic. (b) Outcome level: Delta_m%, the average relative drop against single-task baselines (lower is better), plus per-task metrics. Meta-World uses success rate; Multi-Fashion+MNIST uses average loss.

**Método.** At each step, compute the K task gradients on the shared parameters and solve a small dual problem over the K-simplex for the weights w. Then step along g_0 + (sqrt(phi)/||g_w||) g_w. The result is the direction that maximizes the worst task's local improvement inside a ball of radius c||g_0|| around the average gradient. The ball keeps the iterates converging to a stationary point of the average loss, which MGDA and PCGrad do not guarantee: they may land on any Pareto-stationary point. Theorem 3.2 assumes differentiable L_i with H-Lipschitz gradients, 0 < alpha <= 1/H, a bounded-below L_0, and c < 1. Sec. 3.3 gives a speedup: sample a subset of tasks per step, which pays off for few tasks (K<10).

**Resultados.** Toy 2-task problem: GD got stuck in 2 of 5 runs. MGDA and PCGrad converge to arbitrary Pareto points; CAGrad reaches the average-loss optimum. Multi-Fashion+MNIST: lowest average loss at c=0.2. NYU-v2 (SegNet/MTAN backbone, 3 tasks), Delta_m%: CAGrad 0.20, MGDA 1.38, GradDrop 3.58, PCGrad 3.97, MTAN 5.59, Cross-Stitch 6.96. CityScapes, Delta_m%: CAGrad 11.64, PCGrad 18.29, MTAN 22.60, GradDrop 23.73, MGDA 44.14. Meta-World MT10 success: CAGrad 0.83±0.045, PCGrad 0.72±0.022, CARE 0.84±0.051. MT50: CAGrad 0.52±0.023, PCGrad 0.50±0.017, CARE 0.54±0.031. CIFAR-10 semi-supervised with auxiliary tasks (500/1000/2000 labels): ARML+CAGrad 68.25/74.37/82.81, ARML 67.05/73.22/81.35, ARML+MGDA 49.27/60.11/60.78. Note that even CAGrad has a positive Delta_m on both NYU-v2 and CityScapes, so in that setup it does not quite reach single-task performance.

**Limitações.** Centralized MTL only: federated learning is never mentioned. Conflict is defined on instantaneous per-task gradients at a shared theta, not on multi-step local updates (deltas) computed on separate data silos. The measure R is first-order, small-step and worst-case, and the paper does not use it as a logged diagnostic of how much conflict exists. Scale imbalance ("varying scales with the largest gradient dominating") is named as a cause but folded into the same "conflicting gradients" label without a separate measure. The method assumes the average loss is the true objective; the authors name replacing it as future work. Convergence assumes smooth losses and c<1, and the non-convex results do not converge to the same point across c. Cost grows with K, since it needs K backward passes and a dual solve per step; the speedup trick targets K<10. Internal inconsistencies reported by the reader (not independently checked): the 22x/55x speedup claims in Sec. 5.3 do not match the Table 7 timings (about 2x/5x); c for CityScapes is 0.2 in Sec. 5.2 but 0.4 in App. B.2; Thm. 3.2 and Thm. A.4 differ on the c range for the Pareto-stationarity statement. Reported margins over CARE on Meta-World are within the standard deviation.

**Relevância para o projeto.** Useful mainly for terminology and as an MTL-optimization baseline, not as a federated metric. (1) Definition: it gives the canonical "conflicting gradients" definition (the sign of <g_i, g_0>), and decomposes the harm into a direction effect and a scale/dominance effect. The project's cancellation ratio 1 - ||sum w_i Delta_i|| / sum w_i ||Delta_i|| measures loss of magnitude from misalignment, which is a different quantity. It is closer to Nagura's N/E than to CAGrad's R, which is the worst-case per-task projection onto the chosen update. A direct CAGrad-style diagnostic for the project would be min_i <Delta_i, Delta_agg> / ||Delta_agg||, or the fraction of clients with <Delta_i, Delta_agg> < 0 each round. That tells you whether some client is actually pushed uphill, which low cancellation does not rule out. (2) Negative transfer: CAGrad operationalizes it only at the outcome level, via Delta_m against single-task/independent baselines. That matches the project's federated vs local-only comparison, so Delta_m (average relative drop against local-only per dataset/task) is a standard, citable way to report it. (3) Step dilution: the finding of little inter-dataset cancellation but a ~10x smaller aggregated step relates to CAGrad's scale/dominance cause and to its constraint ||d - g_0|| <= c||g_0||. CAGrad deliberately keeps the step anchored to the average and does not address magnitude shrinkage from averaging near-orthogonal updates. So the project's mechanism is not "conflict" in CAGrad's sense, and the student should not label it as such. (4) Transfer to FL: the method could in principle be applied server-side to client deltas in place of FedAvg, treating clients or datasets as "tasks", as later FL works such as FedMGDA+ and FedCAGrad-style methods do. That is an extrapolation the paper itself does not make.

## Exploiting Shared Representations for Personalized Federated Learning (FedRep) (2021)

- **Autores:** Liam Collins, Hamed Hassani, Aryan Mokhtari, Sanjay Shakkottai
- **Venue:** ICML 2021 (PMLR 139); arXiv 2102.07078 v3, Mar 2023
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.07078 (full text read at https://arxiv.org/html/2102.07078, v3)
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** The paper never uses the terms "negative transfer", "interference", "conflict" or "cancellation" (I grepped the full text). It frames the phenomenon as "data heterogeneity" / "statistical heterogeneity": clients have different minimizers, so a single averaged model performs poorly, sometimes worse than local-only training. It also describes client drift: local updates "move each client away from the best averaged representation".

**Definição (literal).** Sec. 1 (Introduction): "one of the most important challenges in federated learning is the issue of data heterogeneity, where the underlying data distribution of client tasks could be substantially different from each other. In such settings, if the server and clients learn a single shared model (e.g., by minimizing average loss), the resulting model could perform poorly for many of the clients in the network ... In fact, for some clients, it might be better to simply use their own local data (even if it is small) to train a local model; see Figure 1."
Sec. 2 (Problem Formulation): "Indeed, in the presence of data heterogeneity, the error functions f_i will have different forms and their minimizers are not the same. Hence, learning a shared model q may not provide good solution to Problem (1)."
Sec. 1, Benefit (III): "This is unlike standard federated learning where multiple local updates in a heterogeneous setting moves each client away from the best averaged representation, and thus hurts performance."
Abstract: "the success of centralized deep learning suggests that data often shares a global feature representation, while the statistical heterogeneity across clients or tasks is concentrated in the labels."

**Local.** Abstract; Sec. 1 Introduction (paragraph 2 and Benefit III); Sec. 2 Problem Formulation (Eqs. 1-2); Sec. 2.1 (Eqs. 3-4, closed-form heterogeneity error); Sec. 4.2 Definition 1 (Eq. 9) and Theorem 1 (Eq. 10)

**Formulação.** The paper gives no metric for negative transfer. Its closest formal counterpart is the heterogeneity penalty of a single shared model in the linear setting (Sec. 2.1). Personalized objective (Eq. 2): min_phi (1/n) sum_i min_{h_i} f_i(h_i o phi). With f_i = 1/2 ||B w_i - B* w_i*||^2, the personalized form (Eq. 3) reaches zero global error. The single-model form (Eq. 4) has minimizer B w = B* (1/n sum_i w_i*), so its global error is (1/2n) sum_i ||(1/n) B* sum_{i'} (w_{i'}* - w_i*)||^2, "which grows with the heterogeneity of the w_i*". Its single matrix equation also cannot recover col(B*) for k>1. In words: averaging conflicting heads destroys information, while sharing only the representation keeps it. Representation quality is measured by the principal angle distance (Def. 1, Eq. 9): dist(B1,B2) = ||B_hat_{1,perp}^T B_hat_2||_2. Client diversity is Assumption 2: sigma_min of any rn rows of W*/sqrt(rn) > 0, meaning the participating clients' heads span R^k. Federation is defined to help when the per-client sample complexity Theta(d/n + log n) is much smaller than the local-only Theta(d), e.g. when 1 << n << e^{Theta(d)}.

**Métrica.** Linear/synthetic experiments: principal angle distance between the learned and ground-truth representation, and MSE on new clients. Real data: average local test accuracy over all clients, averaged over the final 10 rounds. Transfer: fine-tuned head accuracy on unseen clients (FEMNIST-letters to digits). Nothing measures update conflict, cosine similarity or cancellation between client updates.

**Método.** FedRep follows a FedPer-style split: a global representation phi (the body) is averaged by the server, and each client keeps a personal head h_i. Each round, a selected client first runs tau_h local updates on its head with phi frozen; tau_h is large because the head should come close to solving the inner minimization of Eq. 2. It then runs tau_phi updates on the representation with the new head held fixed, and sends only phi_i to the server. The server averages them: phi^t = (1/rn) sum phi_i. The key difference from FedPer (Arivazhagan et al. 2019) is that the head and body updates are alternated and decoupled. In the paper's words, FedPer "makes simultaneous local updates for both sets of parameters, therefore makes the same number of local updates for the head and the representation". In the linear analysis, the head is solved exactly (argmin), the body takes one gradient step, and the server averages and then QR-orthogonalizes. The body is initialized by the Method of Moments. Real-data setup: 10 head epochs, then 1 or 5 representation epochs; participation r=0.1.

**Resultados.** Theory (Thm 1): in the linear setting, dist(B^T, B*) contracts linearly at rate (1 - eta E0 sigma_min^2 / 2)^{T/2}. The per-client sample complexity is Theta(kappa^4 k^2 (d/rn + k log rn) log(1/eps)), against Theta(d) for local-only, and a new client needs only Theta(k) samples.
Synthetic: convergence speeds up as n grows, and solving the head exactly beats 1 or 10 GD head steps. FedSGD fails to learn B*.
Real data (Table 1, accuracy %): CIFAR10 (100,2): FedRep 87.70 vs FedPer 87.13, Local 89.79, FedAvg 42.65. CIFAR10 (100,5): 75.68 vs 73.84 / 70.68. CIFAR10 (1000,2): 83.27 vs 81.73 / 78.30. CIFAR100 (100,5): 79.15 vs 76.00, FedAvg+FT 79.34 (best). CIFAR100 (100,20): 56.10 vs 55.68, Ditto 56.34 (best). Sent140: 72.41 vs 72.12. FEMNIST: 78.56 vs 76.91 / 60.86.
Notably, Local Only wins the most heterogeneous, data-rich setting (CIFAR10, 2 classes per client, 500 samples per client). FedAvg+FT is very competitive. FedRep's margin over FedPer is small: 0.3 to 3 points.
Fig. 5: FedAvg does not improve with more local epochs, while FedRep is "monotonically non-decreasing" in head epochs.
Fig. 6: a FedRep body adapts better to unseen FEMNIST-digit clients.

**Limitações.** Stated: "Future work remains to analyze the representation learning capabilities of FedRep in non-linear settings". The theory covers only linear two-layer models with sub-gaussian inputs and assumes a shared ground-truth representation B* that is low-dimensional (k << d), with all heterogeneity in the heads/labels.
Observed (not stated by the authors): the paper assumes away the cross-domain case. Heterogeneity is label skew only (CIFAR classes per client, FEMNIST log-normal, Sent140 authors), all clients have the same input modality, and all solve one task type (classification). There is no measurement of update conflict or cancellation. The gains over FedPer are small, and there are no repeated seeds, error bars or significance tests in Table 1. Local-only still wins in one setting, and FedAvg+FT is competitive. Evaluation uses partial participation r=0.1 with tiny networks (a 5-layer CNN and an MLP).

**Relevância para o projeto.** 1) Definition: FedRep is not a source for a definition of "negative transfer". It describes the phenomenon only qualitatively, as heterogeneity: different per-client minimizers make a single averaged model worse than local-only. Its one formal handle is the Sec. 2.1 closed form: averaging heterogeneous heads costs (1/2n) sum_i ||(1/n) B* sum_{i'} (w_{i'}* - w_i*)||^2. Conceptually, that is the cancellation of the head component under averaging, which FedPer/FedRep avoid by keeping heads personal. It does not address cancellation inside the shared body, which is what the project measures with 1 - N/E. Cite Nagura et al. or the gradient-conflict MTL literature for that.
2) Core assumption to test: FedRep rests on the hypothesis that clients share one low-dimensional representation and differ only in labels/heads (Abstract, Sec. 2). The project's setting violates it on purpose: ultrasound, dermoscopy, MRI and X-ray inputs, with seg plus cls tasks. Federated still underperforming local-only is therefore consistent with FedRep's own framing (Fig. 1 and Table 1, where Local Only wins CIFAR10 (100,2)). It is not a contradiction of it. Assumption 2 ("client diversity", heads spanning R^k) and the principal-angle metric (Eq. 9) are possible probes: compare subspace distances between per-dataset trunk features.
3) Mechanism directly relevant to the step-dilution finding: FedRep's prescription is to fit the head to (near) optimality before updating the body (tau_h >> 1, then tau_phi). The project's FedPer updates both at once, as FedPer does. Low cancellation combined with a ~10x smaller aggregated step suggests the trunk is undertrained relative to the personal parts. FedRep-style alternation, or more trunk epochs per round, is a cheap, literature-backed ablation (Fig. 5 shows more local epochs hurt FedAvg but not FedRep heads).
4) Baseline note: FedRep is the canonical successor to FedPer and a standard baseline. Its reported margin over FedPer is small (0.3 to 3 points on label-skew benchmarks), so replacing FedPer with FedRep is unlikely by itself to close a cross-domain gap.

## FedBN: Federated Learning on Non-IID Features via Local Batch Normalization (2021)

- **Autores:** Xiaoxiao Li, Meirui Jiang, Xiaofei Zhang, Michael Kamp, Qi Dou
- **Venue:** ICLR 2021
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.07623 (read in full: https://arxiv.org/html/2102.07623v2)
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "feature shift non-iid" (covariate shift + concept shift across clients). The paper never uses the terms "negative transfer", "interference", "conflict" or "cancellation". The harm it describes is informal: averaging models trained on feature-shifted clients "does not lead to a good model", and FedAvg/FedProx do worse than training on a single client's own data ("SingleSet"). That last outcome is negative transfer in practice, but the paper does not call it that.

**Definição (literal).** Sec. 3 (Preliminary): "We define feature shift as the case that covers: 1) covariate shift: the marginal distributions P_i(x) varies across clients, even if P_i(y|x) is the same for all client; and 2) concept shift: the conditional distribution P_i(x|y) varies across clients and P(y) is the same."
Sec. 1, toy example: "As Fig. 1 shows, the local squared loss is very different between the two clients. Thus, averaging the model does not lead to a good model." and "In particular, the average model and average BN parameters has a high generalization error. At the same time, the average model with local BN parameter performs very well."
Fig. 2 caption: "Averaging model and BN parameters leads to worse solutions."
Sec. 5.2 (Results and Analysis): "Surprisingly, for the above two tasks, the alternative FL strategies are ineffective in the feature shift non-iid datasets, even worse than using single client data for training for most of the clients."

**Local.** Sec. 3 Preliminary (feature shift definition); Sec. 1 "Observation of BN in a FL Toy Example" and Figs. 1-2 (harm from averaging); Sec. 4.3 Definition 4.2, Eqs. (4)-(5), Theorem 4.4, Corollaries 4.5-4.6 (convergence argument); Sec. 5.2 "Results and Analysis" (FL worse than SingleSet).

**Formulação.** The only formal definition is of the data condition, feature shift (Sec. 3). Client i's joint distribution is written P_i(x,y) = P_i(y|x)P_i(x) = P_i(x|y)P_i(y). Feature shift means P_i(x) varies while P_i(y|x) is fixed (covariate shift), or P_i(x|y) varies while P(y) is fixed (concept shift). The theory (Assumption 4.1) narrows this to client-specific input covariance: inputs are centered, with covariance S_i that is independent of the label and may differ between clients.

There is no formal definition of harm or interference at the update level. The penalty of averaging appears only through convergence speed. In a 2-layer ReLU network with one local step, analysed with the neural tangent kernel, FedAvg's auxiliary Gram matrix is G^inf_pq = E_v[σ(vᵀx_p)σ(vᵀx_q)] (Eq. 4). FedBN's is G*^inf_pq = the same expression × 1{i_p = i_q} (Eq. 5): only the diagonal blocks for each client, because cross-client kernel terms are removed. Since λ_min(G*^inf) = min_i λ_min(G^inf_i) ≥ λ_min(G^inf), the linear rate (1 − ημ*_0/2)^t is no slower than FedAvg's (1 − ημ_0/2)^t (Thm 4.4, Cor. 4.5, Cor. 4.6). Put simply, the cross-client coupling through shared normalization slows convergence.

**Métrica.** Nothing is measured at the update or gradient level: no cosine similarity, no conflict score, no cancellation ratio. The paper uses outcome metrics: (1) training-loss convergence curves, FedBN vs FedAvg (Fig. 3, App. E.1); (2) per-client test accuracy, mean (std) over 5 seeds, against SingleSet (local-only), FedAvg, FedProx and, for digits, centralized training (Table 1, Tables 11-12); (3) accuracy as heterogeneity varies, where adding more clients per domain means less heterogeneity (Fig. 4c). Theoretically, the speed penalty is the least eigenvalue of the NTK Gram matrix. The harm of federating is read off as FL accuracy falling below SingleSet, i.e. a per-client local-only comparison.

**Método.** FedBN runs FedAvg but leaves out every BatchNorm layer when averaging. Each client keeps its own BN affine parameters (and, in practice, its running mean and variance), never sends them, and FedAvg averages all non-BN layers with uniform 1/K weights (Algorithm 1, App. C). There are no extra hyperparameters. The theory covers a 2-layer ReLU network with client-specific BN scaling and one local step, using the NTK framework of Dukler et al. (2020). Experiments: (a) digits with 5 clients, one domain each (SVHN, USPS, SynthDigits, MNIST-M, MNIST), truncated to 7,438 training samples per domain with 10% used by default, on a CNN with BN after every layer; (b) Office-Caltech10 (4 domains) and DomainNet (6 domains, top 10 classes) on AlexNet+BN; (c) ABIDE I fMRI connectomes from 4 sites, on a 3-layer MLP with BN. A new client from an unseen domain gets the shared non-BN layers, uses the averaged BN affine parameters and computes BN statistics on its own data (App. G).

**Resultados.** Digits (Table 11, accuracy %): FedBN gets SVHN 71.04, USPS 96.97, Synth 83.19, MNIST-M 78.33, MNIST 96.57. FedAvg gets 62.86/95.56/82.27/76.85/95.87 and SingleSet 65.25/95.16/80.31/77.77/94.38. On SVHN, the most shifted domain, FedAvg falls below local-only (62.86 vs 65.25) and FedBN recovers to 71.04. FedBN comes close to centralized training (Table 12; SVHN 71.04 vs 74.18). Office-Caltech10 (Table 1): FedBN gets A/C/D/W 63.0/45.3/83.1/90.5; FedAvg gets 54.1/44.8/66.9/85.1 and SingleSet 54.9/40.2/78.7/86.4, so FedAvg is below local-only on DSLR (66.9 vs 78.7). DomainNet: FedAvg is far below SingleSet on Quickdraw (56.1 vs 73.1); FedBN gets 71.3, still below SingleSet there. ABIDE: FedBN 65.6/75.1/68.6/65.5 vs FedAvg 62.7/73.1/70.7/64.7, so FedBN loses at UM. FedBN's margin over SingleSet widens as local data shrinks (Fig. 4b, Table 14). FedBN beats FedAvg at every heterogeneity level (Fig. 4c) and every number of local epochs (Fig. 4a). Training loss converges faster and more smoothly (Fig. 3), consistent with Cor. 4.6. On unseen domains (App. G), all three methods score about the same (FedBN 92.45/94.61 vs FedAvg 91.28/93.55).

**Limitações.** The paper does not define negative transfer, interference or cancellation, and does not measure anything at the update level, so it cannot show the mechanism it proposes. It argues that local BN "harmonizes" the clients but never measures that harmonization (for example, update alignment). The theory is very simplified: a 2-layer ReLU network, one local step, equal client sizes, no BN shift parameter, over-parameterized NTK regime. It proves only faster convergence, not better generalization. All tasks are single-task classification with the same label space on every client; there is no segmentation, multi-task learning or label-space heterogeneity. The benchmarks have few clients (4-6), one domain per client, digits truncated to equal sizes, and 5 seeds with no significance testing. FedBN still trails SingleSet on some clients (DomainNet Quickdraw, ABIDE UM), so local BN does not remove the harm of federating. The method only applies to architectures that use BatchNorm. "Concept shift" is defined unusually, as P(x|y) varying, which differs from the common P(y|x) definition.

**Relevância para o projeto.** High relevance as a baseline and design reference, but it offers no definition of the phenomenon. (1) The setting is close to the project's: one domain per client and cross-modality feature shift (ultrasound, dermoscopy, MRI, X-ray), with medical imaging named as the motivating case. FedBN is the standard argument for keeping normalization layers per client under feature shift. (2) The project already has the same idea in partial form. FedPer keeps encoder1 (the stem) personalized. MTnnUNet uses InstanceNorm (ConvInNormLeReLU), which has no running statistics to average, so it already avoids FedBN's main failure mode (BN statistics averaged across modalities). MTResNet34UNet (E7) uses FrozenBatchNorm, which is shared but identical on every client, so it is not hit either. A FedBN-style ablation would therefore mean personalizing the affine parameters of the InstanceNorm layers in the shared trunk. That is cheap to add through federated.shared_modules or a key filter in model_split. (3) Terminology: the paper gives no formal or measured definition of negative transfer. The project's metric 1 − ||Σ wᵢΔᵢ|| / Σ wᵢ||Δᵢ|| and its pairwise cosines go beyond what FedBN measures. When citing FedBN, attribute "feature shift non-iid" (Sec. 3) to it, not a definition of negative transfer. Its practical yardstick, federated accuracy below SingleSet (local-only) for a client, matches the project's federated vs local-only comparison and its finding that federated underperforms. (4) FedBN's toy-example explanation is that clients' loss surfaces differ, so the averaged model is poor even when updates do not cancel. That fits the project's finding of little inter-dataset cancellation but a weaker federated model: harm can come from misaligned optima and normalization, not from opposing update directions. FedBN does not address step dilution at all (it uses plain uniform FedAvg), so it neither explains nor fixes the roughly 10x smaller aggregated step.

## Ditto: Fair and Robust Federated Learning Through Personalization (2021)

- **Autores:** Tian Li, Shengyuan Hu, Ahmad Beirami, Virginia Smith
- **Venue:** ICML 2021 (PMLR 139); arXiv 2012.04221v3. Code: github.com/litian96/ditto
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.04221
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** The paper never uses the words "negative transfer", "interference", "conflict" between updates, or "cancellation"; a grep of the full LaTeX source turns up none of them. Its framing is "competing constraints" and "tension" between fairness, robustness and accuracy. It names statistical heterogeneity as the root cause, and it uses the device-level "relatedness" parameter tau to say when global learning hurts a device compared with local learning. The closest thing to negative transfer is the 1-D point-estimation analysis (Sec. 3.3, Fig. 4): "When 1/tau is small (less related), local outperforms global". The paper calls the resulting unequal per-device performance "representation disparity" (unfairness).

**Definição (literal).** Abstract: "we identify that \emph{robustness} to data and model poisoning attacks and \emph{fairness}, measured as the uniformity of performance across devices, are competing constraints in statistically heterogeneous networks." Sec. 1: "we identify that {statistical heterogeneity} (i.e., non-identically distributed data) is a root cause for tension between these constraints". Sec. 2, Fairness paragraph: "Due to the heterogeneity of the data in federated networks, it is possible that the performance of a model will vary significantly across the devices. This concern, also known as \textit{representation disparity}~\cite{hashimoto2018fairness}, is a major challenge in FL". Sec. 3.3: "$\tau$ controls the degree of relatedness of the data on different devices: $\tau$=$0$ captures the case where the data on all devices are identically distributed while $\tau \to \infty$ results in the scenario where the data on different devices are completely unrelated." Fig. 4 caption: "Impact of data relatedness across all devices. When $1/\tau$ is small (less related), local outperforms global; when $1/\tau$ is large (more related), global is better than local."

**Local.** The abstract; Sec. 1 (Introduction); Sec. 2 (Definition 1, Robustness, and Definition 2, Fairness); Sec. 3.1 (the Ditto objective); Sec. 3.3 (the point-estimation analysis and the Fig. 4 caption on relatedness); Appendix A (federated linear regression and point-estimation theorems for lambda*); Appendix B (heterogeneity assumption ||u_k* - w*|| <= M).

**Formulação.** The paper gives no formal definition of negative transfer. Its formal objects are:

(1) Global objective (Global Obj): min_w G(F_1(w),...,F_K(w)). For FedAvg, G = sum_k p_k F_k(w).

(2) The Ditto objective (Sec. 3.1, tagged "Ditto"), a bi-level problem for each device k: min_{v_k} h_k(v_k; w*) := F_k(v_k) + (lambda/2)||v_k - w*||^2, s.t. w* in argmin_w G(F_1(w),...,F_K(w)). lambda = 0 gives purely local models; lambda -> infinity recovers the global model.

(3) Definition 2 (Fairness): w_1 is fairer than w_2 if std{F_k(w_1)}_k < std{F_k(w_2)}_k, measured over benign devices only.

(4) Definition 1 (Robustness): a model is more robust if it has higher mean test performance on benign devices after training under attack. The attacks are A1 label poisoning, A2 random Gaussian updates and A3 model replacement (scaled updates).

(5) Heterogeneity is modelled as relatedness: device models v_k ~ N(theta, tau^2), and adversaries have tau_a^2 > tau^2. The optimal interpolation is lambda* = (sigma^2/n) * K / (K tau^2 + (K_a/(K-1))(tau_a^2 - tau^2)). The same lambda* is Bayes-optimal (accuracy and robustness) and also the fairest choice within Ditto's solution family. lambda* shrinks with more local data n, less related devices (larger tau), or stronger or more numerous attackers.

(6) The convergence analysis (App. B) assumes ||u_k* - w*|| <= M, read as "boundedness of heterogeneity". lambda scales inversely with M.

**Métrica.** There is no metric for transfer or interference. Robustness is the mean test accuracy over benign devices. Fairness is the standard deviation (or variance) of test accuracy across benign devices. The paper compares Ditto against "global" (FedAvg) and "local" (lambda = 0) as the two endpoints, so the global-vs-local accuracy gap per device is the implicit indicator of harmful sharing. It does not measure gradient or update geometry: no cosine, no update norms, no aggregation cancellation.

**Método.** Ditto is a global-regularized federated multi-task framework, described as a "lightweight personalization add-on" to any global FL objective and solver.

Each round runs two alternating steps on a selected device:
- (i) The device does the usual local update for the global objective G (local SGD on F_k for FedAvg), and the server aggregates as usual. Any robust aggregator (multi-Krum, clipping) can be swapped in here.
- (ii) The device updates its personal model v_k inexactly on h_k(v_k; w^t) = F_k(v_k) + (lambda/2)||v_k - w^t||^2.

Personal models never leave the device, so there is no extra communication or privacy cost. lambda is selected per device on local validation data: {0.05, 0.1, 0.2} under strong attacks, {0.1, 1, 2} otherwise, and fixed values for devices with fewer than 4 validation samples. The paper also tests an alternative solver that fine-tunes h_k after w* converges, and alternative regularizers (EWC/Fisher, symmetrized KL). The alternative regularizers gave no gain. Unlike FedProx, which uses the same L2 term but outputs a single global w, Ditto outputs personal models v_k.

**Resultados.** Theory (linear models):
- In federated point estimation and linear regression, Ditto with lambda* recovers the Bayes estimator. It is the most accurate and most robust choice, and the same lambda* is the fairest choice within Ditto's family.
- Fig. 4: local beats global when devices are weakly related (large tau); global beats local when they are strongly related; Ditto(lambda*) beats both.

Empirics (Fashion-MNIST, FEMNIST, CelebA, Vehicle, StackOverflow, Sent140; attacks A1-A3):
- Robustness: about +6% absolute accuracy on average over the strongest robust baseline (median, clipping, Krum, multi-Krum, k-norm, k-loss).
- Fairness: about 10% lower variance across devices and about +5% accuracy compared with TERM on clean data.
- Fashion-MNIST, clean: global .911 (std .08), local .876 (.10), TERM .909 (.07), Ditto .943 (.06). With 50% A3 adversaries: global .275, local .876, Ditto .873.
- FEMNIST, clean: global .804, local .628, Ditto .834. With 20% A3 adversaries: global .314, Ditto .613, local .620.
- Fair methods (TERM, larger t) overfit corrupted devices. Robust aggregators are either robust but unfair, or fail under heterogeneity.

Comparison with other personalization methods (Table 3):
- FEMNIST clean: Ditto .836 vs L2SGD .817, APFL .811, Per-FedAvg .827, EWC .810, SKL .820, plain fine-tuning .815.
- CelebA clean: Ditto .914, close to the best (SKL .915).
- Ditto plus clipping improves further. Joint optimization beats post-hoc fine-tuning under A3.

**Limitações.** - No operational definition or measurement of negative transfer, interference or update conflict. Harm from sharing is inferred only from global-vs-local accuracy and from adversarial corruption.
- The theory covers only 1-D point estimation and linear regression with Gaussian relatedness (tau known) and a simplified label-poisoning model. lambda* needs tau, which is unknown in practice, so lambda is tuned per device on a small grid. The authors say "More advanced tuning methods are left for future work."
- Single-task, cross-device benchmarks: one classification task, homogeneous input modality, many small devices. There are no multi-task clients, no cross-domain or cross-modality settings, and no segmentation.
- The personal model is a full model regularized toward w. Ditto does not split parameters into shared and personal modules the way FedPer does.
- Attacks are limited to A1-A3; backdoors are left to future work. Fairness is measured as accuracy variance only.
- Ditto roughly doubles local computation, since each device trains both w and v_k.

**Relevância para o projeto.** 1. Terminology. Ditto does not define "negative transfer" or "cancellation". In the project it should be cited for the global-vs-local trade-off governed by device relatedness (tau), not as a source for the cancellation metric. It does give a citable formal statement that when clients are weakly related, a model trained only on local data beats the global model, with the optimal coupling lambda* decreasing in tau and increasing in sigma^2/n. That matches the project's finding that local-only beats federated in cross-domain FL (BUSI/ISIC/TCGA-LGG/SIIM-ACR are very weakly related, so tau is large). Note that Ditto treats this purely as an accuracy and relatedness effect. It says nothing about update geometry. So the project's cancellation measure 1 - ||sum w_i Delta_i|| / sum w_i ||Delta_i|| (Nagura et al. 2020) and its pairwise cosines remain the operational tools, and Ditto offers no competing metric.

2. Mechanism. The project found little inter-dataset cancellation; the dominant cost was step dilution, with an aggregated step about 10x smaller. Ditto's framing suggests the gap to local-only does not need destructive interference to exist. A shared component pulled toward a population optimum w* is suboptimal for weakly related clients even when updates do not cancel.

3. Method and baseline. Ditto is the canonical "interpolate between local and global" personalization baseline. An L2 proximal pull of a client's full or trunk weights toward the federated trunk, with lambda tuned per client or per dataset, would let each client choose its own point between local-only and federated. That directly tests whether federation can at least match local-only: lambda -> 0 recovers local-only. It is cheap to add on top of FedPer: regularize the personalized copy of the trunk toward global_shared.pt.

4. Reporting. Ditto's fairness metric (std of per-client performance) and its practice of always reporting global and local endpoints next to the method align with the project's per-client deltas.

5. Caveat. Ditto's evidence is single-task, single-modality and cross-device, so it does not establish SOTA for cross-domain multi-task medical FL. It is a reference baseline and conceptual framing, not a SOTA candidate for measuring negative transfer.

## Multi-Task Learning as a Bargaining Game (2022)

- **Autores:** Aviv Navon, Aviv Shamsian, Idan Achituve, Haggai Maron, Kenji Kawaguchi, Gal Chechik, Ethan Fetaya
- **Venue:** ICML 2022 (PMLR 162)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.01017 (full text read from https://arxiv.org/html/2202.01017v2; code https://github.com/AvivNavon/nash-mtl)
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** True

**Termo usado.** "gradients conflict" / "conflicting gradients"; also "dominating the update direction" and "negative interaction". The exact phrase "negative transfer" never appears in the paper (0 occurrences). The degradation itself is called "performance degradation compared to single-task models".

**Definição (literal).** Abstract: "Joint training reduces computation costs and improves data efficiency; however, since the gradients of these different tasks may conflict, training a joint model for MTL often yields lower performance than its corresponding single-task counterparts." Sec. 1 (Introduction): "Unfortunately, MTL often causes performance degradation compared to single-task models (Standley et al., 2020). A main reason for such degradation is gradients conflict (Yu et al., 2020a; Wang et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021a). These per-task gradients may have conflicting directions or a large difference in magnitudes, with the largest gradient dominating the update direction." Sec. 3.1, after Eq. 2: "We can consider sum_{j!=i} alpha_j g_j^T g_i = (sum_{j!=i} alpha_j g_j)^T g_i as the interaction between task i and the other tasks; If it is positive there is a positive interaction and the other gradients aid the i'th task, and if it is negative they hamper it."

**Local.** Abstract; Section 1 (Introduction), paragraph 2; Section 3.1, Eq. 2 and the paragraph after it (the interaction term). Axiom 2.4 discussion in Sec. 2.2 covers magnitude dominance.

**Formulação.** The paper gives no standalone formal definition of conflict or negative transfer. It characterizes conflict in two ways. (a) Direction: task gradients point in conflicting directions. (b) Magnitude: one large-norm gradient dominates the combined direction. Operationally, in Eq. 2 the interaction of task i with the others is (sum_{j!=i} alpha_j g_j)^T g_i: positive means the other tasks aid task i, negative means they hamper it. Method: the update Delta_theta lies in the ball B_eps; the disagreement point is 0 (no update); each task's utility is u_i(Delta_theta) = g_i^T Delta_theta. The Nash Bargaining Solution maximizes sum_i log(u_i) (Eq. 1). Claim 3.1 shows the solution is Delta_theta = G alpha with alpha in R^K_+ solving G^T G alpha = 1/alpha (element-wise), so every task receives projection g_i^T Delta_theta = 1/alpha_i > 0. With orthogonal gradients, alpha_i = 1/||g_i|| and the update is the sum of the normalized gradients. Overall degradation is measured by Delta_m% = (1/K) sum_k (-1)^{delta_k} (M_{m,k} - M_{b,k}) / M_{b,k}, relative to single-task learning (STL); higher means worse. Pareto stationarity, the convergence target, holds when some convex combination of the gradients equals zero.

**Métrica.** Conflict is never measured directly as a statistic (no cosine histograms and no cancellation ratio). The outcome metrics are Delta_m% (average per-task relative drop against STL; negative means MTL beats STL) and Mean Rank (MR) across task metrics. RL uses success rate ± SEM. Diagnostics: the smallest singular value of G^T G, used to check the gradient-independence assumption (App. D.5), plus plots of the learned task weights alpha over training (App. D.4).

**Método.** Nash-MTL treats the per-step combination of task gradients on the shared parameters as a cooperative bargaining game. Tasks are the players, the agreement set is B_eps, and the disagreement point is no update. The Nash bargaining solution is unique under four axioms: Pareto optimality, symmetry, independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA), and invariance to affine transforms. That last axiom makes the update invariant to gradient scale, so no single large gradient can dominate. The authors call the result "proportionally fair". Each step computes all K task gradients on the shared parameters (task-specific parameters do not enter), then solves G^T G alpha = 1/alpha. The solver uses a convex surrogate followed by up to 20 concave-convex procedure (CCP) iterations, solved with cvxpy. The update is theta -= eta * G alpha. When tasks interact negatively, alpha_i rises to compensate; when they interact positively, it falls. The update norm is always sqrt(K) (proof in App. A). Theory: with step mu_t = min_i 1/(L K alpha_i) and Lipschitz-smooth (L-smooth) losses, Theorem 5.4 gives convergence to a Pareto stationary point in the nonconvex case, and Theorem 5.5 gives convergence to a Pareto optimal point in the convex case. Both assume task gradients are linearly independent except at Pareto stationary points. Speedup: recompute alpha only every T steps (Nash-MTL-T).

**Resultados.** Baselines are LS, SI, RLW, DWA, UW, MGDA, PCGrad, GradDrop, CAGrad and IMTL-G.
- QM9 (11 regression tasks, 3 seeds): Nash-MTL scores MR 2.5 and Delta_m 62.0±1.4%. The next best are IMTL-G at 77.2 and the scale-invariant (SI) log-loss baseline at 77.8; LS scores 177.6, PCGrad 125.7, CAGrad 112.8 and MGDA 120.5. The authors note that most MTL methods "fall short compared to the simple scale-invariant baseline, which ignores gradient interaction".
- NYUv2 (segmentation, depth, normals; MTAN): Nash-MTL scores MR 1.55 and Delta_m -4.04%, the only clearly negative value, meaning it beats STL. IMTL-G scores -0.76, CAGrad 0.20, MGDA 1.38, LS 5.59 and PCGrad 3.97. MGDA overfits to surface normals.
- Cityscapes (segmentation and depth): Nash-MTL has the best MR, 1.75, but only the second-best Delta_m at 6.82%; UW scores 5.89. CAGrad scores 11.64, PCGrad 18.29 and LS 22.60. With two tasks, Nash-MTL reduces to normalizing each gradient and summing.
- MT10 (Meta-World with SAC, 10 seeds): Nash-MTL reaches 0.91±0.031 success, the same as single-task SAC (0.90). CAGrad reaches 0.83, PCGrad 0.72 and MTL-SAC 0.49.
- Cost: runtime is 40.7 s per episode against 7.3 for MTL-SAC. Nash-MTL-100 cuts this to 7.9 s while still reaching 0.87. On QM9, updating every 5 or 50 steps gives a 3.7x or 9.8x speedup with a small drop.
- Feature-level gradient surrogates fail: QM9 Delta_m rises to 179.2 against 62.0 with full gradients (App. C).
- More CCP steps barely matter (App. D.2).

**Limitações.** 1) Every step needs all K per-task gradients on the shared parameters (K backward passes) plus a small convex solve. On QM9, gradient-manipulation methods take about 7 min per epoch against 0.54 for LS. The cheap feature-level surrogate badly degrades results. 2) The theory assumes task gradients are linearly independent away from Pareto stationarity. On QM9 the smallest singular value of G^T G drops to about 1e-8, so the assumption is near-violated. 3) The authors call scale invariance (Axiom 2.4) "the only non-natural assumption". It deliberately discards gradient-norm information, which can be wrong when magnitudes carry signal, for example task difficulty or data size. 4) Conflict is never quantified. The paper argues for conflict only through Delta_m and a toy example (Fig. 1), so it gives no definition or metric for the degree of negative transfer. 5) Results are not uniformly best: second on Cityscapes Delta_m; 3 seeds and no significance tests. 6) Everything is centralized and single-model. The setting has no federation, no local multi-step drift, no aggregation of model deltas, and no client data heterogeneity.

**Relevância para o projeto.** 1) Terminology. Nash-MTL never says "negative transfer". It attributes MTL underperformance against STL to "gradients conflict", with two distinct parts: conflicting directions and magnitude dominance. That split matches the project's diagnostics. Pairwise cosine and cancellation 1 - ||Σw_iΔ_i|| / Σw_i||Δ_i|| capture direction. Magnitude imbalance is a separate axis that cancellation does not measure, so per-client ||Δ_i|| and the share of the aggregate norm each client contributes are worth reporting. 2) The Eq. 2 "interaction" term (Σ_{j≠i} α_j g_j)^T g_i gives a per-participant conflict measure. The project could compute it per client: does the weighted aggregate of the other clients' deltas have a positive or negative projection on client i's own delta? That is a finer measure than global cancellation and directly tests whether some dataset is "hampered". 3) Step dilution. If client deltas are near-orthogonal (as the project finds: low inter-dataset cancellation, near the orthogonal reference), a weighted average shrinks the step roughly by 1/sqrt(K) or worse. The Nash solution for orthogonal gradients is Σ g_i/||g_i||, a sum rather than an average, and its norm is always sqrt(K) (App. A). This is a principled argument that the aggregate step should not shrink when updates do not conflict. It supports trying server learning-rate scaling or normalized-sum aggregation to counter the roughly 10x dilution. 4) The method could serve directly as an aggregation rule: treat client (or dataset) trunk deltas as "players" and solve G^T G α = 1/α at the server. This is cheap because K is about 8 clients and the deltas are already computed. Caveats: client deltas are multi-step pseudo-gradients, not per-step gradients, and the convergence theory does not cover them. The paper cites only one FL use of Nash bargaining (Kim, 2021). 5) Its evaluation metric Δ_m% against STL maps onto the project's federated-vs-local-only comparison, where local-only plays the STL role. A positive Δ_m is exactly the "federated underperforms local-only" finding. 6) Nash-MTL is a state-of-the-art (SOTA) reference point among 2022 gradient-combination methods (above PCGrad, CAGrad, MGDA and IMTL-G on 3 of 4 benchmarks). Later work (e.g. FAMO, Aligned-MTL) compares against it.

- **Venue:** CVPR 2023 (arXiv:2305.19000v1, 30 May 2023; the arXiv text itself does not name the venue). Code: github.com/SamsungLabs/MTL
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19000
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** True

**Termo usado.** "negative transfer" (only in passing, citing earlier work), "conflicting gradients", "dominating gradients", and "training instability" measured by the condition number of the task-gradient matrix. The paper does not use "cancellation".

**Definição (literal).** Sec. 2 (Related work): "Conflicting gradients having opposing directions often induce a negative transfer." Sec. 3.2 (Challenges): "Simple averaging of gradients across tasks makes a cumulative gradient biased towards the gradient with the largest magnitude" ... "Conflicting gradients with negative cosine distance complicate the training process as well;" ... "along with dominating gradients, they increase inter-step direction volatility that decreases overall performance". Sec. 4 (intro): "a gradient dominance can be measured with a gradient magnitude similarity" ... "gradient conflicts can be estimated as a cosine distance between vectors". Sec. 4.1: "In a multi-task optimization, a cumulative gradient is a linear combination of task gradients" ... "Thus, the stability of a linear system of gradients can be measured as the condition number of a gradient matrix". Sec. 4.2: "The stability criterion reaches its minimum iff both issues are solved jointly and gradients are orthogonal." Fig. 4 caption: "The most intuitive way to define the dominance is the maximum ratio of task gradients magnitudes." (The HTML fetch tool shortened quotes, so check them against the PDF before citing.)

**Local.** Sec. 2 (negative transfer, one sentence); Sec. 3.2 "Challenges" (conflict and dominance); Sec. 4 intro, 4.1 "Condition number" (Eq. 2) and 4.2 (link to conflict and dominance); Fig. 4 caption and Sec. 6.4 (empirical analysis of the stability criterion)

**Formulação.** Stack the task gradients with respect to the shared parameters as G = [g_1, ..., g_T]. The stability criterion is the condition number kappa(G) = sigma_max(G) / sigma_min(G) (Eq. 2). kappa = 1 if and only if the task gradients are mutually orthogonal and have equal norms. Conflict is the cosine between pairs of task gradients (negative cosine means conflict). Dominance is the maximum ratio of task-gradient magnitudes. Negative transfer is not defined formally; the paper treats it as a consequence of conflicting gradients.

**Métrica.** Diagnostics: kappa(G), pairwise cosine between task gradients, and the max/min task-gradient norm ratio. Sec. 6.4 reports that runs with kappa <= 10 tend to reach better overall performance. Outcome metric: Delta m, the average relative change against single-task baselines with the sign flipped for lower-is-better metrics (lower is better). It comes in task-weighted and metric-weighted forms, and the paper prefers task-weighted. RL uses MT10 success rate.

**Método.** Aligned-MTL replaces G with the closest matrix that has orthogonal, equal-norm columns: minimize ||G - G_hat||_F^2 subject to G_hat^T G_hat = I (Eq. 4), rescaled by sigma = sigma_min(G). This gives G_hat = sigma U V^T = sigma G V Sigma^-1 V^T (Eqs. 3 and 5). The update is G_hat w for pre-defined task weights w. In practice it runs in task space (Alg. 1): eigendecompose the T x T Gram matrix M = G^T G, build a balance matrix B from the eigenvalues, set alpha = B w, and return G alpha. Aligned-MTL-UB (Sec. 5.2, Eqs. 6-7) aligns the gradients with respect to the shared representation instead, an upper bound through the encoder Jacobian, so it needs one backward pass (O(1) in T instead of O(T)). Theorem 1 (Sec. 5.3): with step size <= 1/Lambda, the method converges to a Pareto-stationary point consistent with the task weights.

**Resultados.** NYUv2 (segmentation, depth, normals) with MTAN, task-weighted Delta m: Aligned-MTL -8.40%, Aligned-MTL-UB -7.83%, Nash-MTL -7.56%, CAGrad -4.18%, Uniform +5.46%. NYUv2 with PSPNet: Aligned-MTL -1.55% vs Nash-MTL -1.48%. Cityscapes, 3 tasks, PSPNet: Delta m -0.02% (Uniform +14.30%), mIoU 67.06 vs 66.73 single-task. Cityscapes, 2 tasks, MTAN: 5.27% vs Nash-MTL 6.72% and Uniform 22.60%. MetaWorld MT10: success 0.97 vs Nash-MTL 0.91, single-task SAC 0.90, PCGrad 0.72. On a synthetic two-task problem, IMTL and Nash-MTL can stop at arbitrary Pareto points, while Aligned-MTL reaches the optimum more reliably. kappa(G) predicts final performance better than cosine or the magnitude ratio alone.

**Limitações.** As the authors state: it needs one backward pass per task through the shared part, so cost grows linearly with T (Sec. 5.2 and 7). The UB variant "reduces instability, yet, it does not eliminate it since the Jacobian cannot be aligned". The UB variant has no theoretical guarantee outside encoder-decoder networks. As I observe: it is set in centralized MTL with a single dataset and fully shared inputs, with no federated or cross-domain setting. Negative transfer is not defined operationally; the paper just assumes it follows from gradient conflict. Rescaling by sigma_min can shrink the step when G is ill-conditioned. Results are single-benchmark, and statistics over seeds are limited outside RL. The convergence claim is inconsistent across sections ("optimum" in Sec. 2, Pareto-stationary in Thm. 1, "local optimum" in the conclusion).

**Relevância para o projeto.** 1) Definitions to cite: conflict = negative cosine between task gradients; dominance = norm ratio; both are folded into kappa(G). Negative transfer appears only as a consequence of conflict, citing Lee et al. 2018, so this paper is not a primary source for defining negative transfer. 2) Kappa adds something the project's cancellation ratio 1 - ||sum w_i Delta_i|| / sum w_i ||Delta_i|| does not show. Cancellation can be low while the system is still badly conditioned because one client or dataset dominates by norm. Computing kappa of the matrix of client trunk deltas per round (cheap: SVD of the K x K Gram matrix) would separate "little conflict but strong dominance" from true orthogonality. 3) Aligned-MTL treats orthogonal, equal-norm updates as the ideal (kappa = 1). That backs the project's orthogonal reference as the "no conflict" baseline. It also means near-orthogonal deltas are not harmful in themselves. The project's observed cost, a roughly 10x smaller aggregated step, is the norm shrinkage from averaging near-orthogonal vectors, i.e. a magnitude and scale problem rather than a direction conflict. Aligned-MTL's sigma_min rescaling would not fix this and could make it worse. 4) As a method, it could be applied server-side to align client deltas, or on multi_task BUSI/TCGA clients over their seg/cls gradients. Expect at most modest gains for the federated arm, since the measured cross-dataset conflict is small. 5) It is a well-known gradient-surgery baseline (alongside PCGrad, CAGrad, Nash-MTL, IMTL) to place in the related-work map, under centralized MTL optimization rather than federated learning.

## FAMO: Fast Adaptive Multitask Optimization (2023)

- **Autores:** Bo Liu, Yihao Feng, Peter Stone, Qiang Liu
- **Venue:** NeurIPS 2023
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03792
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** True

**Termo usado.** "conflicting gradients (CG)"; "under-optimization" of a subset of tasks; "generalized conflicting gradient". The paper never uses "negative transfer" as a formal term. It frames the phenomenon as per-task optimization imbalance during centralized MTL.

**Definição (literal).** Section 2 (Background, "Optimization Challenge"): "we say that conflicting gradients (CG)" occurs when "∃i, ℓ_i(θ_{t+1}) − ℓ_i(θ_t) ≈ −α∇ℓ_i(θ_t)^T d_t > 0", that is, "certain task's loss is increasing." The paper says CG is "not inherently detrimental" but becomes "undesirable when a subset of tasks persistently undergoes under-optimization due to CG." Introduction: "a subset of tasks are severely under-optimized" because "the average gradient constantly results in small (or even negative) progress on these tasks." Abstract: gradient descent on the average loss gives "poor multitask performance due to severe under-optimization of certain tasks." (The HTML tool capped quotes at 125 characters, so these are short verbatim fragments. The equation is restated from the HTML render.)

**Local.** Abstract; Introduction; Section 2 "Background / Optimization Challenge" (CG condition); Section 3.1–3.2 (Eq. 3 improvement rate, Eq. 4 balanced objective, Proposition 3.2); Algorithm 1; Appendix B (relation to MGDA).

**Formulação.** Setup: k tasks, average-loss objective ℓ0(θ) = (1/k) Σ_i ℓ_i(θ) with ℓ_i ≥ 0, and update θ_{t+1} = θ_t − α d_t.

Conflicting gradient: there exists a task i whose first-order loss change is positive, −α ∇ℓ_i^T d_t > 0, which is equivalent to ∇ℓ_i · d_t < 0. The criterion is the update direction against each task gradient, not a pairwise task-to-task cosine.

Generalized CG: every loss may decrease, but some tasks improve far more slowly than others.

Improvement rate (Eq. 3): r_i = (ℓ_{i,t} − ℓ_{i,t+1}) / ℓ_{i,t}.

FAMO objective (Eq. 4): choose d_t to maximize the worst-case improvement rate min_i r_i, minus ½‖d_t‖². Proposition 3.2: at an interior optimum all r_i are equal. In the paper's words, "decrease all task losses at an equal rate as much as possible." This is equivalent to MGDA applied to log-losses (Appendix B).

Practical update (Algorithm 1): z_t = Softmax(ξ_t). Task weights are w_i = c_t z_{i,t}/ℓ_{i,t} with c_t = (Σ_i z_{i,t}/ℓ_{i,t})^{-1}, and θ_{t+1} = θ_t − α Σ_i w_i ∇ℓ_{i,t}. The logits update by one gradient step per iteration, ξ_{t+1} = ξ_t − β(δ_t + γ ξ_t), where δ_t comes from the observed change in log-losses and γ is a decay term.

**Métrica.** Phenomenon: no direct per-step conflict or cancellation statistic is reported. It is defined through per-task loss change and improvement rate r_i.

Outcome metrics: Δm%, defined as "the average per-task performance drop of a method" relative to single-task learning (STL). It averages the relative change over K tasks, flips the sign for lower-is-better metrics, and multiplies by 100. Lower is better, and negative means better than STL. Mean Rank (MR) is "the average rank of each method across tasks." Wall-clock time per epoch measures efficiency. Reinforcement learning (MetaWorld MT10) uses success rate.

**Método.** FAMO is a dynamic loss-weighting method for centralized MTL. It replaces gradient-manipulation methods (MGDA, PCGrad, CAGrad, NashMTL), which store and combine all k task gradients at O(k) space and time. Instead, it keeps one softmax weight vector over tasks and updates it with a single amortized gradient step per iteration, driven only by the observed change in the log-losses ("leverages the loss history to update the task weighting", "bypassing the necessity of computing all task gradients"). Weighting each gradient by z_i/ℓ_i makes the update move all log-losses at an equal rate, so it targets balanced relative improvement rather than resolving geometric conflict. It costs one backward pass of the weighted loss, O(1) in k. By contrast, the paper notes that NashMTL's approximation needs "3 forward and backward passes."

**Resultados.** NYU-v2 (3 tasks), Table 1: FAMO Δm% = −4.10, the best reported. NashMTL is −4.04 and linear scalarization (LS) +5.59.

QM-9 (11 tasks), Table 2: FAMO Δm% = 58.5 vs NashMTL 62.0 and LS 177.6, so FAMO is best on Δm%. Its MR is 3.27 vs NashMTL 2.09, so NashMTL ranks better. (In my first pass the HTML table columns looked misaligned. Mapping the last value to Δm% matches the paper's claim.)

CityScapes: Δm% = 8.13. CelebA (40 tasks): Δm% about 1.21 per the error-bar table; the main table's columns are ambiguous.

MetaWorld MT10: success 0.83, on par with CAGrad.

Efficiency (Fig. 4): "negligible overhead … compared to the LS method", whereas gradient-manipulation methods need at least O(K) memory and compute. The authors say FAMO "performs consistently well across different supervised learning MTL benchmarks." These results are not uniformly state-of-the-art: it is competitive and much cheaper.

**Limitações.** Stated: "One limitation of FAMO is its dependency on the regularization parameter γ", and future work should find "a more principled way of determining γ."

Inferred:
- Single-seed-style table comparisons, with differences against NashMTL often small.
- Centralized MTL with a single shared model and all tasks on the same data. There is no federated or multi-client setting and no domain heterogeneity.
- It balances loss-decrease rates and does not measure or reduce geometric gradient conflict directly. Log-loss balancing assumes positive losses and can be distorted by losses with very different floors.
- No analysis of transfer between data domains.

**Relevância para o projeto.** Moderate, mainly conceptual.

(1) Definitions. FAMO gives a clean, citable operational definition: conflict is a step that increases some task's loss, ∇ℓ_i · d < 0, which matters only when it persists as under-optimization. That definition is per-task against the update direction. It differs from the project's metrics, which are pairwise cosine between client deltas and the cancellation ratio 1 − ‖Σ wΔ‖/Σ w‖Δ‖ (Nagura et al.'s 1 − N/E). The thesis can use FAMO to separate "conflict" (sign of task progress) from "cancellation" (norm loss at aggregation) from "negative transfer" (worse final performance than a single-task or local model).

(2) Diagnostic idea. Instead of cosines, the project could measure per-client loss change after applying the aggregated update, ℓ_i(θ_global) − ℓ_i(θ_before) or the first-order term ∇ℓ_i · Δ_agg. It could also track per-client improvement rates r_i across rounds. This would show directly whether some clients or datasets are persistently under-optimized, which fits the finding that federated runs underperform local-only despite little inter-dataset cancellation. Step dilution would appear as uniformly small r_i rather than negative r_i: in FAMO's terms, generalized CG or under-optimization without sign conflict.

(3) Method transfer. FAMO's O(1) loss-history weighting could be adapted server-side as adaptive client or dataset aggregation weights driven by each client's reported loss change. This needs no client gradients and only scalars per round, so it is cheap and privacy-friendly. Caveat: FAMO targets balanced optimization in centralized MTL. It does not address the dilution of aggregated step norms, and it has no federated or domain-shift evidence. Do not treat its centralized benchmarks as evidence for the FedPer cross-domain case.

(4) Metric. Δm% versus single-task is the standard MTL reporting convention. It maps naturally onto federated-vs-local-only deltas.

## FedBone: Towards Large-Scale Federated Multi-Task Learning (2023)

- **Autores:** Yiqiang Chen, Teng Zhang, Xinlong Jiang, Qian Chen, Chenlong Gao, Wuliang Huang
- **Venue:** arXiv preprint (cs.LG), v1 30 Jun 2023; arXiv lists no peer-reviewed venue
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17465 (full text read at https://arxiv.org/html/2306.17465v1)
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "conflicting gradients" / "gradient conflicts" between clients. The paper never says "negative transfer" or "cancellation". It also uses "interference" (Fig. 3 caption), "negative impact on each other" and "biased direction" (Introduction).

**Definição (literal).** Sec. 3.4, Definition 1 (Conflicting gradients): "Define the angle between client i graidents ∇_i and client j gradients ∇_j as ω_ij, ∇_i and ∇_j are conflicting gradients when cos ω_ij < 0." Following text: "As shown in Figure 3(a), gradients ∇_i and ∇_j have a negative impact on each other, and direct aggregation will cause a reduction in final gradients." Fig. 3 caption: "(a) The two gradients with conflicting gradient directions are aggregated directly, which can lead to interference." Sec. 3.4 motivation: "one feasible approach is to attribute multi-objective optimization problems to the existence of gradient conflicts and described them as gradients from different tasks conflicting with one another among tasks Yu et al. (2020)". Introduction: "simply aggregating models trained on diverse tasks can lead the federated model to optimize in a biased direction, which results in a decrease in the generalization ability of federated large-scale models".

**Local.** Section 3.4 "Gradients Aggregation via Conflicting Gradients Projection", Definition 1, Eq. (2) (projection), Eq. (3) (attention rescaling), Algorithm 2 (GPAggregation) and Figure 3 caption. Motivation is in Introduction para. 2.

**Formulação.** A pairwise, binary definition on client gradients of the shared ("general") model, taken from PCGrad (Yu et al. 2020): clients i and j conflict iff cos(∇_i, ∇_j) < 0, equivalently ∇_i·∇_j < 0. The fix is Eq. (2), a projection onto the normal plane: ∇_i' = ∇_i − (∇_i·∇_j / ||∇_j||²) ∇_j. Before projection, Eq. (3) rescales each gradient with attention to the previous round's aggregate: ∇_k = softmax(∇_k ∇'^T / d_∇) ∇_k. The aggregate is then the plain mean of the de-conflicted gradients, ∇ = (1/K) Σ_k ∇_k^p (Algorithm 2). The paper never ties "negative transfer" to a performance drop against single-task or local training, and it gives no scalar measure of how much conflict there is.

**Métrica.** The paper never measures conflict. It reports no cosine statistics, no fraction of conflicting pairs and no norm-reduction ratio. The cos<0 test serves only as the switch inside Algorithm 2. The paper reports task metrics only. NYUDv2: seg mIoU, depth RMSE, normal mErr, boundary odsF. PASCAL-Context: semantic seg mIoU, human-parts mIoU, saliency maxF, normals mErr, boundary odsF. Ophthalmic: classification and grading accuracy-type scores, plus lesion segmentation mIoU. Client compute cost: parameters, GFLOPs, memory.

**Método.** FedBone is split federated multi-task learning. Each client keeps a patch embedding, a task adaptation module (deformable convolution plus multi-head self-attention) and a task output head; these are personalized and never aggregated. A cloud server hosts the large shared "general model", a Swin-S pretrained on ImageNet-22K, and computes its forward and backward passes. Every round the server collects each client's gradient on the general model and combines them with GPAggregation (Algorithm 2) in three steps: (1) rescale each client gradient with softmax attention against the previous aggregated gradient, intended to "dampen the influence of clients which converge towards sharp minima"; (2) for every client, PCGrad-style projection away from each other client whose gradient has a negative dot product with it; (3) a uniform average, followed by the optimizer step. Clients are formed by splitting each task's data into 4 equal clients, with an 8:2 train/test split, 200 rounds, SGD, lr 0.01 and batch size 16. Baselines are FedAvg, FedProx, pFedMe and FedEM, each with a fully convolutional task head.

**Resultados.** NYUDv2 (Table 1), FedBone vs FedAvg: seg mIoU 42.34 vs 39.51, depth RMSE 0.5332 vs 0.5580, normal mErr 24.84 vs 26.89, boundary odsF 63.39 vs 62.08. FedBone is best on all four (FedEM is next: 40.55 / 0.5451 / 26.53 / 62.74). PASCAL-Context (Table 2): FedBone is best on 4 of 5 tasks (seg 62.74, parts 58.09, saliency 84.36, normals 15.13). pFedMe beats it on boundary (66.59 vs 66.42), and FedAvg trails badly on seg (52.71). 13-task ophthalmic dataset (Table 4; 12,912 fundus images plus Retinal-Lesions): FedBone has the best lesion segmentation (55.82 mIoU vs FedEM 54.21 and FedAvg 49.71) and is best on most tasks, though FedEM wins some (e.g. RVO, DR). Client cost with Swin-B (Table 3): 1.92M parameters, 11.74 GFLOPs and 3.31 GB on the client, against 95.04M, 123.30 GFLOPs and 33.32 GB for FedAvg. Ablation (Fig. 5, given only as a bar chart): +GPA and +TA each improve on FedAvg, TA gives the larger gain, and both together are best. The authors write that the ablation "supports that heterogeneity between different tasks is a critical factor".

**Limitações.** (1) Conflict is assumed, never shown: there is no measured cosine or conflict frequency, and no evidence that conflicting pairs occur or that projection removes them. (2) The +GPA ablation is shown only as a bar chart and mixes in the attention rescaling, so projection's own effect cannot be isolated. The task adaptation module, not GPAggregation, gives the larger gain. (3) The method projects per-sample/per-batch gradients aggregated on the server (split learning), not multi-epoch local model deltas. In FedAvg/FedPer the deltas are multi-step and the PCGrad argument holds less directly. (4) There is no local-only or single-task baseline, so the paper cannot test negative transfer in the usual sense (federated worse than isolated training). (5) It covers a single seed with no variance or significance testing, and it is an arXiv preprint without peer review. (6) The tasks come from the same image domain within each benchmark (NYUDv2, PASCAL, fundus), so there is no cross-modality federation. (7) Eq. (3) is underspecified: a softmax over a scalar dot product as written, with d_∇ not clearly defined. (8) Split learning needs a forward/backward exchange with the server at every step, so communication cost is high, which the authors acknowledge as future work. (9) The "sharp minima" justification for the attention rescaling is asserted, never tested.

**Relevância para o projeto.** Moderate, and mostly for terminology and contrast. (a) Definition: FedBone uses the PCGrad notion, a pairwise conflict meaning cos < 0 between client gradients on the shared model, which "cause[s] a reduction in final gradients". That is close to what the project measures. The pairwise cosine between client trunk deltas maps directly onto Definition 1, and the cancellation ratio 1 − ||Σw_iΔ_i|| / Σw_i||Δ_i|| measures the "reduction in final gradients" that FedBone only asserts. The project can therefore cite FedBone as an FMTL paper that defines conflict as negative client-gradient cosine without measuring it. The project's intra/inter split and orthogonal reference are stronger evidence than anything FedBone provides. (b) The project found little inter-dataset cancellation and near-orthogonal deltas, so in its setting PCGrad-style projection would rarely fire: cos<0 pairs are what get projected, and orthogonal pairs are left alone. That suggests GPAggregation would not fix the observed step dilution. Projection removes components and can only shrink or keep the aggregate norm, never grow it. (c) FedBone's architecture is FedPer-like: a shared general model plus personalized embedding/stem, task adaptation and head. Its ablation finding that personalized task adaptation matters more than conflict-aware aggregation is consistent with the project's results and supports trying richer personalized adapters. (d) It does not define or measure "negative transfer" against local-only training, so it is not a source for that term. Use it as an example of gradient-conflict FMTL and of medical (ophthalmic) multi-task FL. It is not state of the art for measurement.

## ForkMerge: Mitigating Negative Transfer in Auxiliary-Task Learning (2023)

- **Autores:** Junguang Jiang, Baixu Chen, Junwei Pan, Ximei Wang, Dapeng Liu, Jie Jiang, Mingsheng Long (Tsinghua University / Tencent)
- **Venue:** NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv:2301.12618v3, 16 Nov 2023)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12618
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** True

**Termo usado.** negative transfer (split into weak and strong negative transfer). The paper also uses "gradient conflicts" (measured by gradient cosine similarity), "transfer gain", "distribution shift" and "harmful/detrimental parameter updates"

**Definição (literal).** Abstract: "Occasionally, learning multiple tasks simultaneously results in lower accuracy than learning only the target task, which is known as negative transfer." Sec. 3, Definition 3.1 (Transfer Gain, TG): "Denote the model obtained by some ATL algorithm A as θ_A(T_tgt, T_aux, λ) and the model obtained by single-task learning on target task as θ(T_tgt). Let P be the performance measure on the target task T_tgt. Then the algorithm A can be evaluated by TG(λ, A) = P(θ_A(T_tgt, T_aux, λ)) − P(θ(T_tgt)). (2)" Definition 3.2 (Weak Negative Transfer, WNT): "For some ATL algorithm A with weighting hyper-parameter λ, weak negative transfer occurs if TG(λ, A) < 0." Definition 3.3 (Strong Negative Transfer, SNT): "For some ATL algorithm A, strong negative transfer occurs if max_{λ>0} TG(λ, A) < 0." Sec. 3: "we might be able to avoid weak negative transfer by selecting a proper weighting hyper-parameter λ, yet we cannot avoid strong negative transfer in this way." Sec. 3.1, Finding 1: "Negative transfer is not necessarily caused by gradient conflicts and gradient conflicts do not necessarily lead to negative transfer." Sec. 3.2, Finding 2: "Negative transfer is likely to occur if the introduced auxiliary task enlarges the distribution shift between training and test data for the target task."

**Local.** Abstract; Section 3 "Negative Transfer Analysis" (Definitions 3.1-3.3, Eq. 2); Section 3.1 (Definition 3.4 GCS, Finding 1); Section 3.2 (Definition 3.5 CSD, Eq. 4, Finding 2); Section 4.1 (Eqs. 5-8, Algorithm 1)

**Formulação.** Transfer gain TG(λ, A) = P(θ_A(T_tgt, T_aux, λ)) − P(θ(T_tgt)), where P is target-task performance on held-out data and θ(T_tgt) is the single-task (target-only) model. Weak NT means TG(λ, A) < 0 for the chosen λ, which a better λ can fix. Strong NT means max_{λ>0} TG(λ, A) < 0, which no reweighting can fix. Gradient conflict is measured separately by the gradient cosine similarity cos φ_ij between task gradients g_i and g_j (Def. 3.4). Distribution shift is proxied by the confidence score discrepancy d_F(D, D') = 1 − E_{x~D'} max_y f*_D(x, y) (Def. 3.5, Eq. 4). Benchmarks report Δm = (1/K) Σ_k (−1)^{z_k} (M_{m,k} − M_{b,k}) / M_{b,k}, measured against the single-task baseline (App. C.1).

**Métrica.** Transfer gain (target-task performance of the joint model minus the target-only single-task model, on validation/test data). Gradient cosine similarity is reported as a diagnostic that turns out to be weakly correlated with TG. The confidence score discrepancy proxies the train/test distribution shift. Benchmark headline is Δm, the average relative per-task improvement over STL.

**Método.** This is auxiliary-task learning with update θ_{t+1}(λ) = θ_t − η(g_tgt + λ g_aux) (Eq. 5). The ideal is to choose λ by maximizing target validation performance (Eq. 6), and ForkMerge approximates that over Δt-step intervals. The parameters are forked into two branches: one trains only on the target loss for Δt steps, giving θ(0), and the other trains jointly with the auxiliary task, giving θ(1). The method then searches λ* in [0, 1] to maximize validation performance of the linear interpolation (1−λ*)θ(0) + λ*θ(1) (Eq. 8). The merged parameters are synchronized back into both branches and the cycle repeats (Algorithm 1). Under weak NT a λ* between 0 and 1 is chosen; under strong NT λ* = 0, so the auxiliary-driven update is discarded. Sec. 4.2 extends this to K auxiliary tasks with K+1 branches, one per task-weighting vector (Eqs. 9-10), plus a pruned O(B) variant, and the merge weights act as dynamic task selection. The merge is justified empirically (Fig. 6): interpolating converged models ("mixture of hypotheses") approximates mixing the training distributions, because the loss surfaces are smooth.

**Resultados.** (1) Motivation analysis on DomainNet: with equal task weights, 23 of 30 auxiliary-target domain pairs show negative transfer, and ForkMerge avoids negative transfer in all 30 (Sec. 5.1, Figs. 5-6). (2) Gradient cosine similarity and transfer gain are not strongly correlated, and NT can be more severe when gradients are highly consistent. Weight decay or L2, used as an auxiliary task, conflicts in gradient yet helps, so conflict can act as regularization (Finding 1, Fig. 2). (3) NT tracks distribution shift. For weak-NT targets such as Painting, CSD first falls then rises and TG goes from positive to negative as λ grows. For strong-NT targets such as Quickdraw, CSD rises monotonically and TG stays negative (Finding 2, Fig. 3). (4) Benchmarks: NYUv2 Δm = +4.03% versus EW 0.30%, PCGrad 0.22%, IMTL 2.10%, CAGrad 1.41%, NashMTL 1.11% and Auto-λ 1.17%. DomainNet average 71.1 versus STL 69.8 (Δm +2.00%). AliExpress Δm 1.30% (1.20% with MMoE). CIFAR-10/SVHN semi-supervised: 13.1 / 5.49 error, Δm 46.3%. The authors claim state of the art on all four ATL benchmarks, with training time comparable to gradient-balancing methods (App. D.2, Fig. 10).

**Limitações.** There is no dedicated limitations section. Acknowledged costs: the multi-branch version costs O(K), "much lower than the exponential complexity of grid searching, but still quite large" (Sec. 4.2), and a longer merge interval Δt "increases the risk to produce bad model parameters" (App. A.1). Inferred limitations: (a) the setting is asymmetric (one target task plus auxiliaries), not symmetric MTL or federated learning; (b) it needs a target validation set and repeated validation passes at every merge; (c) the merge assumes the branches stay in one linearly connected basin, since they share an initialization and are synchronized every Δt; (d) the analysis rests mostly on DomainNet with a single architecture family, and the causal claims (Findings 1-2) are correlational; (e) the CSD distribution-shift proxy depends on the model's confidence calibration.

**Relevância para o projeto.** (1) Definition to adopt: negative transfer is an outcome measure, TG = P(joint/federated) − P(target-only). In this project that maps exactly to federated minus local-only per client and task on held-out data, so "federated underperforms local-only" is, in ForkMerge's terms, observed negative transfer (weak NT, unless no weighting fixes it). (2) It separates NT from its candidate mechanisms. The project's cancellation ratio 1 − ||Σ wᵢΔᵢ|| / Σ wᵢ||Δᵢ|| and the pairwise trunk-delta cosines are gradient-conflict diagnostics in the sense of Def. 3.4, and ForkMerge's Finding 1 explicitly warns that conflict and NT are decoupled. This supports the project's own finding: low inter-dataset cancellation does not rule out NT, and NT can come from other causes such as step dilution or distribution shift. (3) Finding 2 suggests a generalization-side diagnostic, measuring how far the shared trunk's training mixture shifts away from each client's test distribution (e.g. a CSD-like confidence gap per client). (4) Template for an ablation: a validation-gated aggregation at each round, θ_client = (1−λ*)·θ_local + λ*·θ_aggregated, with λ* chosen on the client's own validation slice (λ* = 0 means strong NT; 0 < λ* < 1 means weak NT). This is a per-client, per-round estimator of whether the aggregated shared update beats the local one. The interpolation weight also directly tests the step-dilution hypothesis, since λ* > 1 or a rescaled server step would mean the aggregate is merely too short. Caveats: ForkMerge needs the branches to share an initialization and to be merged often, which matches FedAvg rounds. The validation slices already exist in the master partition, but using them for λ* changes model selection, so final test slices must stay untouched.

## Selective Task Group Updates for Multi-Task Optimization (2025)

- **Autores:** Wooseong Jeong, Kuk-Jin Yoon
- **Venue:** ICLR 2025 (poster); arXiv:2502.11986 v2
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11986
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** True

**Termo usado.** negative transfer; inter-task affinity; proximal inter-task affinity; gradient conflict (used informally, not defined)

**Definição (literal).** Abstract: "training all tasks together in a single architecture can lead to performance degradation, known as negative transfer". Section 1: "This phenomenon, characterized by a trade-off among tasks' performances, is referred to as negative transfer."

**Local.** Abstract and Section 1 (Introduction) for negative transfer. Section 3.2 has Definition 1 (inter-task affinity, Eq. 1) and Definition 2 (proximal inter-task affinity, Eq. 2).

**Formulação.** The paper defines negative transfer only informally, as a performance trade-off or degradation when tasks are trained jointly. What it formalizes is the measurement instrument, which is lookahead loss reduction.

Definition 1 (inter-task affinity, Eq. 1; the TAG-style definition of Fifty et al. 2021):
A^t_{i->k} = 1 - L_k(z^t, Θ_s^{t+1|i}, Θ_k^t) / L_k(z^t, Θ_s^t, Θ_k^t), where Θ_s^{t+1|i} = Θ_s^t - η ∇L_i(z^t, Θ_s^t, Θ_i^t).
Positive means a shared-parameter step on task i lowers task k's loss on the same batch. Negative means it raises it, and the paper reads that as negative transfer.

Definition 2 (proximal inter-task affinity, Eq. 2), for a task group G:
B^t_{G->k} = 1 - L_k(z^t, Θ_s^{t+1|G}, Θ_k^{t+1}) / L_k(z^t, Θ_s^t, Θ_k^t).
The key difference is that task k's own task-specific parameters are also updated in the numerator. The measure therefore covers both shared and head updates, and it can be read off the sequential group updates the method already performs.

Affinities are smoothed with an exponential moving average (decay β = 0.001). Two tasks count as positively related when both B_{i->j} and B_{j->i} are positive. Under convexity the paper shows A ≤ B (Appendix A.1). Theorem 1 links higher affinity to a larger gradient dot product g_i·g_k when η is small. Theorem 2 shows that gradient alignment implies a lower post-update loss for task k.

**Métrica.** Negative transfer is measured in two ways.

1. During training: the proximal inter-task affinity B (loss-ratio lookahead on the current batch, EMA-smoothed). Its sign decides grouping.
2. In evaluation: Δm, the average per-task relative performance drop against single-task baselines (Maninis et al. 2019). A negative Δm means multi-task learning does worse than single-task learning.

The paper reasons with gradient dot products in its theory. It does not use cosine similarity or any update-norm cancellation ratio.

**Método.** Selective task group updates.

- Tasks are split into mutually exclusive groups {G_1..G_M}.
- Each training step is split into M sub-steps. The groups are updated one at a time, in random order, on the same batch. Each sub-step backpropagates the group's summed loss through the shared parameters and the group's task-specific heads.
- Proximal affinity is collected for free from these sequential updates (the loss change of task k after group G's step, heads included). It is EMA-tracked.
- Initially every task is its own group. Tasks with mutual positive affinity are clustered together, and the number of groups changes over training (about 1.8 groups for 11 Taskonomy tasks).

Theory (Theorems 3–5) argues that grouping positively related tasks raises affinity. It also argues that sequential group updates let task-specific parameters adapt between shared steps, which can give a lower multi-task loss than one joint backpropagation. Theorem 4 gives a descent and convergence bound for η ≤ min(2/(H𝒦), 1/(H|G_m|)).

**Resultados.** Δm (higher is better, relative to single-task):
- Taskonomy, 11 tasks, ViT-L: −1.42 ± 0.21, against GD −8.65, FAMO −7.58, IMTL −8.03 and Aligned-MTL −6.22 (the best baseline).
- NYUD-v2: −2.58, against Nash-MTL −3.14, IMTL −3.31 and Aligned-MTL −4.12.
- PASCAL-Context: −6.24, against IMTL −7.07, Aligned-MTL −7.20 and GD −9.06.

Ablation on Taskonomy: random, forward and backward group order give about the same result (−1.41/−1.44). One group per task (fully sequential) gives −2.48. Joint backpropagation gives −8.65. So most of the gain comes from sequential updating, and affinity-based grouping adds a little more.

Cost: 1.02 s per batch against 0.33 s for plain GD (Table E.1). That is cheaper than gradient-manipulation methods that need one backward pass per task. Clustering and affinity bookkeeping take about 4% of the time.

Even the best method still has a negative Δm on every benchmark, meaning some negative transfer against single-task models remains.

**Limitações.** - The paper has no dedicated limitations section.
- Proximal affinity is explicitly "an approximation of inter-task affinity". It is a single-batch, first-order lookahead.
- The theory assumes convex, differentiable losses, negligible change in affinity within a step, and an assumed "correlation between the learning of shared parameters and task-specific parameters" (needed for convergence to Pareto-stationary points).
- It is about 3× slower per batch than joint gradient descent.
- It is evaluated only on centralized, dense-prediction MTL with all task labels on every image (NYUD-v2, PASCAL-Context, Taskonomy). There is no federated, non-IID, cross-domain or partial-label setting.
- Negative transfer is never defined formally. Δm mixes negative transfer with capacity effects.
- Cosine similarity and update-cancellation measures are not studied.

**Relevância para o projeto.** 1. Definitions: the paper is a useful reference for how SOTA multi-task optimization (MTO) defines negative transfer. It is an outcome-level trade-off or degradation relative to single-task training, measured with Δm. It is not defined as geometric cancellation. That supports framing the project's federated-vs-local-only gap as negative transfer in the outcome sense, separate from the mechanism probes. The project's 1 − ||Σw_iΔ_i|| / Σw_i||Δ_i|| and pairwise cosine are geometric conflict measures. This paper instead measures transfer as a loss ratio after an actual step.

2. A concrete add-on probe: proximal affinity could be adapted per round. For each client or dataset pair, evaluate L_k on client k's validation batch before and after applying client i's trunk delta (or the aggregated delta), with and without k's personalized head re-adapting. This tests transfer directly instead of through geometry. It fits the project's finding that cancellation is low but federated still underperforms, which suggests the problem is step dilution or misaligned heads rather than opposing directions.

3. Mechanism: the paper's central claim is that letting task-specific parameters adapt between shared updates reduces conflict in the shared parameters. That parallels FedPer's personalized heads and stems. It suggests a federated analogue: sequential, grouped or clustered aggregation (for example, grouping datasets by measured affinity) instead of one simultaneous FedAvg step. The near-orthogonality the project observed in its cosine probes also fits the paper's Theorem 1 link between affinity and dot product: near-zero dot products imply little positive or negative transfer from shared steps.

4. Caveat: the setting is centralized, with labels for every task on every image. It does not transfer directly to federated, cross-domain clients, where each client's data and loss are private. The lookahead would need a held-out batch on each client.

## Can Optimization Trajectories Explain Multi-Task Transfer? (2025)

- **Autores:** David Mueller, Mark Dredze, Nicholas Andrews (Johns Hopkins University)
- **Venue:** Transactions on Machine Learning Research (TMLR), 2025. Read from arXiv:2408.14677v2, 30 Jan 2025, CC BY 4.0. I took the TMLR venue from the brief and did not check it on OpenReview.
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.14677
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "negative transfer" / "positive transfer" (multi-task transfer); "gradient conflict" (split into "directional conflict" and "magnitude conflict"); "generalization gap". The paper does not use "cancellation" and does not formally define "interference".

**Definição (literal).** Sec. 2.1 (Multi-Task Optimization and Transfer): "The hope of multi-task learning is that solving equation 1 will yield a solution that results in better generalization for each task than the solution found by solving each task individually. More formally, let [E_k] be a measure of generalization on unseen samples of the task distribution for task [k], given some metric; our hope is that multi-task transfer is positive, which occurs when [E_k(Θ_MT) > E_k(Θ_ST)]." ... "The UMTG is thought to frequently result in poor performance for multi-task learning, often yielding worse generalization than single-task learning, a phenomenon named negative transfer (Zhang et al., 2023). Negative transfer is often attributed to gradient conflict in the parameters of [the shared encoder], which is typically thought to arise in two different manners (Liu et al., 2021b; Javaloy & Valera, 2022): directional conflict occurs when the angle between task gradients is high, preventing any single direction from locally optimizing all tasks jointly (Yu et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021, e.g.); separately, magnitude conflict can arise when the magnitude of task gradients are disparate, resulting in the under-optimization of certain tasks (Chen et al., 2018, e.g.)." Sec. 3.1: "this transfer occurs as a generalization gap---a difference in generalization between two trajectories at comparable training loss---early into training and is then maintained throughout the rest of optimization." (Bracketed math symbols were stripped by the HTML rendering and are reconstructed from context.)

**Local.** Sec. 2.1 (Eqs. 1–2, definition of transfer, negative transfer and directional vs magnitude conflict); Sec. 3.1 (generalization gap); Sec. 4, Eqs. 3–5 (sharpness, gradient coherence, Fisher information); Sec. 5, Eq. 6 (gradient similarity / conflict measure); Sec. 5.2 and Sec. 6 (main conclusions)

**Formulação.** Transfer for task k is the test-performance difference ΔE_k = E_k(Θ_MT) − E_k(Θ_ST), where E_k is a generalization metric on held-out data from task k's distribution. Positive transfer means ΔE_k > 0; negative transfer means multi-task training generalizes worse than single-task training (ΔE_k < 0). This is an outcome-level definition on held-out performance, not a gradient-level one. The objective (Eq. 1) is min over the shared parameters θ_sh and task parameters θ_k of Σ_k w_k L_k. The UMTG (Eq. 2) is the uniform average or sum of the task gradients on the shared parameters. Gradient conflict is an optimization-level quantity. It has a directional form (large angle between task gradients) and a magnitude form (very different gradient norms). It is measured (Eq. 6) as the cosine similarity between the target task's gradient and the UMTG on the concatenated shared-encoder parameters, averaged over 200 random batches. The generalization gap is the difference in generalization between the single-task and multi-task trajectories at comparable training loss.

**Métrica.** Transfer: ΔE = E(MTL) − E(STL), the test accuracy or metric of the multi-task model minus that of the single-task model. Conflict (Eq. 6): cos(∇L_target, UMTG) on the shared-encoder parameters, averaged over 200 batches and tracked per epoch. Trajectory factors: worst-case ℓ∞ sharpness (Eq. 3, Andriushchenko et al. 2023, ρ=1e-3, |B|=128); gradient coherence, the trace of the gradient covariance (Eq. 4, Smith et al. 2021); early-phase Fisher information trace (Eq. 5, Jastrzebski et al. 2021). Generalization gap: the STL vs MTL test difference at matched training loss. Results are reported as Pearson r between average conflict and each optimization factor or test accuracy.

**Método.** This is an empirical, diagnostic study. It proposes no new method. Settings: FashionMTL (a synthetic target task with one helpful auxiliary task and one label-permuted "NoisyFashion" task that causes negative transfer; LeNet), MNISTS (MNIST, FashionMNIST, NotMNIST; LeNet), CIFAR-100 as 20 five-class tasks (ResNet18), CelebA with 40 attributes (ResNet18), Cityscapes segmentation plus depth (DeepLabV3/ResNet101), and GLUE with 8 tasks (RoBERTa-base). Training uses Adam with hyperparameter searches. (1) Sec. 3 plots training loss against test performance for the STL and MTL trajectories to locate when transfer appears. (2) Sec. 4 compares sharpness, Fisher information and gradient coherence along the STL and MTL trajectories for tasks with significant positive or negative transfer. It also checks whether specialized multi-task optimizers (MGDA, PCGrad, GradNorm) change generalization through these factors. (3) Sec. 5 builds CIFAR-100 target tasks with random auxiliary sets of size 2, 5, 10 and 20, which varies the amount of conflict. It then correlates the average target-vs-UMTG gradient similarity with the optimization factors (Sec. 5.1) and with target-task test accuracy (Sec. 5.2).

**Resultados.** (1) Transfer, positive or negative, shows up as a generalization gap early in training. In FashionMTL it is visible at training losses more than 3 orders of magnitude above the converged loss. Final training-loss minimization therefore does not explain transfer. (2) Sharpness, Fisher information and gradient coherence do not explain it either. MTL usually worsens or leaves these factors unchanged whether transfer turns out positive or negative. Table 1 examples: CIFAR-100 Fish ΔE=+3.49; Large Carnivores ΔE=−3.39; CelebA Double Chin ΔE=+5.30; GLUE STS-B ΔE≈−0.01 despite Δsharpness=189.82. (3) When SMTOs (MGDA, PCGrad, GradNorm) change which tasks win or lose, the matching changes in optimization factors or in training-loss minimization do not explain it. (4) Sec. 5.1: lower gradient similarity, meaning more conflict, correlates consistently with worse optimization factors (higher FIM explosion, sharpness and gradient covariance). Conflict therefore predictably hurts optimization. (5) Sec. 5.2: "the gradient similarity between the target-task and the multi-task gradient has very little correlation with target-task generalization. In fact, for the target-task Fish, the auxiliary setting with the lowest gradient similarity to the target-task ... yields the strongest generalization of all auxiliary settings." Conclusion (Sec. 6): "high amounts of gradient conflict are highly correlated with [worse optimization], while having almost no correlation with task generalization. Overall, we show that predicting which tasks will experience positive transfer and which will experience negative transfer, through the lens of optimization alone, is currently not feasible." The abstract says conflict "is correlated with negative effects to task optimization, but is not predictive of generalization."

**Limitações.** The authors write (Limitations): "our takeaways are limited by the number of settings and the breadth of models that we consider ... there remains the possibility that our results do not generalize to other tasks or domains," and the conclusions are limited to the optimization factors they studied. Further limitations relevant here: (a) the setting is centralized multi-task learning with i.i.d. task data, with no federated learning, no local multi-step updates, no client drift and no aggregation of model deltas; (b) conflict is measured as per-batch gradient cosine against the UMTG, not as the cosine or cancellation of multi-step parameter deltas; (c) the Sec. 5 correlation analysis covers only 3 CIFAR-100 target tasks; (d) the evidence is correlational over a modest number of auxiliary-set configurations; (e) there are no medical, cross-modality or cross-domain tasks. The nearest are MNISTS (cross-dataset digits and clothing) and Cityscapes segmentation plus depth. (f) The HTML rendering drops the Pearson r values in Figure 5, so the exact correlation sizes were not extracted. There is also a minor inconsistency in per-task CIFAR sample counts (Sec. 2.2 vs App. B).

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is the main citation for the claim that conflict is not the same as negative transfer. It also gives the outcome-level definition the thesis should adopt. Negative transfer for client/task k means E_k(federated) < E_k(local-only) on held-out data, which matches the project's federated-vs-local-only comparison exactly. It does not mean high gradient or delta conflict. The paper separates three levels: (1) conflict, an optimization quantity measured by the cosine between task gradients; (2) its effect on optimization, which the paper shows conflict does predictably hurt; (3) transfer, a generalization outcome that conflict does not predict. The project's cancellation metric, 1 − ||Σ w_i Δ_i|| / Σ w_i ||Δ_i|| (Nagura et al. N/E), and its pairwise cosine between client trunk deltas are level-1 measures in this taxonomy. They differ in that they use multi-step deltas, not per-batch gradients, and they are taken at aggregation. So the project's finding (little inter-dataset cancellation, near-orthogonal deltas, yet federated underperforms local-only) agrees with Mueller et al.: low conflict does not guarantee positive transfer, and the loss to local-only must come from elsewhere. The paper's "magnitude conflict" (disparate norms leading to under-optimization) is the closest named concept to the project's step-dilution finding (the aggregated step is about 10x smaller), and near-orthogonal averaging is a mechanism that produces exactly that shrinkage. The sharp-transfer-early result suggests the project should look at early rounds, for example the federated-vs-local validation gap in the first rounds. It also explains why gradient-surgery fixes (PCGrad-style) are unlikely to recover the gap. Caveats for the thesis: the paper's setting is centralized, i.i.d., same-domain-ish multi-task learning. Its conclusion therefore supports the project's interpretation by analogy and should not be cited as direct federated evidence. Following the project's rules, any correlation the project itself reports should stay exploratory.

## FedGain: Toward Negative-Gain-Free Client Collaboration in Federated Learning (2026)

- **Autores:** Yuqing Zhang, Changli Zhou (corresponding), BingHuang Huang, Hui Tian (Huaqiao University, College of Computer Science and Technology)
- **Venue:** ICML 2026 (poster; https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/poster/60625)
- **URL:** https://openreview.net/forum?id=yLn1COP0AN
- **Acesso:** abstract_only · **confiança:** medium · **candidato a SOTA:** True

**Termo usado.** "negative gain(s)" (federation-vs-local negative transfer); also "Effective Federated Capacity" (EFC) and a "modified (Heterogeneous) Federated Scaling Law" (HFSL)

**Definição (literal).** Abstract (ICML poster page): "Data heterogeneity is a fundamental challenge in Federated Learning (FL), where induced model drift often results in "negative gains" for global models on data-abundant clients, with performance falling below that of local training." Poster, "Challenge & Motivation" panel: "Prevalent Negative Gains: Data heterogeneity (distribution shift & quantity skew) causes global models to underperform local training, breaking Neural Scaling Laws in FL." Framework panel goal: "The training results perform no worse than local training."

**Local.** Abstract (ICML 2026 virtual poster page) and the official poster PNG (Challenge & Motivation and Framework panels). The full paper could not be read: OpenReview PDF and API sit behind a bot challenge (HTTP 403), and I found no arXiv or PMLR version.

**Formulação.** There is no explicit equation for "gain" in the material I could reach. In words, a client has negative gain when the collaborative (global or cluster) model's test performance on that client is below the model it would get from local training alone, i.e. gain_i = Perf(collab model on client i) - Perf(local model on client i) < 0. The results tables report "NGP" (lower is better). It is almost certainly the Negative Gain Proportion, the % of clients with negative gain: its values are multiples of 2.5 or 5, which fits a percentage of 20 or 40 clients. The poster does not spell out that acronym, though, and it does not define "RSD" (lower is better, probably the relative standard deviation of per-client accuracy). Formulas shown on the poster (Method panel): Effective Federated Capacity M_eff^(i) = m_i + sum_{j!=i} beta(D(P_i,P_j)) * m_j, with distribution distance D(P_i,P_j) = 1 - mu_i^T mu_j / (||mu_i|| ||mu_j||). That is cosine distance between client mean feature embeddings from a shared extractor trained with InfoNCE, and it is NOT computed on update deltas. Data-scale-dependent generalization term Psi_i(alpha,m,D,delta) = C * (m_i + sum_{j in C_k, j!=i} alpha_j m_j e^{-gamma D})^{-nu}. Error bound for the cluster model, with delta in (0,1/2): r_i(h_{C_k}) <= r_i(h_i*) + 2 sum_{j in C_k, j!=i} alpha_j D(P_i,P_j) + 2 Psi_i(alpha,m,D,delta). Read together, collaboration pays only when the drop in the power-law generalization term (more effective samples) outweighs the 2*sum alpha_j D bias from distribution shift. Negative gain is the case where it does not.

**Métrica.** Per-client accuracy versus a Local Train baseline. NGP (almost certainly the % of clients whose collaborative model scores below local training). RSD (spread across clients, probably the relative standard deviation). Scaling curves of test loss versus M_eff, and a "data utilization efficiency" (%) under IID versus non-IID sampling.

**Método.** This is a clustered FL (CFL) framework that separates structure optimization from task training. (1) A shared feature extractor trained with InfoNCE embeds each client's data, and pairwise distribution distance is the cosine distance between client mean embeddings. (2) A modified federated scaling law, test loss as a power law in effective samples where other clients' samples are discounted by exp(-gamma*D), defines Effective Federated Capacity. Unlike CFL methods that cluster on distribution distance alone, this also accounts for data-quantity skew. (3) The collaboration structure, meaning which clients join which cluster, is solved to minimize the generalization-error bound above, trading distribution-shift bias against scale gain. (4) Any GFL or PFL algorithm (FedAvg, FedProx, FedNova, FedCSD, fine-tuning, Per-FedAvg, pFedMe, Ditto, FedAS) then runs inside each cluster. FedGain with FedAvg is the basic CFL variant.

**Resultados.** From the poster table, mean (std): CIFAR-10 (feature shift), CIFAR-100 ("quality shift") and FMNIST (label shift). Local Train scores 35.63 / 85.63 / 29.18 accuracy. Plain FedAvg scores 44.74 / 46.62 / 26.27 with NGP 10 / 55 / 50%, so on FMNIST and CIFAR-100 most clients lose against local training. FedAvg+FedGain scores 50.44 / 90.16 / 37.95 with NGP 0 / 0 / 0. Across the 9 base algorithms, FedGain cuts NGP to 0-17.5%, against 5-70% for the base algorithms. In the ablation, distribution-only clustering leaves NGP at 5 / 60 / 60%, while quantity-only clustering gets 0 / 10 / 10. Data quantity matters at least as much as distribution distance. Scaling plot: under Dirichlet non-IID, test loss still follows a power law in M_eff, while plotted against raw sample count the curve breaks. The authors say FedGain beats the CFL baselines FedCluster, FeSEM and CFL-GP.

**Limitações.** I could read only the abstract and the poster. I could not verify the exact gain/NGP/RSD definitions, the number of clients, beta(.), how the clustering problem is solved, or the confidence intervals. The experiments are small-scale vision benchmarks (CIFAR-10/100, FMNIST) with a single task per client and a single domain per benchmark, using synthetic shift. There is no multi-task, no cross-modality, no segmentation and no medical data. Clustering removes negative gain partly by not collaborating: a client whose best cluster is itself falls back to local training, so a "negative-gain-free" result can mean the federation simply did not happen. The distance works in feature/data space (client mean embeddings) and not on update geometry, so it does not measure aggregation-time cancellation or conflict. It also needs a shared InfoNCE extractor, plus sharing of mean embeddings, which has privacy implications.

**Relevância para o projeto.** High for terminology and framing, lower for mechanism. (1) It gives a citable, recent top-venue name for exactly what the project sees: the federated model scoring below local-only on a client. The paper calls this "negative gain" and measures it per client as a share of clients. The project could report an NGP-style metric, the fraction of client×task×fold cells where FedPer loses to local-only, next to mean deltas. (2) It locates the cause in the same two factors the project has: distribution shift (cross-domain BUSI/ISIC/TCGA/SIIM) and quantity skew (small BUSI against large ISIC/SIIM). The bound r_i(h_C) <= r_i(h_i*) + 2*sum alpha_j D_ij + 2*Psi says negative gain appears when the bias from mixing distant clients is larger than the gain from extra effective samples. That explanation runs through the objective and data, not through update cancellation, so it fits the project's finding of little inter-dataset cancellation: negative transfer can happen without update conflict. (3) Its ablation, where quantity-aware grouping beats distance-only grouping, backs testing per-dataset or cluster-wise sharing in the project, i.e. federating only within compatible groups, and backs looking at aggregation weights and quantity skew. It also connects to the step-dilution finding: under hierarchical 50/50 weighting a large client's effective contribution is cut down. (4) Caveat: FedGain's D is a cosine distance between mean features, not the project's cosine between trunk deltas or Nagura-style N/E cancellation, so the two measures are not interchangeable. Cite FedGain for the definition of negative gain and the scale-versus-shift trade-off, not as a measure of gradient interference.

## OmniFM: Toward Modality-Robust and Task-Agnostic Federated Learning for Heterogeneous Medical Imaging (2026)

- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** medium · **candidato a SOTA:** True

**Termo usado.** The paper never says "negative transfer", "interference", "gradient conflict" or "cancellation". Its terms are "modality-induced (optimization) drift", "representation divergence", "aggregation drift", "conflicting minima" and "diverging loss landscapes". The phenomenon is framed as cross-modality heterogeneity, not task conflict.

**Definição (literal).** §1 Introduction: "Consequently, local models optimize against diverging loss landscapes; during aggregation [...] the global model is pulled toward conflicting minima." / "After aggregation, the global model shifts toward a suboptimal saddle-like region [...] leading to slower convergence and oscillatory behavior across communication rounds." §2: "cross-modality setting that exacerbates representation divergence and aggregation drift". §3.2 (SPAlign): "Although prompting provides modality-invariant cues, local updates may still drift toward modality-specific minima." (These are verbatim fragments as extracted from the arXiv HTML. The fetch tool limited quote length, so some sentences are excerpts, and the [...] marks show where text was cut.)

**Local.** §1 Introduction (paragraph on modality heterogeneity, citing Fig. 2); §2 Related Work (FMIL); §3.2 SPAlign (Eqs. 5-6); §3.3 Knowledge Bank Update (Eq. 7)

**Formulação.** The paper gives no formal definition or measure of the phenomenon. The idea stays qualitative: modality shifts give each client a loss basin with a different minimum, and averaging the client models lands between those basins. Fig. 2 illustrates this as a sketch only. The related formal parts are as follows. The global objective (Eq. 2) is min Σ_k (n_k/N) F_k(θ_k). The remedy, SPAlign (Eq. 5), is L_align(s, S_g) = ||s − s̄_g||²₂, where s̄_g = (1/k) Σ_{s'∈S_g} s' is the barycenter of the top-k global spectral prototypes retrieved for that client. The local objective (Eq. 6) is L_task(h_ψ([Z‖r‖c]), y) + λ·L_align. The low-frequency component is S_LP = P_LP(|FFT(x)|), but the cutoff and the exact low-pass operator are not given.

**Métrica.** The paper has no metric for drift, conflict or cancellation: no gradient or update cosine, no update norms, no divergence statistic and no quantitative loss curves. Fig. 2 is illustrative. The claim that low-frequency spectra are closer across modalities than full spectra is stated without numbers. Only task metrics are reported: accuracy (classification), PSNR (super-resolution), Dice/IoU (segmentation) and VQA accuracy/scores.

**Método.** OmniFM shares low-frequency spectral (assumed modality-invariant) knowledge instead of model weights. Each client computes the FFT magnitude of its input, applies a low-pass operator and encodes the result into a spectral embedding s_k. The server keeps a global knowledge bank of these embeddings (§3.3), and clients upload only spectral embeddings, not backbone or head parameters. The method has three components. (1) Global spectral knowledge retrieval: top-k prototypes are pulled from the bank. (2) Embedding-wise cross-attention fuses the retrieved priors with local features. (3) Prefix-suffix spectral prompting combines global prefix prompts with client-specific suffix CLS tokens. The SPAlign term pulls a client's spectral embedding toward the barycenter of its retrieved prototypes, a FedProx-like proximal term in spectral-embedding space. The paper is internally inconsistent on what is shared. §3.2 says backbone g_φ and head h_ψ are "retained locally", while §3.3 and Fig. 3 mention broadcasting aggregated backbone parameters.

**Resultados.** OmniFM is best in every reported cell.

At 20% participation: 96.85%, 84.47% and 56.31%. FedPer is the strongest baseline in most settings, but FedDyn leads at 100% in Scenarios 1 and 2.
- Super-resolution (BreaKHis, 3 clients), PSNR at x2: OmniFM 42.21, FedPer 39.52, FedAvg 35.95, Local 35.63. In Scenario 2 FedAvg beats FedPer. Gaps shrink at x8.
- Segmentation (FeTS2022, 4 institution groups), Dice/IoU: OmniFM 77.41/68.75, FedPer 73.86/62.36, FedAvg 44.16/27.73, Local 44.23/27.89. FedAvg roughly equals Local, and FedPer is far above both.

- Internal inconsistencies: the Table 6 average Dice (77.41) does not match the mean of its four groups (about 79.3) or the ablation SEG value (75.04).

**Limitações.** - No limitations section.
- The drift/conflict phenomenon is claimed and drawn, never measured: no update cosine, norm, cancellation or loss-landscape numbers.
- "Task-agnostic" means the same pipeline is reused for separate federations, each with a single task. No federation mixes segmentation and classification clients, so cross-task interference is never tested.
- Segmentation uses one modality (FeTS T1ce), so cross-modality segmentation is not tested.
- Single-seed point estimates, with no variance or significance reported.
- The low-pass cutoff is unspecified.
- What is shared versus personalized is described inconsistently.
- Numeric inconsistencies between tables.
- Local-only baselines are reported only for SR and segmentation, not classification. There, FedAvg roughly equals Local, which says little about whether federation beats local training for a personalized method.
- The SPAlign gains are about 1 point, with no variance reported, so its claimed role in "stabilizing aggregation" is weakly supported.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is the closest recent comparison point by setting (cross-modality medical FL, FedPer as a strong baseline), but it supports the project's measurements only weakly. (1) Terminology: it does not define negative transfer or cancellation. Its "modality-induced drift / conflicting minima" is a qualitative basin story, so it cannot serve as a source for a formal definition. Use Nagura et al. (N/E), the MTL gradient-conflict literature and FedMTL/FMTL-Bench for that. (2) The project's own finding, little inter-dataset cancellation with step dilution as the dominant cost, partly cuts against OmniFM's premise that modality heterogeneity causes conflicting updates. This is a citable contrast: OmniFM asserts conflict without measuring it, while the project measures it (cancellation 1−N/E with an orthogonal reference, plus pairwise cosine) and finds mostly near-orthogonality. (3) It reports FedPer beating FedAvg and Local by a wide margin on segmentation (73.9 vs 44 Dice), which differs from the project's federated < local-only result. Possible reasons: FeTS is single-modality, it uses different budgets, and Local looks under-trained there. The comparison is worth a caveat. (4) Its design idea, sharing only low-frequency or spectral knowledge plus a proximal alignment term, is a candidate mitigation or ablation, for example federating only modality-invariant parts or adding a FedProx-style term. A proximal term would not fix step dilution, though, which is a magnitude problem, not a direction problem. (5) It never mixes tasks inside a federation, so the project's seg+cls multi-task-client setting stays unaddressed by this "SOTA".
