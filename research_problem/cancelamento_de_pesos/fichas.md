## Clustered Federated Learning: Model-Agnostic Distributed Multi-Task Optimization under Privacy Constraints (2019)

- **Autores:** Felix Sattler, Klaus-Robert Müller, Wojciech Samek
- **Venue:** arXiv:1910.01991v1 (4 Oct 2019). Later published in IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks and Learning Systems (2021); this from memory, not checked here. The full text read was arXiv v1.
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "congruent" / "incongruent" clients (data-generating distributions). The paper never uses "cancellation", "weight cancellation" or "negative transfer". The phenomenon is described as client gradients/weight-updates that are "of positive norm and point into opposite directions" at a stationary point of the FL objective. The diagnostics are the cosine similarity between client weight-updates (alpha_ij), the "separation gap" g(alpha), the "average update norm" (server update) and the "maximum client norm".

**Definição (literal).** Sec. I, p.1, after Assumption 1 (Eq. 3): "In the following we will call a set of clients and their data generating distributions ϕ congruent (with respect to f and l) if they satisfy Assumption 1 and incongruent if they don't." Sec. II, Fig. 2 caption, p.3: "In the incongruent case Federated Learning converges to a stationary point of the FL objective where the gradients of the two clients are of positive norm and point into opposite directions. In the congruent case there exists an area (marked grey in the plot) where both risk functions are minimized. If Federated Learning converges to this area the norm of both client's gradient updates goes to zero. By inspecting the gradient norms the two cases can be distinguished." Sec. II-A, p.5: "When client distributions are incongruent, the stationary solution of the Federated Learning objective by definition can not be stationary for the individual clients. Hence the norm of the clients' gradients has to be strictly greater than zero." Sec. VI-B, Fig. 7 caption, p.10: "In both cases Federated Learning converges to a stationary point of F(θ) and the average update norm (32) goes to zero. In the congruent case the maximum norm of the client updates (33) decreases along with the server update norm, while in contrast in the incongruent case it stagnates and even increases."

**Local.** Sec. I, Assumption 1 / Eq. (3), p.1 (congruence). Sec. II, Eqs. (11)-(13) and Fig. 2, p.3 (opposing gradients at the stationary point). Sec. II-A, Eqs. (32)-(33), p.5 (norm criteria). Sec. V-A, Eq. (44), p.7 (cosine on weight-updates). Algorithm 5 line 14, p.8. Sec. VI-B, Fig. 7, p.10.

**Formulação.** Assumption 1 (FL), Eq. 3: there exists θ* with R_i(θ*) <= R_i(θ) for all θ in B_ε(θ*), for i=1..m. Clients that satisfy it are congruent; clients that do not are incongruent. FL objective, Eq. 10: F(θ)=Σ_i |D_i|/|D| r_i(θ)=a1 R1 + a2 R2. At a stationary point, Eq. 11: 0=a1∇R1(θ*)+a2∇R2(θ*). In the incongruent case, Eq. 12: ∇R1(θ*) = -(a2/a1)∇R2(θ*) ≠ 0, so the cosine is α_ij = +1 if I(i)=I(j) and -1 otherwise (Eq. 13). In other words, the weighted client gradients cancel exactly while each one stays non-zero. Split criteria: (a) Eq. 32, ||Σ_i |D_i|/|D_c| ∇r_i(θ*)|| < ε1 (server/average update near zero); (b) Eq. 33, max_i ||∇r_i(θ*)|| > ε2 > 0 (clients far from their own stationary points). In practice (Alg. 5, line 14) this becomes ||Δθ_c|| < ε1 and max_i ||Δθ_i|| > ε2, with Δθ_c = (1/|c|) Σ_i Δθ_i, an unweighted mean in Alg. 5. Cosine on weight-updates, Eq. 44: α_ij = <Δθ_i,Δθ_j>/(||Δθ_i|| ||Δθ_j||). Bipartition, Eq. 25/35: argmin over c1∪c2 of max_{i∈c1,j∈c2} α_ij. Separation gap, Def. 2 / Eq. 28: g(α)=α_intra^min - α_cross^max; g>0 iff the bipartition is correct. Theorem 1 bounds α_cross^max and α_intra^min through the relative approximation noise γ_i = ||∇R_I(i) - ∇r_i|| / ||∇R_I(i)||. The split is accepted if γ_max < sqrt((1-α_cross^max)/2) (Eq. 39). No ratio of the two norms is defined. The two thresholds (ε1, ε2) are applied separately.

**Métrica.** (1) Pairwise cosine similarity between client weight-updates, Eq. 44 (the paper finds weight-updates over 3 local epochs separate clients better than full gradients, Figs. 5-6). (2) Separation gap g(α)=α_intra^min - α_cross^max (Def. 2). (3) Norm of the averaged (server) update, ||mean_i Δθ_i||, versus the maximum client update norm max_i ||Δθ_i||. Incongruence shows as a server norm near zero while the max client norm stays high or grows (Fig. 7). The two norms are thresholded separately with ε1 and ε2; they are never combined into a normalized ratio.

**Método.** Clustered Federated Learning (CFL) runs standard FedAvg until it reaches an approximately stationary point. If the server update is small but some client update is still large, the server computes pairwise cosine similarities of client weight-updates. It then finds the bipartition that minimizes the maximum cross-cluster similarity (O(m^3), Alg. 1), accepts it if the γ_max criterion (Eq. 39) holds, and recurses on each sub-cluster from θ* (Alg. 3/5). This yields a parameter tree of increasingly specialized models. It needs no change to the FL protocol, works with any non-convex model, and is compatible with permutation-based privacy, since cosines and norms are invariant to orthonormal transforms. New clients join by descending the tree (Alg. 4). The paper frames it as a post-processing step that can always fall back to the FL solution.

**Resultados.** Theory: at a stationary point, client-update cosine provably separates clients with different data-generating distributions (Separation Theorem 1, Corollary 1), even when empirical risk approximates true risk only loosely (simulations with γ>1 and k>10, Fig. 3). Experiments use MNIST and CIFAR-10 with 20 clients in 4 clusters built by label swap or label permutation. On MNIST a positive separation gap needs only about 20 samples per client; on CIFAR it needs about 500 (Fig. 5). It appears after about 10 rounds (Fig. 6). Weight-updates separate better than gradients. With 2 MNIST clients over 1000 rounds, the server update norm goes to zero in both cases. The max client norm decays in the congruent case and stagnates or rises in the incongruent case (Fig. 7). CIFAR-10 permuted labels: FL stagnates at about 20% accuracy; three CFL splits (rounds 50/100/150) bring it to about 60% (Fig. 8). On Ag-News LSTM, perplexity improves from about 42 (FL) to under 36 after 3 splits (Fig. 9).

**Limitações.** All incongruence is synthetic: label swaps or permutations give conditional-distribution conflict ϕ(y|x), with identical inputs across clients, except Ag-News topics. There is no real cross-domain or medical setting and no multi-task heads. The guarantees hold only at a stationary point and use full gradients; weight-updates are justified by a first-order Taylor argument. The norm criteria use absolute thresholds ε1 and ε2 that are scale-dependent; there is no normalized cancellation ratio and no orthogonal/random baseline. In high dimensions, near-zero cosines are expected for unrelated updates, which the paper mentions only in passing as making separation easier. Clustering is all-or-nothing on the full model, with no partial or shared-trunk sharing. Clustering needs at least k-1 FL convergence phases. Experiments are small (20 clients, small CNNs) and use a single run each.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is the closest classic antecedent of the project's diagnostics. The pairwise cosine between client deltas is exactly CFL's α_ij (Eq. 44). The project's cancellation ratio 1 - ||Σ w_i Δ_i|| / Σ w_i ||Δ_i|| is a normalized, single-number version of CFL's two-threshold test: server update norm small (Eq. 32) and max client norm large (Eq. 33). CFL uses the gap between these norms as its operational sign that clients are incongruent, meaning no shared parameter fits them all. Terminology to cite: CFL calls the phenomenon "incongruence", not "cancellation" or "negative transfer". It characterizes it as client updates that stay large but point in opposing directions, so that the weighted average vanishes at a stationary point (Eqs. 11-13). Caveats for the project: (a) CFL's signal is defined at or near convergence, so cancellation measured early or mid-training is not directly comparable. (b) CFL never compares against an orthogonal reference. The project's orthogonal_reference control is an addition that CFL lacks: in high dimensions a server norm of about (1/√m)·client norm comes from orthogonality alone, not opposition. (c) The project's finding (low excess cancellation between datasets, mostly near-orthogonal deltas, with step dilution as the dominant cost) is the case CFL's binary cosine framing (+1 versus -1) does not cover. Near-orthogonal cross-domain updates produce a small server step (dilution) without the opposed directions that CFL's theory treats as incongruence. CFL's remedy, clustering clients (here roughly by dataset), is still a reasonable baseline or ablation for the FedPer cross-domain setup, as an alternative to sharing one trunk across all datasets.

## SCAFFOLD: Stochastic Controlled Averaging for Federated Learning (2020)

- **Autores:** Sai Praneeth Karimireddy, Satyen Kale, Mehryar Mohri, Sashank J. Reddi, Sebastian U. Stich, Ananda Theertha Suresh
- **Venue:** ICML 2020 (PMLR v119); arXiv:1910.06378
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06378
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "client-drift" (client drift). The paper does not use "negative transfer", "cancellation" or "conflict". It measures heterogeneity with bounded gradient dissimilarity (G, B) and Hessian dissimilarity (delta).

**Definição (literal).** Abstract: FedAvg "suffers from 'client-drift' when the data is heterogeneous (non-iid), resulting in unstable and slow convergence." Section 3.2: "Even if all the clients start from the same point x, each of the y_i will move towards their client optimum x_i*." Figure 1 caption: "Client-drift in FedAvg is illustrated for 2 clients with 3 local steps ... The local updates y_i (in blue) move towards the individual client optima x_i* (orange square)." Section 3.2: "To counter this drift, FedAvg is forced to use much smaller step-sizes which in turn hurts convergence." Section 1: "The difference (c−c_i) is then an estimate of the client-drift which is used to correct the local update."

**Local.** Abstract; Section 1 (Introduction); Section 3.2 and Figure 1 (client drift and FedAvg lower bound, Theorem II); Section 2 (assumptions A1, A2)

**Formulação.** Client drift is never defined as a single scalar. It is characterized through:
(A1) (G,B)-bounded gradient dissimilarity: (1/N) Σ_i ||∇f_i(x)||^2 ≤ G^2 + B^2 ||∇f(x)||^2 for all x (G≥0, B≥1; G=0, B=1 means IID-like).
(A2) delta-bounded Hessian dissimilarity: ||∇²f_i(x) − ∇²f(x)|| ≤ δ for all x.
Drift is the gap between the local iterate y_i after K local steps, which heads toward x_i*, and the global-gradient trajectory. SCAFFOLD's local step (Eq. 3) is y_i ← y_i − η_l (g_i(y_i) + c − c_i). The control variates satisfy c_i ≈ g_i(y_i) and c ≈ (1/N) Σ_j g_j(y_i), so the correction term (c − c_i) estimates the drift. Option I updates c_i^+ = g_i(x) with an extra pass over the local data at the server model. Option II sets c_i^+ = c_i − c + (x − y_i)/(K η_l). Server update (Eq. 5): c ← c + (1/N) Σ_{i∈S} (c_i^+ − c_i).

**Métrica.** The paper has no empirical drift metric (it reports no cosine or norm-ratio). Heterogeneity is measured only by the theoretical constants G, B (A1) and δ (A2). The paper does not quantify drift directly. It judges methods by convergence speed: communication rounds to reach a target test accuracy (EMNIST logistic regression, 0.5 accuracy, Table 3) and final accuracy, while varying local epochs and the percentage of client "similarity" (0%, 10%, ... of IID data).

**Método.** SCAFFOLD adds server and client control variates (c, c_i) to FedAvg. Each local SGD step is corrected by (c − c_i), an estimate of the difference between the global and local update directions, so local trajectories track the global gradient instead of drifting to client optima. Clients are stateful and keep c_i across rounds. They send both the model delta and the control-variate delta, and the server averages both, with a global step size η_g.

**Resultados.** (1) Theorem I: a FedAvg convergence bound whose heterogeneity terms (scaled by G and B) do not vanish. Theorem II: a lower bound showing that μ-strongly convex functions satisfying A1 exist for which FedAvg's error is Ω(G²/(μR²)), and that this "is unavoidable even if there is no stochasticity". With heterogeneity, FedAvg can be slower than large-batch SGD.
(2) Theorem III: SCAFFOLD's rate "holds for arbitrarily heterogeneous clients unlike Theorem I" and "matches that of SGD with K times larger batch-size, proving that SCAFFOLD is at least as fast as SGD". It is also robust to client sampling.
(3) Theorem IV (quadratics only): under δ-Hessian similarity, local steps provably help. The complexity becomes ~δ/μ for large K and σ=0, and the optimum number of local steps is K ≈ β/δ.
(4) EMNIST experiments: "Across all parameters (epochs and similarity), SCAFFOLD is the fastest method"; "SCAFFOLD is consistently the best"; "SCAFFOLD is resilient to client sampling." The Table 3 numbers were not visible in the extracted text.

**Limitações.** These are stated in the paper:
- Theorem IV covers only quadratics: "Generalizing these results to other functions is a challenging open problem."
- Assuming G is negligible "is quite strong" (Appendix D.1).
- Clients "are stateful and retain the value of c_i across multiple rounds", which is a problem for cross-device settings with large client pools.
- For the non-convex experiments, "much more extensive experiments (beyond current scope) are needed".

These are my assessment, not stated in the text I read:
- Sending the control-variate delta adds uplink communication, which is commonly described as roughly doubling it. I could not confirm a verbatim "twice" sentence.
- The analysis assumes a single shared model with one objective per client. It does not address personalized heads (FedPer), multi-task objectives, or cross-domain feature shift.
- Drift is defined as an optimization-trajectory bias, not as directional conflict between update vectors.

**Relevância para o projeto.** SCAFFOLD is the canonical source for the term "client drift": with non-IID data, the local steps y_i move toward the client optima x_i*, so the average is a biased estimate of the global descent direction. This differs from the project's cancellation ratio 1 − ||Σ w_i Δ_i|| / Σ w_i ||Δ_i||. Drift can exist with little cancellation, because client updates can all be biased in a correlated direction, or can be nearly orthogonal (as the project's orthogonal reference shows) without cancelling each other.

The project's two findings line up with SCAFFOLD's mechanism:
- Little inter-dataset cancellation fits drift being a bias rather than opposition.
- The step dilution (~10x smaller aggregated step) parallels SCAFFOLD's observation that FedAvg must use "much smaller step-sizes" to counter drift.
- Federated underperforming local-only is consistent with Theorem II (FedAvg can be slower than SGD under heterogeneity, i.e. large G).

Practical implications:
- The A1 constants (G, B) give a formal vocabulary for heterogeneity across BUSI, ISIC, TCGA-LGG and SIIM-ACR. A cheap empirical proxy is the ratio of the mean squared client-gradient norm to the squared norm of the averaged gradient at the global model.
- SCAFFOLD's (c − c_i) correction, applied to the shared trunk only, plus a global server step size η_g > 1, are direct candidate interventions against step dilution.
- The global server step size η_g separates "the direction is fine but the step is too small" from "the direction is wrong".
- Caveat: SCAFFOLD assumes all parameters are shared and every client has the same objective, so applying it to FedPer with task-heterogeneous seg/cls clients would be an extension, not a replication.

## Tackling the Objective Inconsistency Problem in Heterogeneous Federated Optimization (2020)

- **Autores:** Jianyu Wang, Qinghua Liu, Hao Liang, Gauri Joshi, H. Vincent Poor
- **Venue:** NeurIPS 2020 (arXiv:2007.07481)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.07481
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "objective inconsistency" (also "solution bias", "surrogate objective", "inconsistent objective"; the size side is "effective number of steps" tau_eff and "slowdown" tau_bar/tau_eff). The paper never uses "cancellation" or "weight cancellation".

**Definição (literal).** Abstract: "Naive weighted aggregation of such models causes objective inconsistency, that is, the global model converges to a stationary point of a mismatched objective function which can be arbitrarily different from the true objective." Sec. 1 (Introduction, paragraph "Heterogeneity in Local Updates Causes Objective Inconsistency"): "standard averaging of client models after heterogeneous local updates results in convergence to a stationary point – not of the original objective function F(x), but of an inconsistent objective F̃(x), which can be arbitrarily different from F(x) depending upon the relative values of τ_i." Sec. 1, consistency defined as the opposite case: "objective consistency (that is, the surrogate objective F̃(x) equals to the true objective F(x))". Sec. 4.1 on the step-size side: "the server can scale up or scale down the effect of the aggregated updates by setting the parameter τ_eff larger or smaller than τ̄ (analogous to choosing a global learning rate [25, 40]). We refer to the ratio τ̄/τ_eff as the slowdown".

**Local.** Abstract; Sec. 1 Introduction; Sec. 3, Lemma 1 (quadratic case study); Sec. 4.1, Eqs. (4)-(6); Sec. 4.2, Theorems 1-2, Eqs. (8)-(10); Sec. 5, Eq. (12)

**Formulação.** FedAvg update: x^{t+1} - x^t = sum_i p_i Delta_i, with Delta_i = x_i^{(t,tau_i)} - x^{(t,0)} = -eta G_i a_i and p_i = n_i/n. Eqs. (5)-(6) rewrite this as -tau_eff * sum_i eta w_i d_i, with d_i = G_i a_i / ||a_i||_1 (the normalized gradient), tau_eff = sum_i p_i ||a_i||_1 (the effective local steps) and w_i = p_i ||a_i||_1 / sum_j p_j ||a_j||_1. General rule, Eq. (4): x^{t+1} - x^t = -tau_eff sum_i w_i eta d_i, which optimizes F̃(x) = sum_i w_i F_i(x) instead of F(x) = sum_i p_i F_i(x). For FedAvg with vanilla SGD: w_i = p_i tau_i / sum_j p_j tau_j. Lemma 1 (quadratic F_i = 1/2||x - e_i||^2): FedAvg converges to x̃* = sum_i tau_i e_i / sum_i tau_i, the minimizer of F̃ = sum_i tau_i F_i / sum_i tau_i. Theorem 2, Eq. (10): min_t ||∇F(x^t)||^2 <= 2[chi^2_{p||w}(beta^2 - 1) + 1] eps_opt + 2 chi^2_{p||w} kappa^2. The second term is the "non-vanishing error due to obj. inconsistency", with chi^2_{p||w} = sum_i (p_i - w_i)^2 / w_i. Appendix D.1 gives a matching lower bound Omega(chi^2 kappa^2). Slowdown is tau_bar/tau_eff with tau_bar = sum_i tau_i / m. FedNova, Eq. (12): x^{t+1} - x^t = -tau_eff^{(t)} sum_i p_i eta d_i^{(t)}. With SGD this equals (sum_i p_i tau_i) sum_i p_i Delta_i / tau_i, so each Delta_i is rescaled by (sum_j p_j tau_j)/tau_i.

**Métrica.** The paper has no empirical metric of inconsistency computed from client updates. Theory quantifies it in two ways. The weight bias is chi^2_{p||w}, the chi-square divergence between the intended weights p and the implicit weights w; with the dissimilarity constant kappa^2 it gives an error floor chi^2 kappa^2 that does not vanish. The magnitude side is the slowdown tau_bar/tau_eff. Fig. 4 plots "weight bias" (chi-square distance), "slowdown" and "relative variance" against the FedProx parameter. The quadratic case study measures the optimality gap between x̃* and x*. Experiments report only test accuracy and training curves.

**Método.** The paper gives a generalized update rule, Eq. (4). It factors any aggregation whose local change is a linear combination of local gradients into three parts: a direction set by the weights w_i over normalized gradients d_i, and a step length tau_eff. FedAvg, FedProx, SGD with decayed learning rate and local momentum are all special cases. In each of them w_i and tau_eff are implicitly tied to the local solver's ||a_i||_1, so clients that do more local work get more weight. FedNova removes the bias by averaging the normalized updates d_i with the true weights p_i. It then chooses tau_eff separately (for example sum_i p_i tau_i, which acts like a global learning rate). Clients only need to send Delta_i / ||a_i||_1.

**Resultados.** Theorem 1: any algorithm following Eq. (4) converges to a stationary point of F̃ = sum_i w_i F_i at rate O(1/sqrt(m tau_bar T)), and the slowdown tau_bar/tau_eff scales the first error term. Theorem 2: the error on the true objective has a floor 2 chi^2 kappa^2 that a smaller learning rate does not remove. The floor is zero only when p = w (same steps and same solver everywhere) or when local objectives are identical. FedProx: larger mu brings w closer to p but shrinks tau_eff, so it reduces inconsistency at the cost of slower convergence. Theorem 3: FedNova converges to a stationary point of the true F at O(1/sqrt(m tau_tilde T)), even when tau_i and the local solvers vary over time. With client sampling the rate is O(1/sqrt(q tau_tilde T)). Experiments: VGG-11 on CIFAR-10 split across 16 clients by Dir(0.1), 100 rounds, 3 seeds, local steps ranging from 16 to 408 (E=2) or 16 to 1020 (E~U(2,5)). Table 1 test accuracy, FedAvg vs FedNova: vanilla 60.68 vs 66.31 (E=2) and 64.22 vs 73.22 (random E); momentum 65.26 vs 73.32 and 70.44 vs 77.07; proximal 60.44 vs 69.92 and 63.74 vs 73.41; VR/SCAFFOLD 74.72 vs 74.72; momentum+VR (FedNova only) 79.19. The text summarizes this as a "6-9% improvement" from reweighting and about 10% for FedNova-Prox over FedProx. Hybrid local+server momentum reaches 81.15% against 77.49% for server momentum alone. On Synthetic(1,1) logistic regression (30 clients, power-law sizes) FedNova converges faster and ends best in all three settings.

**Limitações.** The framework covers only local solvers whose update is a linear combination of local gradients (SGD, proximal, momentum with reset buffers, decayed learning rate). Adam and other adaptive client optimizers with per-coordinate scaling are not covered exactly. The fix addresses the bias that comes from unequal local work (||a_i||_1). It does nothing about directional disagreement between clients: the kappa^2 / beta^2 dissimilarity stays in the vanishing terms, so non-IID drift remains and needs VR methods such as SCAFFOLD. The analysis is for a single global model with full sharing (no personalization or partial sharing), smooth non-convex losses, and bounded dissimilarity. Experiments are small: one CNN on CIFAR-10, plus synthetic data, single-task, no medical or cross-domain data. The "true" objective is assumed to be sum_i p_i F_i with p_i = n_i/n; other target weightings are possible in principle, since w_i = p_i can be set to any intended mixture, but the paper does not study them. Choosing tau_eff is left as a heuristic (global-learning-rate analogue). The paper does not measure update cancellation or cosine conflict.

**Relevância para o projeto.** 1) Terminology: FedNova is not a source for a definition of "cancellation / cancelamento de pesos". It defines a different phenomenon, "objective inconsistency": a bias in the effective aggregation weights caused by unequal local work, which makes FedAvg optimize sum_i w_i F_i instead of the intended sum_i p_i F_i. Cite it as the canonical reference for that bias and for separating the aggregate's direction (w_i, d_i) from its length (tau_eff, "slowdown" tau_bar/tau_eff). Do not cite it for cancellation.

2) Step dilution: Eq. (4) is the cleanest formal language available for the project's step-dilution finding. The aggregated step length is tau_eff * ||sum_i w_i eta d_i||, and the paper says outright that the server can scale it like a global learning rate. The project's cancellation ratio 1 - ||sum w_i Delta_i|| / sum w_i ||Delta_i|| measures only the directional loss inside the sum. A ~10x smaller aggregated step with low cancellation therefore points at the magnitude/tau_eff side (plus weighting) rather than at conflict. That supports the project's reading and suggests a server learning rate or a tau_eff rescale as a direct ablation.

3) Budget modes: in `steps` mode (equal steps_per_round) with a plain-SGD-like solver, tau_i is equal across clients, so FedNova's step-count inconsistency is zero (w_i = p_i). In `epochs` mode tau_i is proportional to n_i, so FedAvg's implicit weight becomes about p_i * tau_i, which over-weights large clients (ISIC/SIIM vs BUSI) beyond the configured hierarchical dataset_weights. FedNova-style normalization (Delta_i / tau_i, then the configured weights, then a common tau_eff) would restore the intended 1:1 dataset mixture. This is another reason why comparing arms across budget modes is invalid. Caveat: if the clients use Adam, the a_i framework applies only approximately.

4) Measurement: the paper offers no cancellation metric. Its chi^2_{p||w} weight-bias quantity could be logged next to the cancellation ratio, using w_i proportional to p_i * ||Delta_i|| / ||d_i||, i.e. the effective local work.

5) Scope gaps: the paper does not cover FedPer partial sharing, multi-task or cross-domain settings, and it says nothing about why federated underperforms local-only.

## FedNNNN: Norm-Normalized Neural Network Aggregation for Fast and Accurate Federated Learning (2020)

- **Autores:** Kenta Nagura, Song Bian, Takashi Sato (Graduate School of Informatics, Kyoto University)
- **Venue:** arXiv preprint 2008.04538v1 [cs.LG], 11 Aug 2020. The paper names no peer-reviewed venue.
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.04538
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "weight updating direction divergence (WUDD) problem"; "norm-based weight divergence analysis"; averaging "significantly diminishes the norm of the update vectors"; local models "tend to cancel each other out"

**Definição (literal).** Abstract: "we find out that averaging models from different clients significantly diminishes the norm of the update vectors, resulting in slow learning rate and low prediction accuracy." Section 3: "the local models tend to cancel each other out, resulting in a extremely small updating vector on the server" ... "We refer to this phenomenon as the weight updating direction divergence (WUDD) problem." Section 3.2: "As long as N is much less than the required amount of weight updates to fully perceive a dataset," [the aggregated model cannot reach reasonable accuracy] ... "learning cannot be executed effectively and efficiently in a simple FedAvg setting."

**Local.** Abstract; Section 3 (Norm-based Weight Divergence Analysis), Eqs. 4-6; Section 3.2. The quotes come from the arXiv HTML version (v1). Reading was done through a web fetch, so the wording is near-verbatim; compare against the PDF before citing.

**Formulação.** N_{t+1} := || sum_{k=1}^m (n_k/n) Δw^k_{t+1} ||   (Eq. 4, norm of the aggregated server update)
E_{t+1} := sum_{k=1}^m (n_k/n) ||Δw^k_{t+1}||   (Eq. 5, weighted mean of the client update norms)
N_{t+1} <= E_{t+1}   (Eq. 6, triangle inequality). WUDD is the regime where N << E.
FedNNNN update: w_{t+1} <- w_t + β (E_{t+1}/N_{t+1}) sum_k (n_k/n) Δw^k_{t+1}   (Eq. 8), which gives ||w_{t+1} - w_t|| = β E_{t+1}   (Eq. 9).
With momentum: d_{t+1} <- γ d_t + β (E/N) sum_k (n_k/n) Δw^k ; w_{t+1} <- w_t + d_{t+1}   (Eqs. 12-13).
Mapping to the project: the project's cancellation ratio equals exactly 1 - N/E.

**Métrica.** The paper compares N (norm of the weighted average of client deltas) with E (weighted average of the client delta norms) per round, and plots the norms during training (per layer for the CIFAR10 CNN). The ratio N/E is the implicit measure of shrinkage, and the project's cancellation ratio is 1 - N/E. Task performance is measured as test accuracy and convergence speed over rounds.

**Método.** FedNNNN is a server-side change to FedAvg. The server rescales the averaged client update by β·E/N, so the global step has norm β·E: β times the mean client-update norm, which undoes the shrinkage that averaging causes. It keeps the direction of the averaged update. It also adds server momentum γ·d (Eqs. 12-13) and keeps a separate evaluation model alongside the normalized model. The clients need no extra communication and share no data. β and γ are tuned per setting, with values of about 0.6-0.8.

**Resultados.** Setup: MNIST and CIFAR10 with small CNNs. There are K=100 clients, all of them participating each round (C=1). Training uses 5 local epochs and batch size 50, for 100 rounds on MNIST and 250 on CIFAR10. In the non-IID setting each client holds images of only 2 classes; the unbalanced setting uses power-law client sizes; and the n_k/n weights are made uniform. Test accuracy (FedAvg / FedProx / FedNNNN), in %:
- CIFAR10 IID-B: 81.3 / 81.4 / 84.7
- CIFAR10 non-IID-B: 72.6 / 74.2 / 76.7
- CIFAR10 non-IID-UB: 56.6 / 55.6 / 61.9
- MNIST non-IID-UB: 93.5 / 87.9 / 98.9
The abstract claims "up to 5.4% accuracy improvement" and faster convergence. In the ablation, norm-normalization alone is the main gain in balanced non-IID (76.7 on CIFAR10), while momentum alone helps most on CIFAR10 non-IID-UB (62.0).

**Limitações.** - Each experiment was run once, with a single seed and no variance reported.
- β and γ appear to have been chosen per setting by test accuracy, which risks selection bias.
- Only toy benchmarks were used (MNIST, CIFAR10, small CNNs, label-skew non-IID). There is no feature or domain shift, no multi-task setting and no personalization.
- The norm is rescaled globally rather than per layer, and the paper reports that the CIFAR10 conv-layer norms oscillate under FedNNNN.
- The method keeps the direction and inflates the magnitude, so if the client updates truly conflict, it amplifies a noisy averaged direction. The paper does not separate harmful conflict from benign cancellation of orthogonal noise.
- It gives no theoretical convergence analysis beyond N ≤ E.
- The conclusion states no limitations.
- It is not peer reviewed (arXiv only) and has few citations.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is the closest prior formalization of the project's metric. N/E is exactly the complement of the project's cancellation ratio 1 - ||Σ w_i Δ_i|| / Σ w_i ||Δ_i||, so the project can cite Eqs. 4-6 as the literature definition. The paper calls it the "weight updating direction divergence (WUDD) problem" and describes local models that "cancel each other out".

The paper's view that the cost of cancellation is a shrunken server step, which slows learning, matches the project's "step dilution" finding: the aggregated step was about 10x smaller. In the paper, though, the shrinkage comes from divergent directions under label skew.

The project's own data adds three points the paper lacks:
1. Cancellation between datasets was low.
2. A large part of the shrinkage may simply be the near-orthogonality of high-dimensional updates. That is why the project reads N/E against an orthogonal reference of about 1/sqrt(m)-type, which FedNNNN does not do.
3. The project's aggregation is FedPer and hierarchical, with only the trunk shared.

FedNNNN suggests a cheap, directly testable ablation: rescale the aggregated trunk delta by β·E/N, optionally with server momentum (related to FedAvgM and FedOpt's server learning rate). This would test whether federated underperforms local-only because of step size rather than gradient conflict. If rescaling closes the gap, the cost is dilution; if it does not, or it destabilizes training, conflict or domain heterogeneity is the cause.

When citing, note that the evidence is weak (single runs, toy label-skew data). Pair it with the server-learning-rate and adaptive-optimizer literature (FedAvgM: Hsu et al. 2019; FedOpt: Reddi et al. 2021) and with the gradient-conflict literature (PCGrad, FedGA) for the negative-transfer framing.

## Gradient Surgery for Multi-Task Learning (PCGrad) (2020)

- **Autores:** Tianhe Yu, Saurabh Kumar, Abhishek Gupta, Sergey Levine, Karol Hausman, Chelsea Finn
- **Venue:** NeurIPS 2020 (arXiv 2001.06782). Venue is from prior knowledge; the arXiv HTML text I read does not state it.
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.06782
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "conflicting gradients", "gradient interference" / "destructive gradient interference", "dominating gradients", "gradient magnitude similarity", "multi-task curvature", "the tragic triad", "gradient surgery". The paper never uses the words "cancellation" or "negative transfer" for its own phenomenon.

**Definição (literal).** Sec. 2.2 (The Tragic Triad), verbatim: "We hypothesize that a key optimization issue in multi-task learning arises from conflicting gradients, where gradients for different tasks point away from one another as measured by a negative inner product. However, conflicting gradients are not detrimental on their own. Indeed, simply averaging task gradients should provide the correct solution to descend the multi-task objective. However, there are conditions under which such conflicting gradients lead to significantly degraded performance. Consider a two-task optimization problem. If the gradient of one task is much larger in magnitude than the other, it will dominate the average gradient. If there is also high positive curvature along the directions of the task gradients, then the improvement in performance from the dominating task may be significantly overestimated, while the degradation in performance from the dominated task may be significantly underestimated." ... "Hence, we can characterize the co-occurrence of three conditions as follows: (a) when gradients from multiple tasks are in conflict with one another (b) when the difference in gradient magnitudes is large, leading to some task gradients dominating others, and (c) when there is high curvature in the multi-task optimization landscape." Definition 1 (Sec. 2.2): "We define φ_ij as the angle between two task gradients g_i and g_j. We define the gradients as conflicting when cos φ_ij < 0." Introduction (Sec. 1): "We define two gradients to be conflicting if they point away from one another, i.e., have a negative cosine similarity. We hypothesize that such conflict is detrimental when a) conflicting gradients coincide with b) high positive curvature and c) a large difference in gradient magnitudes."

**Local.** Sec. 1 (Introduction); Sec. 2.2, Definitions 1-3; Sec. 2.3, Algorithm 1; Sec. 2.4, Definitions 4-5 and Theorems 1-2. The definitions carry no equation numbers.

**Formulação.** Def 1 (conflict): cos φ_ij = <g_i, g_j> / (||g_i|| ||g_j||) < 0.
Def 2 (gradient magnitude similarity): Φ(g_i, g_j) = 2||g_i||_2 ||g_j||_2 / (||g_i||_2^2 + ||g_j||_2^2). It equals 1 when the norms are equal and goes to 0 as they diverge.
Def 3 (multi-task curvature): H(L; θ, θ') = ∫_0^1 ∇L(θ)^T ∇²L(θ + a(θ' - θ)) ∇L(θ) da. High curvature means H > C for some large positive constant C.
Def 4: L = L1 + L2, g = g1 + g2.
Def 5 (curvature bounding measure): ξ(g1, g2) = (1 - cos²φ12) ||g1 - g2||² / ||g1 + g2||².
PCGrad (Alg. 1): for each task i, visit the other tasks j in random order; if g_i^PC · g_j < 0, set g_i^PC ← g_i^PC - (g_i^PC · g_j / ||g_j||²) g_j. The update is Δθ = Σ_i g_i^PC.
Thm 1 (convex, L-Lipschitz gradient, step t ≤ 1/L): PCGrad converges either to a point where cos φ12 = -1 or to the optimum L(θ*).
Thm 2 (non-convex, L-Lipschitz, H(L; θ, θ^MT) ≥ ℓ||g||² with ℓ ≤ L): L(θ^PCGrad) ≤ L(θ^MT) if (a) cos φ12 ≤ -Φ(g1, g2), (b) ℓ ≥ ξ(g1, g2) L, and (c) t ≥ 2 / (ℓ - ξ(g1, g2) L).

**Métrica.** Per-step pairwise cosine between task gradients; conflict means a negative cosine. Magnitude imbalance is measured with Φ, and curvature with an estimate of H. In Sec. 5.3 / Fig. 4 (two Meta-World tasks, reach and press-button-top), they plot over training: (1) the estimated multi-task curvature, (2) the percentage of iterations where the two task gradients have positive cosine, and (3) among iterations with negative cosine, the percentage where condition (a), cos φ ≤ -Φ, and the implication of condition (b), ξ ≤ 1, hold. There is no aggregate norm-ratio or cancellation metric such as ||Σg|| / Σ||g||. Every quantity is pairwise and defined on gradients within a single step, not on multi-step parameter deltas.

**Método.** PCGrad is "gradient surgery". When two task gradients conflict (negative cosine), each is projected onto the normal plane of the other, which removes the conflicting component. Gradients that do not conflict are left unchanged, so positive transfer is kept. Task order is shuffled randomly for symmetry. The method is model-agnostic, adds no hyperparameters, and plugs into any optimizer (SGD, Adam). It changes both the direction and the effective magnitude of the summed update, and can be applied to the shared parameters only (as with routing networks).

**Resultados.** NYUv2 (SegNet/MTAN, Table 1): MTAN+PCGrad with uncertainty weights reaches 20.17 mIoU / 56.65 pixel accuracy, versus 17.72 / 55.32 for the best MTAN; depth relative error is 0.2467 versus 0.2577; mean normal angle error is 30.01 versus 31.25-31.60. CIFAR-100 with 20 coarse-label tasks: PCGrad on a single network reaches 71% accuracy, beating cross-stitch and independent training, and a further large gain comes from combining it with routing networks. CelebA (40 binary tasks): lower average error than Sener and Koltun's MGDA-UB. MultiMNIST and CityScapes results are in Appendix E. Meta-World with SAC: PCGrad solves all 10 MT10 tasks and about 70% of MT50 with the best data efficiency; single and multi-head SAC fail to learn half the skills, and independent SAC reaches similar final success only after far more samples. Ablations: modifying only magnitude or only direction is worse than full PCGrad, which also beats GradNorm and CosReg. CosReg pushes every cosine to 0; the authors take this as evidence that keeping positive-cosine gradients matters. A random task order beats a fixed one on MT50. Sec. 5.3: condition (a) holds most of the time before Task 2 is learned and declines once PCGrad starts to solve it, which the authors read as evidence that the triad is the decisive factor.

**Limitações.** The theory covers two tasks only (Theorem 2 is a one-step sufficient condition), and Theorem 1 needs convexity. If cos φ12 is near -1, PCGrad zeroes the update, so convergence can stall or slow down. The authors argue SGD noise prevents this but give no guarantee. Cost grows with the number of tasks, since every task needs its own gradient: SAC+PCGrad takes about 2x the wall-clock time and memory (MT10: 1 day vs 12 h, 1 GB vs 0.5 GB). On supervised MTL the authors themselves conclude PCGrad acts as a regularizer "rather than an improvement on optimization speed or convergence" (Appendix B), so the triad-as-optimization-cause story is supported mainly by RL and toy landscapes. The empirical triad analysis uses a single pair of RL tasks. "High curvature" depends on an unspecified constant C. Everything is centralized: one model, gradients within a single step on a shared batch. There is no federated setting, no multi-step local deltas, no client or domain heterogeneity, and no aggregation weights. The paper does not claim to have measured negative transfer as such, only optimization interference.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is the canonical source for defining update "conflict" by sign of cosine (Def. 1). The project's pairwise cosine between client trunk deltas is the federated, multi-step analogue of Def. 1, so cite PCGrad for that diagnostic, and note that it is defined for per-step task gradients, not for client deltas accumulated over many local steps. The project's cancellation ratio 1 - ||Σ wᵢΔᵢ|| / Σ wᵢ||Δᵢ|| has no counterpart in PCGrad. It mixes the paper's direction term (Def. 1) and magnitude term (Def. 2, Φ) into one aggregate norm-loss quantity, and it ignores curvature (Def. 3). It is therefore the project's own metric, related to but not defined by PCGrad, and should be presented that way. The paper's key conceptual point fits the project's finding: "conflicting gradients are not detrimental on their own" and harm comes only together with magnitude dominance and high curvature. Low inter-dataset cancellation is thus consistent with the absence of triad-type interference. The project's dominant cost is a roughly 10x smaller aggregated step (step dilution) with near-orthogonal updates, and that is a different mechanism. Orthogonal updates (cos ≈ 0) shrink the norm of the averaged step without conflicting, and PCGrad does nothing about it, because it only acts when cos < 0. The CosReg ablation (forcing cos = 0 hurts) also suggests orthogonality is not itself the remedy. Implication: PCGrad-style projection (or FedPCGrad-type federated variants) is unlikely to close the gap to local-only. The project should instead frame its result as magnitude/step-size loss under near-orthogonality and test server learning rate or FedOpt rescaling, while reporting cos < 0 fractions and Φ per round so the triad can be checked formally. Within a multi_task client, PCGrad could be applied between the seg and cls losses on the shared trunk, which matches its original intra-model setting.

## Federated Learning with Fair Averaging (FedFV) (2021)

- **Autores:** Zheng Wang, Xiaoliang Fan, Jianzhong Qi, Chenglu Wen, Cheng Wang, Rongshan Yu (Xiamen University; University of Melbourne)
- **Venue:** IJCAI 2021 (arXiv:2104.14937, v5, 16 Jun 2021)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.14937
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "conflicting gradients" / "gradient conflicts" (following Yu et al. 2020, PCGrad), split into "internal conflicts" (among clients selected in a round) and "external conflicts" (between the combined update and the most recent gradients of clients that were not selected or dropped out). The paper ties conflict to "large differences in the magnitudes" (domination of the average). It never uses the words "cancellation" or "negative transfer".

**Definição (literal).** Sec. 3, Definition 1: "Client i's gradient g_i ∈ R^d conflicts with client j's gradient g_j ∈ R^d iff g_i · g_j < 0." Sec. 3.2, Definition 2: "In the t-th communication round, there are internal conflicts if there is at least a pair of client gradients <g^t_i, g^t_j> such that g^t_i conflicts with g^t_j, where g^t_i, g^t_j ∈ G^t = {g^t_1, g^t_2, ..., g^t_m} and G^t is the selected clients' updates." Sec. 3.3, Definition 3: "In the t-th communication round, there are external conflicts if there is a client h ∉ S^t whose latest gradient g^{t−k}_h conflicts with the combined update g^t, where 0 < k < τ, 0 < τ < t." Abstract: "we identify a cause of unfairness in FL — conflicting gradients with large differences in the magnitudes." Introduction: "The gradient conflicts seem not detrimental on their own since it has been shown that a simple average of gradients is valid to optimize the global objective function [Li et al., 2020c]. However, when the conflicting gradients have large differences in their magnitudes, model accuracy can suffer significant reductions for some of the clients, as shown in Figure 1." (The PDF text layer drops the "fi"/"fl" ligatures. These were put back, and nothing else was changed.)

**Local.** Abstract; Sec. 1 (Introduction, Fig. 1 caption); Sec. 3 Definition 1; Sec. 3.2 Definition 2; Sec. 3.3 Definition 3; Algorithms 1-3 (pp. 2-4 of the IJCAI version)

**Formulação.** Pseudo-gradient update of client k in round t: g^t_k = θ^t − θ^t_k (Alg. 1, line 4). Conflict: g_i · g_j < 0, which is the same as cos(g_i, g_j) < 0. Internal-conflict removal (Alg. 2): sort the clients by ascending training loss to get the projection order PO^t. For every client k except the top α·m highest-loss clients, which keep their original gradient: for each g_ij in PO^t, if g^PC_k · g_ij < 0, set g^PC_k ← g^PC_k − (g^PC_k · g_ij / ||g_ij||^2) g_ij (a PCGrad-style projection onto the normal plane). Then g^t = (1/m) Σ_k g^PC_k. External-conflict removal (Alg. 3): for each of the past τ rounds, from oldest to most recent, sum the stored latest gradients g_con of clients whose gradient conflicts with g^t, and if g^t · g_con < 0, project g^t ← g^t − (g^t · g_con / ||g_con||^2) g_con. Magnitude restoration (Alg. 1, line 10): g^t ← g^t / ||g^t|| · ||(1/m) Σ_k g^t_k||, i.e. the step length is reset to the norm of the plain average. Theorem 2 bound: |g_k · g'| ≤ ((m−1)/m)(max_i ||g_i||)^2 f(m, k, ε1, ε2). Convergence (Thms 3-4, convex and L-smooth with η ≤ 1/L): to a Pareto-stationary point or the optimum.

**Métrica.** Conflict is detected as a binary sign test per pair (dot product, equivalently cosine, < 0) on whole-model flattened client updates. The paper does not report a continuous conflict statistic, a cancellation ratio, or the share of conflicting pairs. Outcomes are measured as fairness and accuracy across clients: mean, variance, worst 5% and best 5% of per-client test accuracy, plus convergence speed in rounds (Figure 2).

**Método.** FedFV is a server-side aggregation rule that replaces FedAvg's plain average. Before averaging, it iteratively projects each client's update onto the normal plane of every other client update it conflicts with (negative dot product). The projection order is set by training loss, so high-loss clients act as the last projection targets and end up least conflicted with the final direction; a fraction α of high-loss clients keeps its raw update. It then projects the aggregate away from the stored recent updates of unselected clients over a τ-round history (external conflicts). Finally it rescales the aggregate to the norm of the plain average, because projection changes the length.

**Resultados.** CIFAR-10 (100 clients, pathological 2-shard label skew, 10% sampled per round, 2-layer MLP, 2000 rounds, full batch, E=1, 5 seeds): FedAvg reaches 46.85 mean / 12.57 variance / 19.84 worst-5%. FedFV (α=0.1, τ=10) reaches 50.42 / 9.70 / 32.24, against best baselines q-FedAvg (q=5): 45.25 / 9.59 / 26.56 and FedMGDA+: 25.12 worst-5%. Fashion-MNIST (3 clients, all selected, so no external conflicts): FedFV α=2/3 reaches 80.28 mean with variance 1.77, against FedAvg 80.42 / 11.50. AFL has a lower variance (1.12) but a lower mean (78.14). The authors report up to 7.2% (CIFAR-10) and 78% (MNIST) better accuracy than the state of the art, with faster convergence. Ablation on projection order (variance, CIFAR-10 / FMNIST): loss-based 13.19 / 13.76, random 14.12 / 20.14, reverse 16.28 / 22.05.

**Limitações.** - Theory holds only for convex, L-smooth objectives, and the authors say the analysis of external conflicts is incomplete (future work).
- Experiments are small: 2-layer MLPs and a 2-conv CNN on CIFAR-10, MNIST and Fashion-MNIST; one local epoch, often full batch, so updates are close to true gradients rather than multi-step client drift.
- Only synthetic label-skew heterogeneity is tested. There is no feature or domain shift, no multi-task setting and no personalization.
- Conflict is a binary sign test on the whole flattened model, with no per-layer or per-block analysis and no measure of how much of the aggregate norm is lost.
- Pairwise projection costs O(m²d) and needs a stored history of every client's last update.
- The final rescaling to the norm of the plain average deliberately keeps FedAvg's step length. FedFV changes direction only and cannot recover step length lost to averaging.
- Results report the best hyperparameters per method. The paper defines "fairness" as low variance of accuracy across clients, not as overall utility.

**Relevância para o projeto.** FedFV is the canonical FL source for the project's pairwise client-update cosine diagnostic. Its Definition 1 (g_i·g_j < 0, i.e. negative cosine, after PCGrad) is the standard way to define "conflict" between client updates, and it treats conflict and magnitude imbalance as separate factors whose combination does the harm. That matches the project's two measurements: pairwise cosine for direction, and the cancellation ratio 1 − ||Σw_iΔ_i|| / Σw_i||Δ_i||, which combines direction and weighted magnitude.

Three caveats for the thesis:
1. FedFV never defines or measures "cancellation" or "negative transfer". The project's ratio is the student's own norm-loss metric and should not be attributed to FedFV. FedFV supports only the conflict-sign definition and the magnitude-domination argument.
2. The introduction concedes that conflict "seems not detrimental on [its] own" because plain averaging still optimizes the global objective. This fits the project's finding that inter-dataset cancellation barely exceeds the orthogonal reference: near-orthogonal updates are not conflicts by FedFV's definition, since the dot product is not negative.
3. FedFV's last step rescales the aggregate to ||(1/m)Σg_k||, the plain-average norm, so it fixes direction but not step length. It therefore does nothing for the step-dilution cost the project identified (an aggregated step about 10x smaller than a client's own step). The paper also states that unequal dataset sizes with a fixed batch size give clients with more data more local steps and therefore larger update magnitudes, which is the mechanism behind the project's steps-vs-epochs budget issue.

FedFV is also a baseline candidate (PCGrad applied at aggregation) for the FedPer shared trunk. It works on whole-model, single-task, label-skew settings with no personalized heads, so applying it to a cross-domain multi-task shared trunk would be an extension rather than a replication.

## Gradient Vaccine: Investigating and Improving Multi-task Optimization in Massively Multilingual Models (2021)

- **Autores:** Zirui Wang, Yulia Tsvetkov, Orhan Firat, Yuan Cao
- **Venue:** ICLR 2021
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.05874
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "gradient similarity" (cosine of task gradients) as a measure of "loss geometry"; "negative interference" (Introduction); "conflicting" gradients (inherited from PCGrad); "similarity goal / target" phi^T. The paper never uses the words "cancellation" or "weight cancellation".

**Definição (literal).** Sec. 3.2: "two tasks have a positive gradient similarity of cos(θ)=ϕij". Appendix E.1 defines ϕij = g_i·g_j / (‖g_i‖‖g_j‖). Sec. 3.1, on PCGrad: it "implicitly makes the assumption that any two tasks must have the same gradient similarity objective of zero." Also: "many language pairs in our model share positive cosine similarities" and "PCGrad is not effective for positive gradient similarities". Sec. 2.2 findings: "Gradient similarities reflect language proximities." and "Gradient similarities correlate positively with model quality." Introduction: "While this approach ignores task relatedness and might induce negative interference".

**Local.** Sec. 2.2 (observations); Sec. 3.1 (PCGrad critique, Eq. 1); Sec. 3.2 (GradVac, Eq. 2 and Eq. 3); Appendix E.1 (cosine definition); Algorithm 1 line 13 (trigger condition)

**Formulação.** Similarity: \phi_{ij} = \frac{g_i \cdot g_j}{\|g_i\|\|g_j\|}. PCGrad (Eq. 1), applied only when \phi_{ij} < 0: g_i' = g_i - \frac{g_i\cdot g_j}{\|g_j\|^2} g_j. GradVac (Eq. 2), applied when \phi_{ij} < \hat\phi^T_{ij} (Algorithm 1): g_i' = g_i + \frac{\|g_i\|(\phi^T_{ij}\sqrt{1-\phi_{ij}^2} - \phi_{ij}\sqrt{1-(\phi^T_{ij})^2})}{\|g_j\|\sqrt{1-(\phi^T_{ij})^2}} g_j. The coefficient comes from the Law of Sines, and the update stays in span(g_i, g_j), changing both magnitude and direction. Adaptive target (Eq. 3), an EMA per task pair (i,j) and parameter group k: \hat\phi^{(t)}_{ijk} = (1-\beta)\hat\phi^{(t-1)}_{ijk} + \beta\phi^{(t)}_{ijk}. PCGrad is the special case \phi^T = 0.

**Métrica.** Pairwise cosine similarity between task (language-pair) gradients. It is computed on a held-out multi-way aligned set of 3k sentences every 1,000 training steps, along the training trajectory. In the paper's words: "We examine gradient similarities at various granularities, from specific layers to the entire model". It is reported per layer, for encoder and decoder, and for the whole model, and visualized as similarity matrices clustered by language family. It is related to BLEU (model quality).

**Método.** First an empirical study. A 375M Transformer-Big multilingual NMT model is trained on in-house data for 25 languages (50 pairs with English, 10 families), separately for Any->En and En->Any. Gradient cosine similarities are tracked across layers and steps. Then GradVac: for each task pair it keeps an EMA estimate of the "natural" gradient similarity, per parameter group (e.g. per layer). Whenever the current cosine falls below this target, even if it is still positive, it modifies g_i inside span(g_i, g_j) so that its cosine with g_j equals the target. This generalizes PCGrad, which acts only when cosine < 0 and implicitly targets orthogonality.

**Resultados.** (1) Gradient similarity clusters by language family; European languages form a large cluster. (2) Higher similarity correlates with higher BLEU. En->Any gradients are less similar than Any->En gradients. (3) Similarity varies across layers and training steps, and encoder and decoder differ. (4) PCGrad seldom triggers because many pairs have positive cosine. Average BLEU on WMT, read from the tables (not quoted text): En->Any rises from 22.48 (baseline) and 22.87 (PCGrad) to 23.89 (GradVac); Any->En rises from 25.61 and 25.93 (PCGrad) to 26.34. Massive-scale En->Any average: 18.25 (T=5) to 19.08. XTREME, mBERT fine-tuning: NER average 82.9 to 83.8, POS 88.6 to 88.9. The best setting found was {LRL_only, all_layer, beta=1e-2}.

**Limitações.** The conclusion states no explicit limitations. It only claims the approach is "generic and applicable beyond multilingual settings". Observed limitations: (a) it uses single-model, centralized multi-task gradients at each step, not multi-step client deltas averaged after local training, so no aggregation or FedAvg setting is studied. (b) Pairwise modification costs O(T^2) per step, with a random task order as in PCGrad. (c) It uses cosine only: it never measures how much the magnitude of the summed update shrinks, i.e. cancellation of the norm. (d) The target is the EMA of the observed similarity, which assumes historical alignment is "good" alignment. (e) The domain is NLP with in-house data; there are no vision or medical experiments. (f) The evidence is correlational (similarity vs. BLEU, similarity vs. language family), not causal. (g) The gains are modest (+0.4 to +1.4 BLEU) and come from a single run, with no reported seeds or variance.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is the main reference for treating pairwise cosine between updates as a continuous diagnostic instead of a binary "conflict" test. It supports reading the project's client-delta cosine matrix as follows: near-zero cosine (orthogonal, which the project's orthogonal_reference captures for the cancellation ratio) is a distinct regime from negative cosine (true conflict). GradVac also argues that positive but below-natural similarity can still hurt, which PCGrad-style sign tests miss. The project's finding is little inter-dataset cancellation with near-orthogonal deltas. In GradVac's terms, cross-domain clients (BUSI/ISIC/LGG/SIIM) behave like distant language families: low but not negative similarity. Gradient surgery that only fixes negative cosines (PCGrad, FedPCGrad-like) would therefore rarely activate and is unlikely to fix the gap. Two cautions. First, GradVac works on per-step task gradients inside one model, while the project measures multi-step client deltas after local training under FedPer aggregation. Second, GradVac says nothing about magnitude. When deltas are near-orthogonal, ‖Σ w_i Δ_i‖ is about ‖Δ‖/√N for equal weights, so the step-dilution effect the project observed (~10x smaller aggregated step) is the norm-side counterpart of near-zero cosine. A cosine-only metric like GradVac's cannot see it, which supports keeping the cancellation ratio and the step-norm ratio as separate quantities. Possible follow-ups: report per-layer and per-round cosine trajectories, as GradVac does; check whether intra-dataset similarity exceeds inter-dataset similarity, the analogue of language families; and consider a server-side GradVac-style alignment toward an EMA target per block, though it would not address dilution.

## Tackling the Non-IID Issue in Heterogeneous Federated Learning by Gradient Harmonization (FedGH) (2023)

- **Autores:** Xinyu Zhang, Weiyu Sun, Ying Chen (Nanjing University)
- **Venue:** arXiv preprint 2309.06692 (cs.LG), v2 dated 7 Mar 2024. The paper calls itself a "letter" (IEEE letter format), but the HTML page names no journal, so the venue is unconfirmed.
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06692 (read in full at https://arxiv.org/html/2309.06692v2)
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "gradient conflict" between clients at server aggregation; "conflicting client pairs"; "gradient conflict ratio"; "local drifts"; "weight divergence" (cited from [28]); remedy named "gradient harmonization" / "gradient deconfliction". The paper never says "cancellation" or "weight cancellation".

**Definição (literal).** Sec. II-B ("Gradient Conflict in Heterogeneous FL"): "Assuming that each client has uploaded the local gradient g_t at the t-th communication round, where gradients from distinct layers are flattened to a 1-d vector. We identify gradient conflict when the cosine similarity between g_t^i and g_t^j, ∀i≠j, becomes negative, i.e., (g_t^i · g_t^j)/(‖g_t^i‖·‖g_t^j‖) [< 0]". Sec. II-C: "If the value is negative, indicating an obtuse angle between two gradient vectors, a gradient conflict between clients i and j is identified." Sec. II-B conclusion: "These experiments demonstrate that stronger heterogeneity leads to more severe local drifts, consequently inducing more violent gradient conflicts." (The "< 0" was lost when the HTML was converted to text; the surrounding text confirms it.)

**Local.** Sec. II-B, the inline inequality just before Fig. 2; Sec. II-C, step (1); projection in Eq. (1) and Algorithm 1

**Formulação.** Pseudo-gradient of client k = its local model delta g_{t+1}^k = w_{t+1}^k − w_t (ClientUpdate returns w_{t+1}^k − w_t), with all layers flattened into one vector. Clients i and j conflict iff cos(g^i, g^j) = g^i·g^j / (‖g^i‖‖g^j‖) < 0. Harmonization (Eq. 1), using a frozen copy G̃ of the original gradients: g^i ← g^i − (g^i·g̃^j / ‖g̃^j‖²) g̃^j, and symmetrically for g^j. Algorithm 1: for each client k, and for each other client j in random order, project only if g^k·g̃^j < 0. Then aggregate: w_{t+1} = w_t + Σ_k (n_k/n) g^k. There is no theorem or convergence proof.

**Métrica.** (1) Pairwise cosine similarity between whole-model client deltas, sorted over client pairs (Fig. 2a/2c). Severity is reported as the minimum pairwise cosine: −0.06 at Dir α=0.1 and −0.12 at α=0.01 with 5 clients; −0.22 with 10 clients. (2) "Gradient conflict ratio": the fraction of client pairs with negative cosine, tracked over rounds (Fig. 2b), which rises as training goes on. Measured with FedAvg, a 2-layer CNN and CIFAR-10. There is no magnitude-based or aggregate-norm measure, so nothing like ‖Σ w_i Δ_i‖ / Σ w_i‖Δ_i‖.

**Método.** A server-side plug-in in the style of PCGrad, which the paper credits as its inspiration (PCGrad comes from multi-task learning). Before aggregation, the server computes the pairwise cosine between client updates. For every pair at an obtuse angle, it removes from each update its component along the other's original update, i.e. projects onto the normal plane. It then runs the usual weighted FedAvg sum. The module has no hyperparameters, works with FedAvg, FedProx, FedNova and FedDecorr, and leaves client training unchanged. The cost is O(K²) dot products over the full model.

**Resultados.** Benchmarks: CIFAR-10 (2-layer CNN), CIFAR-100 and Tiny-ImageNet (MobileNetV2), 20 clients with Dirichlet / class-count (C) skew; LEAF (Celeba, FEMNIST, Twitter, Synthetic, Shakespeare, Reddit). Top-1 gains: +1.53% to +5.15% on the image datasets (FEMNIST: +5.04% over FedAvg, +5.15% over FedProx), up to +3.18% on Twitter, +0.67% to +4.88% on Synthetic, and small gains on Shakespeare and Reddit (+0.59%, +0.48%). Gains grow with heterogeneity: +0.16% to +1.18% under mild skew vs +1.89% to +3.11% under the strongest. Rounds to reach target accuracy fall (2.3× faster on Celeba, 1.6× on FEMNIST). Client-count ablation: about no change at K=5, but +2.01% to +2.49% (CIFAR-10) and +0.72% to +4.05% (CIFAR-100) at larger K. The authors conclude it suits cross-device FL with many clients. Local-epoch ablation: +0.86% to +2.76% on CIFAR-10. Conflicts appear even under IID data, only less severe.

**Limitações.** One fixed random seed; last-round accuracy only; no variance or confidence intervals. Only label-skew (Dirichlet) partitions and LEAF, with no feature- or domain-shift or cross-modality heterogeneity. Classification only, with a single global model: no personalization, no partial sharing, no multi-task. The conflict test is binary (sign of cosine) on the flattened whole model; it ignores magnitude and per-layer structure and never quantifies how much aggregate norm is lost. Projections depend on order (random order) and cost O(K²). Little or no benefit, sometimes slight drops, at small K=5 and in some FedDecorr settings. Extra time overhead on datasets with little data per client. No theoretical convergence analysis. The paper has no dedicated limitations section; these are inferred from its text and tables.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This paper is the closest operational match to the project's pairwise trunk-delta cosine. It treats client updates (w_local − w_global) as pseudo-gradients and defines inter-client "gradient conflict" as negative cosine, i.e. an obtuse angle. It measures conflict as the minimum pairwise cosine and the conflict ratio (fraction of negative pairs). The project can report both directly from the cosine matrices already stored in aggregation_history.json, split into intra- and inter-dataset pairs, giving a literature-standard conflict statistic next to the cancellation ratio. Two caveats. (a) FedGH defines conflict by the sign of the cosine only. It has no concept equal to the project's magnitude-aware cancellation 1 − ‖Σw_iΔ_i‖/Σw_i‖Δ_i‖, and no orthogonal reference; the project's metric is closer to the 'gradient diversity / norm-loss' family. FedGH supports the finding that near-orthogonal updates (cos ≈ 0) do not count as conflict, and its own reported conflicts are mild (min cos −0.06 to −0.22). That fits the project's observation of little inter-dataset cancellation. (b) FedGH cannot address step dilution. Projection only removes components, so it can only shrink or keep the aggregated norm, never restore it. If the dominant cost is a roughly 10x smaller aggregated step from averaging near-orthogonal updates, FedGH would leave that unchanged or make it slightly worse. Remedies that rescale the server step (server learning rate or FedOpt-style optimizers, normalized averaging as in FedNova) fit that problem better. Gains in FedGH also grow with many clients, whereas the project has few clients from a few domains, the regime where FedGH showed about no benefit (K=5). Still a useful cheap ablation arm on the shared trunk (apply Eq. 1 to trunk deltas only, FedPer-compatible) and a citable definition of "gradient conflict" in FL. The term comes from PCGrad, which links the FL and MTL negative-transfer literatures.

## FedExP: Speeding Up Federated Averaging via Extrapolation (2023)

- **Autores:** Divyansh Jhunjhunwala, Shiqiang Wang, Gauri Joshi
- **Venue:** ICLR 2023 (arXiv:2301.09604v2, 6 Mar 2023)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09604
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** True

**Termo usado.** The paper never says "cancellation" or "weight cancellation". It uses "pseudo-gradients" (the client updates Delta_i), "aggregated client update" (Delta-bar), "server step size" (eta_g), "extrapolation" (eta_g > 1, taken from Pierra's EPPM), "gradient diversity" (the heterogeneity measure that its lower bound reduces to) and "client drift". The phenomenon is the averaged update being much shorter than the individual client updates, which is the project's step dilution. The paper describes it through the ratio sum_i ||Delta_i||^2 / (2M ||Delta-bar||^2).

**Definição (literal).** Sec. 2, "Server Optimization in FedAvg", Eq. (4): "recent work (Reddi et al., 2021; Hsu et al., 2019) has focused on optimizing the server aggregation process by treating the client updates Delta_i^(t) as "pseudo-gradients" and multiplying by a server step size when aggregating them ... where Delta-bar^(t) = (1/M) sum_i Delta_i^(t) is the aggregated client update in round t and eta_g acts as server step size. Note that setting eta_g = 1 recovers the vanilla FedAvg update."

Sec. 3.2, after Eq. (8): "Note that depending on the values of {Delta_i^(t)}, we may have (eta_g^(t))_opt >> 1."

Sec. 3.3, "Connection with Gradient Diversity": "In the case where tau = 1 and clients run full-batch gradient descent, our lower bound 8 reduces to sum_i ||grad F_i(w^(t))||^2 / 2M ||grad F(w^(t))||^2 which is used as a measure of data-heterogeneity in many FL works ... Our lower bound suggests using larger step-sizes as this gradient diversity increases".

Sec. 3.3, "Importance of Adding Small Constant to Denominator": "This is especially true towards the end of training where we can have ||Delta-bar^(t)||^2 ≈ 0 but ||Delta_i^(t)||^2 ≠ 0."

Math was copied from the arXiv HTML rendering; prose quotes are verbatim.

**Local.** Sec. 2 Eq. (2)-(4); Sec. 3.1 Eq. (5) and Pierra's lambda^(t); Sec. 3.2 Eq. (6)-(8); Sec. 3.3 Eq. (9) and the "Connection with Gradient Diversity" paragraph; Algorithm 1, line 11

**Formulação.** Client update (pseudo-gradient): Delta_i^(t) = w^(t) - w_i^(t,tau) (Eq. 3). Aggregate: Delta-bar^(t) = (1/M) sum_i Delta_i^(t). Generalized FedAvg update: w^(t+1) = w^(t) - eta_g Delta-bar^(t) (Eq. 4).

POCS / EPPM extrapolation (Pierra 1984): lambda^(t) = sum_i ||P_i(w) - w||^2 / (M ||(1/M) sum_i P_i(w) - w||^2), which is >= 1 by Jensen.

Approximate projection condition (Eq. 6): (1/M) sum_i ||w_i^(t,tau) - w*||^2 <= ||w^(t) - w*||^2.

Optimal step size (Eq. 8): (eta_g)_opt = <w^(t) - w*, Delta-bar> / ||Delta-bar||^2 >= sum_i ||Delta_i||^2 / (2M ||Delta-bar||^2).

FedExP step size (Eq. 9): eta_g^(t) = max{1, sum_i ||Delta_i^(t)||^2 / (2M (||Delta-bar^(t)||^2 + epsilon))}.

Link to the project's metric (my derivation, not in the paper). With uniform weights, let c = 1 - ||Delta-bar|| / mean_i ||Delta_i||. Then ||Delta-bar|| = (1-c) mean_i ||Delta_i||, and the FedExP ratio is at least 1/(2(1-c)^2). For M equal-norm, mutually orthogonal updates, ||Delta-bar||^2 = ||Delta||^2 / M, so eta_g = M/2. A ~10x shorter aggregate (c ≈ 0.9) would give eta_g ≈ 50 before epsilon damps it.

**Métrica.** There is no cancellation metric as such. Per round, the server computes sum_i ||Delta_i||^2 / (2M ||Delta-bar||^2): the mean squared client-update norm over the squared norm of the averaged update, halved. The adaptive eta_g^(t) is reported (plots in App. D.5). Performance is measured as rounds to a target test accuracy (Table 1) and as training loss and test accuracy curves (Fig. 3). In the synthetic case it also tracks the distance to the optimum ||w^(t) - w*||^2.

**Método.** FedExP casts FedAvg as an approximate parallel Projection Onto Convex Sets (POCS) method. In the overparameterized convex regime every client's minimizer set contains the global minimum, and local SGD acts as an approximate projection onto that set. Following Pierra's extrapolated parallel projection method (EPPM), the server picks a per-round step size of at least 1 from the ratio of the mean squared client-update norm to the squared norm of the averaged update (Eq. 9). It adds a constant epsilon to avoid blow-up when the average approaches zero; a large epsilon recovers FedAvg. The final model is the average of the last two iterates, because the extrapolated iterates oscillate. The method adds no client computation or communication, works with partial participation and secure aggregation, and can be combined with SCAFFOLD or server momentum.

**Resultados.** - **Rounds to target accuracy (Table 1):**
  - EMNIST, 84%: 186 rounds vs FedAvg 328 (1.76x), SCAFFOLD 232, FedAdagrad 277.
  - CIFAR-10, 72%: 267 vs 434 (1.62x).
  - CIFAR-100, 40%: 242 vs 500 (2.06x).
  - CINIC-10, 58%: 318 vs 450 (1.42x).
- **Setup:** ResNet-18 on Dirichlet(0.3) splits with 100-200 clients, 20 participating per round, 20 local steps.
- **Speedups:** "consistent speedup of over 1.4-2x over FedAvg" on realistic tasks, and an order-wise gain on overparameterized linear regression, where Eq. 6 holds.
- **SCAFFOLD:** fails to beat FedAvg on CIFAR and CINIC, but extrapolated SCAFFOLD is the best method on CIFAR-10 (Fig. 4).
- **Step size:** FedExP takes "much larger steps in some (but not all) rounds".
- **Theory:** convergence holds for non-convex and underparameterized objectives. Extrapolation reaches the region near the optimum faster but can settle at a higher error floor from noise.

**Limitações.** - **Assumptions behind the motivation:** it assumes overparameterized convex objectives with a common minimizer (Eq. 6). The authors expect Eq. 6 to hold only "during the initial phase of training" under SGD noise or underparameterization.
- **Scope of the theory:** the convergence analysis assumes full-batch gradients and full participation ("purely a theoretical limitation").
- **Error floor:** larger steps can raise the error floor near the optimum, and the client step size must be used to control it.
- **Monotonicity:** the method decreases the distance to w* monotonically but not the loss F, so the last iterate oscillates and two iterates must be averaged.
- **Tuning and blow-up:** epsilon needs tuning, and without it the step size blows up late in training when ||Delta-bar|| ≈ 0.
- **Uniform weights:** Eq. 9 is written only for uniform 1/M aggregation, with no weighted or hierarchical variant.
- **Experimental scope:**
  - Experiments cover only image classification with one global model. There is no personalization (FedPer), no multi-task setting and no cross-domain clients.
  - Experiments use a single dataset per run with label skew.
- **Direction:** it rescales the averaged direction but cannot fix it when client updates conflict, i.e. when the average points away from some clients' optima.

**Relevância para o projeto.** **Fit to the finding:** this is the most direct remedy in the literature for the project's step-dilution finding.

- **Same quantity as the cancellation ratio.** The FedExP ratio sum ||Delta_i||^2 / (2M ||Delta-bar||^2) is a squared-norm version of the project's ratio 1 - ||sum w_i Delta_i|| / sum w_i ||Delta_i||. It is computed from the same per-round trunk deltas already logged in aggregation_history.json, so eta_g^(t) can be computed offline from the existing logs. That shows how large a step FedExP would have taken each round.
- **Why orthogonal updates matter.** For M near-orthogonal updates, FedExP prescribes eta_g ≈ M/2. This matches the project's observation that cancellation sits near the orthogonal reference ("little conflict") while the aggregated step is still ~10x shorter. In FedExP's framing, near-orthogonality is exactly the case that calls for extrapolation, not a benign one. The project should therefore keep "conflict" (opposed directions, i.e. excess cancellation above the orthogonal reference) separate from "dilution" (norm loss from averaging non-aligned updates). FedExP addresses only dilution.
- **Terminology for the write-up.** The paper does not use the term "weight cancellation". It frames the phenomenon as server step-size / pseudo-gradient-norm adaptivity and links it to "gradient diversity" (Yin et al. 2018) and "client drift". These are the citable literature terms.
- **Adapting it to FedPer.** Use it with care:
  - Apply eta_g only to the shared trunk, since personalized stems and heads are not aggregated.
  - Use a weighted extension, sum w_i ||Delta_i||^2 / ||sum w_i Delta_i||^2 (still >= 1 by Jensen), to respect the hierarchical dataset weights.
  - Tune epsilon to interpolate toward FedAvg.
  - Average the last two global iterates.
- **Expected effect is uncertain.** The theory assumes a common minimizer across clients. That is questionable for clients from four imaging domains, and Eq. 6 may fail after the first rounds. If it does, extrapolation could hurt, amplify noise or raise the error floor.
- **What it does not show.** It gives no evidence on negative transfer between tasks or domains, or on federated vs local-only gaps. It is a convergence-speed method only, so it might close the gap only if the gap really comes from under-training of the shared trunk within the fixed round budget.
- **Interaction with the budget mode.** FedExP's eta_g depends on the per-client update norms, which in turn depend on the local budget mode (steps vs epochs). Any test must hold the budget mode fixed.

## TIES-Merging: Resolving Interference When Merging Models (2023)

- **Autores:** Prateek Yadav, Derek Tam, Leshem Choshen, Colin Raffel, Mohit Bansal
- **Venue:** NeurIPS 2023
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01708
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** True

**Termo usado.** "interference" between parameters (also "parameter interference"). It has two named sources: "Interference from redundant parameters" and "Interference from sign disagreement" ("sign conflict"). The paper never uses the words "weight cancellation". It describes the effect as aggregation that "shrinks the parameter's value".

**Definição (literal).** Abstract: "existing merging methods often ignore the interference between parameters of different models". Sec. 1 (Introduction): "interference can stem from two major causes" ... "Interference from redundant parameters" ... "Interference from sign disagreement". Sec. 1: "A given parameter might have a positive value for some models and a negative value for others." ... "simply aggregating the values lead to interference that shrinks the parameter's value in the merged model." Sec. 1, on both causes: "both of which can reduce parameter magnitudes in the merged model and eliminate subtle distinctions among values". Sec. 3 ("Disagreement between Parameter Signs"): "Different fine-tuned models might introduce opposing changes to a parameter in their task vectors, causing interference due to conflicting signs" ... "sign conflicts occur even when merging only 2 models from different tasks". Sec. 3 ("Redundancies in Model Parameters"): "in a given task vector, many values are redundant" ... "and removing them does not affect the performance of the task."

**Local.** Abstract; Section 1 (Introduction, the two causes of interference); Section 3 (Problem Setting: "Redundancies in Model Parameters", "Disagreement between Parameter Signs"; Figure 4); Section 4.2 (steps and equations); Appendix B.3; Appendix A (Limitations)

**Formulação.** Task vector (Sec. 3): tau_t = theta_ft^t - theta_init^t. Interference is defined in words, not by a formula. Sign conflict at parameter p: the trimmed task vectors {tau_hat_t^p} do not all have the same sign. The paper reports it as the fraction of parameters with a sign conflict, computed after trimming each task vector to its top 20% by magnitude. TIES steps (Sec. 4.2): (1) Trim: keep the top-k% entries of each tau_t by magnitude and set the rest to zero, giving tau_hat_t. (2) Elect sign: gamma_m^p = sgn(sum_t tau_hat_t^p), "the sign with greater total movement". (3) Disjoint merge: A_p = {t : sgn(tau_hat_t^p) = gamma_m^p}, and tau_m^p = (1/|A_p|) sum_{t in A_p} tau_hat_t^p. The zeros are ignored ("the disjoint mean always ignores the zero values"). The merged model is theta_m = theta_init + lambda * tau_m.

**Métrica.** The paper does not give a scalar interference score. It measures the problem in two ways. (a) Sign conflict: the percentage of parameters whose trimmed task vectors disagree in sign. Fig. 4 plots it for 2 to 11 merged models. It grows with the number of models and "reaches almost 80%" when less is trimmed (App. B.3). (b) Redundancy: how much task performance survives when only the top-k% of task-vector entries are kept (Fig. 3). The success measure is the merged model's downstream accuracy against single-task and multitask models. A further diagnostic (Sec. 7.4) replaces the elected sign with the sign of a multitask model ("oracle sign").

**Método.** The method merges several task-specific fine-tuned models that share one initialization into a single multitask model, without further training. It works on task vectors (fine-tuned minus initial weights) in three steps. Trim keeps the top-k% of each task vector by magnitude and zeroes the rest, which removes redundant small changes. Elect sign picks each parameter's sign by total signed mass. Disjoint merge averages only the entries that agree with the elected sign. This stops opposite-sign updates from cancelling each other and stops zeros from shrinking the mean.

**Resultados.** With a validation set, TIES beats the strongest baseline (task arithmetic, Fisher, RegMean, averaging) by an average of 2.3% absolute in NLP (T5) and 1.7% in vision (ViT). PEFT (IA3): +2.5% on average over 11 tasks. Without a validation set: +6.6% (ViT-B/32) and +2.7% (ViT-L/14). Out of domain: +1.0% (T5-base) and +4.4% (T5-large). Sign conflicts appear even between 2 models, persist after trimming and increase with the number of models. Ablations show every step contributes. Using the multitask model's signs (oracle) brings the merge close to multitask training (72.0 vs 73.1), so the authors conclude sign election is the main bottleneck.

**Limitações.** From Appendix A: "a limited theoretical understanding of why and when weight interpolation works"; "merging relies on common initialization and model architecture"; merging individual-task models "still lags behind the simultaneous multitask training"; and "using the signs from a multitask model performs better" than the elected signs. Further limits relevant to this project, which are my reading rather than the paper's: TIES is one-shot, post-hoc merging of fully fine-tuned models, not iterative aggregation of per-round deltas. The sign heuristic needs a hyperparameter k and a scaling lambda, normally tuned on validation data. The analysis is per coordinate, so it ignores how coordinates relate inside a layer. The paper reports no cancellation score in norm space.

**Relevância para o projeto.** TIES is the main per-coordinate analogue of the project's "weight cancellation". The project's metric 1 - ||sum w_i Delta_i|| / sum w_i ||Delta_i|| is a norm-level aggregate. TIES works per coordinate: it measures and removes sign conflicts, and it names a second source of shrinkage, redundant small updates averaged with zeros. Both can make the merged step smaller.
(1) Diagnostic. The project can compute the TIES statistic on client trunk deltas at each round, as a coordinate-level counterpart to the norm ratio. Two candidates: the fraction of trunk parameters with sign conflict across clients (optionally after a top-k trim), and the fraction of signed mass lost to opposite signs, sum_p (sum_i w_i|Delta_ip| - |sum_i w_i Delta_ip|) / sum_p sum_i w_i|Delta_ip|. Both can be split into intra- and inter-dataset parts.
(2) Interpretation. In high dimensions, low norm-level cancellation can coexist with many coordinate-level sign conflicts, and the orthogonal reference already reflects this. A shrunk aggregate step without strong inter-dataset cancellation is close to what TIES calls magnitude loss from averaging. The project's step-dilution finding (aggregated step about 10x smaller) may come partly from averaging near-orthogonal or sparse updates, not only from opposing ones. TIES's disjoint mean, which drops zeros and conflicting entries, or a rescaled average, is a direct mitigation to test.
(3) Caveats. TIES assumes one-shot merging from a common init. FedPer aggregates every round and shares only part of the model (the trunk). Sign election would be a new server-side aggregation rule; in FL there is related work on sign-based or masked aggregation. Any comparison must keep the same budget mode, and p-values stay exploratory.

## Editing Models with Task Arithmetic (2023)

- **Autores:** Gabriel Ilharco, Marco Tulio Ribeiro, Mitchell Wortsman, Suchin Gururangan, Ludwig Schmidt, Hannaneh Hajishirzi, Ali Farhadi
- **Venue:** ICLR 2023 (arXiv:2212.04089)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.04089
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "task vector"; "task arithmetic" (negation, addition, analogies); "interference" (used once, informally, in Sec. 6); "close to orthogonal" for task vectors. The paper never uses "cancellation" or "weight cancellation".

**Definição (literal).** Sec. 2 (Task Vectors), p.2: "For our purposes, a task is instantiated by a dataset and a loss function used for fine-tuning. Let θpre ∈ R^d be the weights of a pre-trained model, and θ^t_ft ∈ R^d the corresponding weights after fine-tuning on task t. The task vector τt ∈ R^d is given by the element-wise difference between θ^t_ft and θpre, i.e., τt = θ^t_ft − θpre." Sec. 6 (Discussion, 'Similarity between task vectors'), p.7: "We observe that vectors from different tasks are typically close to orthogonal, and speculate that this enables the combination of task vectors via addition with minimal interference. We also observe higher cosine similarities when tasks are semantically similar to each other." Figure 5 caption: "Task vectors are typically close to orthogonal."

**Local.** Sec. 2, p.2 (task vector definition and operations); Sec. 6 and Fig. 5, p.7 (cosine similarity, orthogonality, interference)

**Formulação.** τ_t = θ_ft^t − θ_pre. Applying a vector: θ_new = θ + λ τ_new, where λ is chosen on held-out validation sets. Negation: τ_new = −τ. Addition: τ_new = Σ_i τ_i. Analogy ("A is to B as C is to D"): τ_new = τ_C + (τ_B − τ_A). Similarity is measured as the cosine between flattened task vectors, cos(τ_i, τ_j) (Fig. 5). Normalized accuracy is the accuracy on a task divided by the accuracy of the model fine-tuned on that task.

**Métrica.** Interference is not measured directly. The paper uses (a) pairwise cosine similarity between task vectors (Fig. 5, CLIP, 8 vision tasks; off-diagonal values are mostly about 0.01 to 0.18), and (b) downstream proxies: the average normalized accuracy of the summed-vector model relative to the individual fine-tuned models (where 1.0 means no loss), and, for negation, the drop in target accuracy against the change in control (ImageNet) accuracy.

**Método.** The paper fine-tunes a shared pre-trained model (CLIP ViTs, T5, GPT-2) separately on each task and takes the weight difference as a task vector. Models are then edited with element-wise arithmetic on these vectors: negation for forgetting, a sum for multi-task models, and analogies for generalising to tasks with no data. A single scaling λ is tuned on validation data. A discussion section relates the success of addition to the near-orthogonality of the task vectors, and looks at learning rate and how the vectors evolve during fine-tuning.

**Resultados.** Negation: average target accuracy of ViT-L/14 drops by 45.8 pp (64.8 to 19.0), while ImageNet control accuracy only moves from 75.5 to 72.9. GPT-2 toxic generations fall from 4.8% to 0.8%, with control perplexity within 0.5 points. Addition: pairs of task vectors keep 98.9% normalized accuracy on average. Adding all 8 vision task vectors gives the best model 91.2% average normalized accuracy, so the loss grows as more vectors are summed. On GLUE, average accuracy goes from 78.1 to 78.6. Analogies: +3.4 pp on subpopulations with no target data. The cosines between task vectors are near zero, and the largest values occur for semantically related tasks (MNIST/SVHN/GTSRB, EuroSAT/RESISC45). Higher learning rates hurt task-vector addition more than they hurt individual fine-tuning. Intermediate task vectors align with the final direction quickly.

**Limitações.** (1) The link between near-orthogonality and "minimal interference" is stated as speculation ("speculate"). There is no formal definition or measurement of interference or cancellation. (2) The method needs the same architecture and the same pre-trained initialization. (3) The analysis is one-shot: each vector comes from full independent fine-tuning and they are merged once, not iteratively over rounds. (4) The models are large pre-trained models. Near-orthogonality may be partly an artifact of high dimensionality, and the paper offers no random or orthogonal baseline. (5) Sensitivity to learning rate is flagged ("recommend more caution"). (6) Negation works poorly when the behaviour is already strong in the pre-trained model (App. B.7). (7) The paper does not quantify how much the summed vector shrinks relative to its parts. The 8-task drop to 91.2% hints at a loss but does not attribute it.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is the formal object behind the project's metric. The client delta Δ_i (local trunk minus global trunk at the start of the round) is a per-round task vector, with the round's global trunk playing the role of θ_pre. FedAvg is weighted task-vector addition, θ_new = θ + Σ w_i Δ_i, with the scaling fixed at λ = 1. The paper's evidence that task vectors are near-orthogonal (cosines around 0.01 to 0.18, higher for related tasks) supports reading the project's cancellation ratio 1 − ||Σ w_iΔ_i|| / Σ w_i||Δ_i|| against an orthogonal reference rather than against zero. Near-orthogonal deltas do not cancel in sign, but their weighted sum is still much shorter than the weighted sum of their norms: for n equal-norm orthogonal vectors with uniform weights, the norm of the mean is 1/√n of each. That is consistent with the project finding little cancellation beyond orthogonal between datasets while the aggregated step is about 10x smaller ("step dilution"). Task arithmetic sidesteps this with a sum (λ tuned on validation, effectively λ > 1/n), not an average, which suggests a server learning rate or rescaling (FedOpt-style) as a remedy. The finding that semantically related tasks have higher cosine predicts that intra-dataset deltas align more than inter-dataset ones, which matches the intra/inter split the project measures. Caveats: the paper's setting is one-shot merging from a shared pretrained init. The project's deltas are short, iterative and from a trunk trained from scratch, and the paper does not define cancellation, so it should be cited for the task-vector formalism and the near-orthogonality observation, not as a source of a cancellation definition.

## Task Arithmetic in the Tangent Space: Improved Editing of Pre-Trained Models (2023)

- **Autores:** Guillermo Ortiz-Jimenez, Alessandro Favero, Pascal Frossard (equal contribution of first two)
- **Venue:** NeurIPS 2023 (oral). Read from arXiv v3, 21 Nov 2023
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12827
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** False

**Termo usado.** "weight disentanglement" (Property 3); "task arithmetic property" (Property 1); "disentanglement error" xi(alpha1, alpha2) (Eq. 6); "eigenfunction localization" (Sec. 6.1). The paper never uses "weight cancellation" or "interference" as a defined term.

**Definição (literal).** Sec. 4, Property 3 (Weight disentanglement): "A parametric function f : X × Θ → Y is weight disentangled with respect to a set of task vectors T = {τ_t}_{t∈[T]} and the corresponding supports D = {D_t}_{t∈[T]} if f(x, θ0 + Σ_{t=1}^T α_t τ_t) = Σ_{t=1}^T g_t(x, α_t τ_t) + g_0(x), (4) where g_t(x, α_t τ_t) = 0 for x ∉ D_t and t = 1, …, T, and g_0(x) = 0 for x ∈ ⋃_{t∈[T]} D_t. In essence, this definition captures the idea that the function f can be decomposed as a sum of spatially-localized components, i.e., vanishing outside a spatial region, whose functional variation is entirely captured by each τ_t." Also Sec. 4: "we argue that the only necessary condition to perform task arithmetic with a model f is that the model is weight disentangled with respect to the set of fine-tuning tasks." And: "this additive decomposition does not imply linearity ... weight disentanglement is a property of the predictors and not related to the performance on different tasks." Sec. 2, Property 1: "In short, a model satisfies Property 1 if adding τ_t does not modify the output of the model outside D_t."

**Local.** Section 2 (Property 1, Eq. 1: task arithmetic); Section 4 (Property 3, Eq. 4-5: weight disentanglement; Eq. 6: disentanglement error); Section 6.2 (emergence during pre-training, Table 3); Section 8 (Conclusion)

**Formulação.** Task vector: τ_t = θ_t* − θ0 (fine-tuned minus pre-trained weights). Task supports D_t ⊂ X are non-intersecting. Property 1 (task arithmetic, Eq. 1): f(x, θ0 + Σ_t α_t τ_t) = f(x, θ0 + α_t τ_t) if x ∈ D_t; = f(x, θ0) if x ∉ ∪_t D_t, for (α_1..α_T) ∈ A ⊆ R^T. Property 3 (weight disentanglement, Eq. 4): f(x, θ0 + Σ_t α_t τ_t) = Σ_t g_t(x, α_t τ_t) + g_0(x), with g_t = 0 outside D_t and g_0 = 0 on ∪_t D_t (shown equivalent to Property 1 via Eq. 5). Disentanglement error (Eq. 6): ξ(α1, α2) = Σ_{t=1}^{2} E_{x∼μ_t}[ dist( f(x, θ0 + α_t τ_t), f(x, θ0 + α1 τ1 + α2 τ2) ) ], with dist(y1, y2) = 1(y1 ≠ y2) (prediction disagreement) for classification. Linearized model: f_lin(x, θ) = f(x, θ0) + (θ − θ0)^T ∇_θ f(x, θ0); the method fine-tunes f_lin directly to get τ_lin. Proposition 1 (kernel view): a kernel can do task arithmetic if each task is represented by a subset of NTK eigenfunctions localized in that task's support.

**Métrica.** Functional, not geometric. (1) Disentanglement error ξ(α1, α2) (Eq. 6): the rate of prediction disagreement on each task's own data between the single-task-edited model and the jointly edited model, plotted as a heatmap over a grid of (α1, α2). (2) Task addition: absolute and normalized multi-task accuracy (normalized by single-task fine-tuned accuracy) over 8 vision tasks. (3) Task negation: target-task accuracy after subtracting τ while keeping 95% of control-task (ImageNet) accuracy. (4) Eigenfunction localization: local energy Σ_ρ φ_ρ(x)^2 of NTK eigenfunctions over training vs. control supports. The paper does not use cosine similarity or norm-based cancellation as a measure of interference.

**Método.** The paper formalizes task arithmetic (Ilharco et al.) as a property and shows that the NTK/linear regime is not required to explain it for non-linear CLIP models. It then names weight disentanglement as the necessary condition and measures it with the disentanglement error. To get more of it, the method fine-tunes in the tangent space: it trains the first-order Taylor linearization f_lin around θ0 (via Jacobian-vector products) and uses the resulting τ_lin for addition and negation. It also explains disentanglement through NTK eigenfunctions that are localized per task, and shows that the property comes from pre-training by repeating the experiments from random initialization.

**Resultados.** Task addition over 8 tasks, absolute accuracy (non-linear FT vs linearized FT): ViT-B/32 71.4 vs 76.5; ViT-B/16 75.5 vs 81.3; ViT-L/14 85.1 vs 88.5. Normalized accuracy: 76.5 vs 85.4, 80.0 vs 86.0, 88.8 vs 93.5. Task negation, target accuracy (lower is better): 24.0 vs 10.9, 19.2 vs 11.3, 18.0 vs 7.9. Headline claim: "up to 5.8 points more and 13.1 points less in accuracy on task addition and task negation". Post-hoc linearization is more disentangled (higher normalized accuracy) but worse in absolute terms because its single-task accuracy is lower. Disentanglement error is low only in a small neighbourhood of θ0 (α ≤ 1) and grows for α1, α2 > 1, which explains why task arithmetic works best with small learning rates, few steps and optimal α below 1. From random initialization, task addition stays at chance (ViT-B/32: single-task 48.5, multi-task 5.5; random-init accuracy 5.3), so "task arithmetic is not achievable on randomly initialized ViTs" and "task arithmetic is a property acquired during pre-training". The paper also reports that weight disentanglement in non-linear models "cannot be explained by their NTK". There is some evidence beyond CLIP ViTs: a ConvNeXt, a closed-vocabulary ImageNet ViT (weaker), and text models in App. D.7.

**Limitações.** There is no dedicated limitations section. (1) It is a model-merging/editing setting: one-shot addition of task vectors fine-tuned independently from one strong pre-trained checkpoint (CLIP), not iterative aggregation over many rounds. (2) It assumes task supports D_t do not intersect, i.e. disjoint input domains. (3) Mostly classification with a frozen zero-shot head; no segmentation and no dense-prediction tasks. (4) Linearized training doubles memory and roughly doubles gradient cost; inference is about 2-3x a forward pass. Linearized models lose single-task accuracy, and the paper says "this linear approximation is often invalid at finite widths". (5) Disentanglement holds only near θ0 (small α, small learning rate, few steps). (6) The disentanglement error is measured for task pairs on a 2-D α grid, not for many tasks at once. (7) How disentanglement arises during pre-training is left as an open question. Caveat on the orchestrator's pick rationale: the paper does NOT explicitly discuss weight orthogonality or cosine similarity of task vectors (grep of the full text finds "cosine" only for the LR schedule). The claim that "orthogonality alone is insufficient" is an inference from its purely functional definition, not a statement in the paper.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This is a conceptual correction to how the project's cancellation metric 1 − ||Σ w_i Δ_i|| / Σ w_i ||Δ_i|| and pairwise cosines can be read. Those metrics are weight-space geometry. This paper defines non-interference functionally: adding one task's update must not change outputs on the other task's input support. On that definition, near-orthogonal client deltas (low cancellation, near the orthogonal_reference) do not show that client updates are disentangled. A BUSI-trunk update can still change ISIC features, even though the domains are disjoint, which is the paper's D_t assumption. A project analogue of Eq. 6 would be: evaluate client t's data with (global trunk) vs (trunk + only Δ_t) and measure output/Dice/prediction disagreement. That would be a functional interference probe to complement cancellation. Three findings bear directly on the project. (a) Disentanglement is absent at random init and is a product of pre-training. The default MTnnUNet trunk trains from scratch, so averaging domain-specific updates is not expected to compose well. This supports the E7 ImageNet-pretrained MTResNet34UNet arm as a test. (b) Disentanglement holds only near θ0, with small steps and α < 1. Large local budgets (many steps_per_round on small clients) push updates out of the disentangled region, and FedAvg's weighted mean acts like α_t = w_i < 1 per client. That ties directly to the step-dilution finding: averaging shrinks each client's effective step (α ≈ w_i), much like task-vector scaling. In the regime where updates are not disentangled, increasing α (a server learning rate) to undo the dilution would bring back interference. (c) Linearized/tangent-space fine-tuning is a possible lever but not a direct fit: the setting is one-shot merging rather than multi-round FL, and there are no segmentation results. Overall, the paper is a definitional reference for "interference" (functional) as distinct from "cancellation" (geometric), not a SOTA FL method.

## Localizing Task Information for Improved Model Merging and Compression (2024)

- **Autores:** Ke Wang, Nikolaos Dimitriadis, Guillermo Ortiz-Jimenez, Francois Fleuret, Pascal Frossard
- **Venue:** ICML 2024 (PMLR 235); arXiv 2405.07813
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07813
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** True

**Termo usado.** "task interference" (as opposed to "information erasure"); "selfish weights" (used by only one task); "catastrophic weights" (used by no task); also "universal weights" (used by all tasks). The paper does not use the words "cancellation" or "weight cancellation". It mentions sign disagreement only through TIES (Yadav et al. 2023).

**Definição (literal).** Section 3: "To better understand the causes for performance degradation, we make two hypotheses: Information erasure: large amount of information specific to each task is erased when merging the multi-task vector. Task interference: the task-specific information is preserved in the multi-task vector, but can not manifest properly due to interference between the tasks." Section 4: "all the information embedded in the original checkpoints is not erased but rather preserved in the multi-task vector." Section 5.2: "weights used by only one task, which we term selfish weights" and "a subset of parameters which are used by no task at all, which we term catastrophic weights". Section 7: the degradation "is not due to information erasure during merging process, but because of task interference during evaluation."

**Local.** Section 3 (the two hypotheses); Section 4 with Eqs. 1-5 (TALL masks); Section 5.2 with Eq. 8 (consensus mask, selfish and catastrophic weights); Section 7 (conclusion)

**Formulação.** Task vector: tau_t = theta_t - theta_0. Multi-task vector: tau_MTL = sum_t tau_t (Section 3). Per-task reconstruction (Eq. 1): theta_hat_t = theta_0 + m_t ∘ tau_MTL ≈ theta_t. Mask objective (Eqs. 2-3): m_t* = argmin over m_t in {0,1}^P of ||m_t ∘ tau_MTL - tau_t||_1. It decomposes per coordinate (Appendix B, Eqs. 13-14), giving Eq. 4: m_t = 1{|tau_t| >= |tau_MTL - tau_t|}. The TALL mask with a tunable lambda_t (Eq. 5): m_t = 1{|tau_t| >= |tau_MTL - tau_t| * lambda_t}, with lambda_t chosen from {0.2,...,0.6} on validation. The consensus mask (Eq. 8): m_consensus = 1{sum_t m_t >= k}, with k=2 by default. Consensus merging applies m_consensus to tau_MTL (or to the TIES vector) before scaling and adding it to theta_0. Normalized accuracy (Appendix A.1, Eq. 10): the mean over tasks of acc(merged)/acc(fine-tuned). Implicit cancellation reading: coordinate p "belongs" to task t when the task's own update |tau_t,p| is at least as large as the sum of the other tasks' updates at that coordinate, |tau_MTL,p - tau_t,p|. A coordinate where the other tasks dominate or cancel the task's update is masked out for that task.

**Métrica.** Mostly downstream performance. (1) Normalized accuracy (merged/fine-tuned) of the multi-task vector with per-task TALL masks applied: if it reaches about 100%, the information was not erased. (2) A controlled experiment on the 8-task ViT benchmark: each task fine-tunes a random disjoint, equal-size subset of weights, so the task vectors partition the weight space. Task arithmetic still degrades there, which isolates interference from erasure. (3) A weight profile: for each parameter, the number of tasks whose TALL mask selects it, giving the fractions of catastrophic (0 tasks), selfish (1), and universal (all) weights. (4) Absolute and normalized accuracy of consensus merging, and storage in Gb. There is no geometric or norm-based cancellation metric (no cosine, no ||sum|| / sum||.||).

**Método.** TALL masks are per-task binary masks found in closed form: they keep the coordinates of the merged task vector where the task's own update dominates the sum of the others'. Applying them to the single multi-task vector recovers each fine-tuned model. This supports compression: one merged vector plus T binary masks instead of T checkpoints. Consensus Merging is a training-free add-on to Task Arithmetic or TIES. It removes catastrophic weights (no task's mask selects them) and selfish weights (only one task selects them), keeping only weights that at least k=2 tasks find important. It then merges as usual. Masks are built with access to the individual task vectors, i.e. a central merger rather than a federated setting.

**Resultados.** The per-task masks retrieve more than 99% of single-task accuracy from the multi-task vector. Compression: the 20-task ViT-L/14 setting drops from 57 Gb to 8.2 Gb (13.7% of storage) while keeping 99.7% of performance, and TALL Mask + TIES stays above 99% normalized accuracy in all cases. Consensus TA on ViT-L/14 with 20 tasks: 78.9% vs 74.0% for TA and 75.7% for TIES (+4.9 and +1.1 points). ViT-B/32 (Consensus TA / TA / TIES): 8 tasks 75.0/70.8/75.1, 14 tasks 70.4/65.4/68.0, 20 tasks 65.4/60.6/63.4. NLP (T5): 8 QA tasks, Consensus TIES 69.1 vs TIES 62.8 (+6.3); 11 tasks, Consensus TA 67.5 vs 63.6. The gains grow with the number of tasks and are close to zero at 8 tasks on ViT-B/32, where Consensus TIES is slightly below TIES. In the controlled disjoint-support experiment, task arithmetic still degrades even though the task vectors share no coordinates, i.e. even with zero cancellation. The paper reads this as task interference being the culprit. Weight profiles show that TIES pruning and sign resolution shift weights toward universal and selfish.

**Limitações.** There is no dedicated limitations section. Storing per-task masks costs more than plain task arithmetic (Section 5.2). The threshold k "affects performance and depends on the task number and combination" (Section 5.2), and Section 6.5 reports that Consensus TIES does best at k=1. lambda_t is tuned per task on validation data. Everything is one-shot merging of independently fine-tuned checkpoints from one pretrained model (CLIP ViT, T5). There is no iterative or federated aggregation, no personalized heads, and no domain shift beyond the tasks' natural differences. "Information is preserved" is shown only as recoverability through an oracle mask built from the original task vectors, which a merged model without masks cannot use. Interference is defined operationally (information present but not manifesting) rather than measured geometrically. Cancellation is not quantified.

**Relevância para o projeto.** The paper gives a citable, authoritative split between two failure modes of aggregating task updates. (a) "Information erasure": in the project's terms, cancellation, where conflicting updates annihilate in the sum. (b) "Task interference": the information survives in the sum but the wrong coordinates (selfish or catastrophic) hurt each task when evaluated. Its controlled experiment with disjoint task supports, which has zero cancellation by construction, still degrades under task arithmetic. That matches the project's finding that low inter-dataset cancellation does not mean harmless aggregation. Its per-coordinate rule |tau_t| >= |tau_MTL - tau_t| is a coordinate-wise counterpart to the project's global ratio 1 - ||sum w_i Delta_i|| / sum w_i ||Delta_i||. The project could compute per-coordinate "ownership" or a selfish/universal profile of trunk deltas per round to see whether its trunk updates are mostly selfish (dataset-specific), which would point to interference and dilution rather than cancellation. Step dilution is a related but distinct point. Task arithmetic sums the task vectors and tunes a scaling coefficient, while FedAvg averages them. Averaging updates with mostly disjoint or orthogonal support shrinks each client's effective step, roughly by its weight. The paper's framing explains why removing selfish weights (consensus) helps a merged model, but it does not deal with step magnitude. Caveats for transfer: one-shot merging of fully fine-tuned checkpoints, not multi-round FedAvg; no personalized stems or heads (FedPer already personalizes the task-specific parts); a natural-image/NLP domain. Its "interference" terminology is not the same as "negative transfer" in MTL or FL.

## Task Singular Vectors: Reducing Task Interference in Model Merging (2025)

- **Autores:** Antonio Andrea Gargiulo, Donato Crisostomi, Maria Sofia Bucarelli, Simone Scardapane, Fabrizio Silvestri, Emanuele Rodola
- **Venue:** CVPR 2025 (arXiv 2412.00081)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.00081
- **Acesso:** full_text · **confiança:** high · **candidato a SOTA:** True

**Termo usado.** "task interference"; "Singular Task Interference (STI)"; "inter-task interactions"; "destructive overlaps" (App. E.2). The paper does not use "cancellation" for this; it frames interference as overlap between per-task singular subspaces.

**Definição (literal).** Sec. 3.3: "We hereby introduce a score of task interference based on the interplay of TSVs from different tasks, which we term Singular Task Interference (STI) [...] (6) where U, Σ and V are obtained by concatenating the singular value decompositions [...] of per-layer task matrices [...]. In the expression above, high inner product values for U⊤U and V⊤V imply a higher likelihood of interference, with minimal interference ideally yielding identity matrices. The underlying intuition is that overlapping singular vectors suggest shared features in the weight space across tasks. Such overlap can introduce interference when models are merged, ultimately degrading performance on individual tasks." App. E.2 (Heuristic interference measure): "Given that a formal definition of interference in model merging is not yet established, we adopt an operational definition: interference is any cross-task interaction that hinders the merging process." Sec. 1: "This flattened view limits these approaches to coarse-grained measures like cosine similarity for assessing inter-task interactions."

**Local.** Sec. 3.3, Eq. (6); Appendix E.2; Introduction (Sec. 1)

**Formulação.** Task matrix per layer l: Δ_i^(l) = θ_ft_i^(l) − θ_pre^(l); merged θ_MT^(l) = θ_pre^(l) + α Σ_i Δ_i^(l) / T (Eq. 2). SVD Δ_i = U_i Σ_i V_i^T. Concatenate U = [U_1..U_T], Σ = blockdiag(Σ_1..Σ_T), V = [V_1..V_T], so Σ_i Δ_i = U Σ V^T (Eq. 3). Low-rank: Δ̂_i = Σ_{j=1}^{k} σ_j^i u_j^i (v_j^i)^T (Eq. 4), k = rank/T. STI({Δ_i}) = || (U^T U − I) Σ (V^T V − I) ||_1 (Eq. 6) — zero when singular vectors of different tasks are mutually orthogonal. TSV-M: replace U, V by nearest orthogonal matrices via Procrustes, min ||U⊥ − Û||_F s.t. U⊥^T U⊥ = I, solution U⊥ = P Q^T with Û = P D Q^T (Eq. 8), equivalent to whitening X(X^T X)^{-1/2} (Prop. 4.1); M̂ = U⊥ Σ V⊥^T, θ_MT = θ_pre + α M̂. Theorem 6.1 bounds approximation error for T > 4 under a rank condition on k.

**Métrica.** STI (Eq. 6), computed per layer (matrix-valued weights) from the concatenated per-task SVD factors; reported per transformer layer/block (Fig. 6, Fig. 14) and before/after merging alongside normalized accuracy (Fig. 8, 13, 15). Merge quality measured by absolute and normalized accuracy (merged / individually fine-tuned) on 8/14/20 vision tasks.

**Método.** Treats each layer's weight difference (task matrix) as a matrix rather than flattening to a vector, computes its SVD (Task Singular Vectors), and observes task matrices are low-rank. TSV-Compress keeps only ~1/T of each task's top singular components (task-specific, needs known/routed task). TSV-Merge truncates each task to its top 1/T components, concatenates them, and decorrelates (whitens / orthogonal Procrustes) the stacked left and right singular vectors across tasks before summing, which drives STI toward zero; training-free, single alpha.

**Resultados.** Table 2 (abs. acc, normalized in parentheses), TSV-M vs Task Arithmetic: ViT-B/32 8/14/20 tasks 85.86 (92.31) / 80.06 (87.88) / 77.07 (84.29) vs TA 70.79 / 65.32 / 60.52; ViT-B/16 89.01 / 84.58 / 80.57; ViT-L/14 92.98 (96.98) / 89.17 / 87.72. ~+15 pts over TA and ~+10.7 over Consensus TA on ViT-B/32. TSV-C retains >99% of fine-tuned accuracy (e.g. 92.62 vs 92.83 on ViT-B/32, 8 tasks), comparable to TALL-Masks. Ablation (Table 4, ViT-B/32): interference reduction (orthogonalization) alone +3.6 to +7.4 pts; low-rank alone slightly below TA; both combined +9.7 to +14.4 pts; orthogonalizing full-rank matrices incurs large approximation error. Per-layer STI is highest in early transformer layers (shared, generic features) and decreases in deeper, specialized layers (Sec. 6.2). Lower STI after TSV-M co-occurs with higher accuracy (Sec. 6.4).

**Limitações.** Interference is explicitly only an operational/heuristic notion; STI is validated only by its correlation with merge accuracy, and the authors call TSV-M "more empirical" than theoretical (App. E.1/E.2). Setting is one-shot, training-free model merging of independently fine-tuned CLIP ViT models from a common pretrained checkpoint on vision classification benchmarks only — not iterative FL rounds, not segmentation, not CNNs/medical imaging. Uniform rank 1/T per task (adaptive rank left to future work). TSV-C requires task identity / a router. Theorem holds only for T > 4 and a rank bound. Compression claims ("10% size" vs "~2x original model storage") are not clearly reconciled. Orthogonalizing changes each task's update direction, so it trades fidelity for decorrelation. No dedicated limitations section.

**Relevância para o projeto.** Gives a structure-aware alternative to the project's flat measures (cancellation ratio 1 − ||Σ w_iΔ_i|| / Σ w_i||Δ_i|| and pairwise cosine of flattened trunk deltas), which the paper explicitly calls "coarse-grained". STI can be computed per conv layer of the shared trunk (reshape conv kernels to out × in·k·k matrices) from per-client deltas each round, with intra/inter-dataset splits by taking the off-diagonal blocks of U^T U and V^T V between clients of the same or different datasets. Important caveat: STI measures subspace OVERLAP, which is not the same as cancellation — two updates can overlap in the same singular directions and add constructively (sign of σ / alignment of u·v pairs matters), so STI captures potential interference, while the norm ratio captures realized magnitude loss. That distinction fits the project's finding: low inter-dataset cancellation plus near-orthogonal flattened deltas suggest updates live in different subspaces, in which case STI would also be low, and the observed cost is not conflict but averaging an update over T roughly orthogonal directions, shrinking each one's contribution ~1/T (step dilution). TSV-M addresses exactly this regime in merging: when subspaces are (made) orthogonal, the sum can be kept without dividing by T, i.e. the merged update preserves each task's component at full scale — a direct hint for remedies to step dilution (e.g. orthogonalized/low-rank aggregation of trunk deltas, or rescaling the aggregated step when STI is low). Their per-layer finding (interference concentrated in early, shared layers) motivates reporting the project's cancellation/cosine per encoder block. Transfer is limited: one-shot merging of fine-tuned ViTs vs many FedAvg rounds from scratch on small medical CNN data, and no notion of client weighting w_i.

Moreover, we show that these conflicts manifest in insufficient global average updates and mutual constraints between clients."

**Local.** Abstract; Sec. 3.2 "Conflicting Client Updates" (Eq. 2); Sec. 3.3 "Server Aggregation Conflicts" (Eqs. 3-4), pp. 15446-15447. The "conflict rate" plotted in Fig. 1b and Fig. 4 is defined only in the supplementary appendix, which I did not read.

**Formulação.** Setup (Eq. 1): FedRep/FedPer-style split. A shared representation θ_G and personalized heads h_i, with min_{θ_G} Σ_i p_i min_{h_i} L_i(h_i, θ_G; D_i) and p_i = |D_i|/Σ|D_j|. Client update: Δθ_i = −α∇_θ L_i. Server update: Δθ_avg = Σ_i p_i Δθ_i.
Pairwise effect (Eq. 2, first-order Taylor): ΔL_j = L_j(h_j, θ_G + Δθ_i) − L_j(h_j, θ_G) = −(1/α) Δθ_i·Δθ_j + o(||Δθ_i||). A conflict means Δθ_i·Δθ_j < 0.
Layer-wise sum (Eq. 3): ΔL_j = Σ_{k=1}^K ΔL_j^(k) = −(1/α) Σ_k Δθ_i^(k)·Δθ_j^(k).
Total aggregation effect (Eq. 4): ΔL_total = Σ_i Σ_{j≠i} [−(1/α) Σ_k p_i p_j Δθ_i^(k)·Δθ_j^(k)] = −(1/α) Σ_k ( ||Δθ_avg^(k)||² − Σ_i p_i² ||Δθ_i^(k)||² ). The global loss increases when the gap ||Δθ_avg^(k)||² − Σ_i p_i²||Δθ_i^(k)||² is negative, which is the weighted sum of cross inner products.
Mitigation stage 1 (Eq. 5, PCGrad-style, per layer): Δθ_⊥^(k) = (1/N) Σ_i [ Δθ_i^(k) − Σ_{j≠i, cos θ_ij^(k)<0} (Δθ_i^(k)·Δθ_j^(k) / ||Δθ_j^(k)||²) Δθ_j^(k) ].
Mitigation stage 2 (Eq. 6, soft-margin/SVM-like QP): min_{d,ξ} ½||d − Δθ_⊥||² + C Σ_i ξ_i s.t. ε||Δθ_i|| − dᵀΔθ_i ≤ ξ_i and ξ_i ≥ 0. Dual (Eq. 8): max_λ ε Σ λ_i||Δθ_i|| − Σ λ_i Δθ_⊥ᵀΔθ_i − ½ ΣΣ λ_iλ_j Δθ_iᵀΔθ_j with 0 ≤ λ_i ≤ C. Solution: d* = Δθ_⊥ + Σ λ_i* Δθ_i, then θ_G ← θ_G + d*, solved independently per layer (Alg. 1).

**Métrica.** There are two diagnostics. (1) The analytical quantity, per layer: the squared-norm gap ||Σ p_iΔθ_i||² − Σ p_i²||Δθ_i||², which equals Σ_{i≠j} p_i p_j Δθ_i·Δθ_j. Negative values mean aggregation-induced loss increase (Eq. 4). The paper presents it as a theoretical decomposition and does not report it as an empirical curve. (2) The empirical metric is a "conflict rate (%)" per round. Its exact formula is in the appendix, which I did not read. The main text implies it counts conflicting client-update pairs or proportions (negative cosine). It is plotted against base-model depth (Fig. 1b, FedRep on three datasets) and against rounds (Fig. 4, Flowers102, N=20/60, P=30/60%). The task metric is average personalized test accuracy.

**Método.** FedCALM is a two-stage, layer-wise server aggregation rule for personalized FL with a shared body and personal heads. Stage 1 runs per layer: if two clients' updates have negative cosine, each update's component along the other is projected out (PCGrad applied across clients), and the results are averaged into a "conflict-free" update Δθ_⊥. Stage 2 (CALM) solves a small convex QP per layer, through its dual. The QP finds the update d closest to Δθ_⊥ such that every client gets at least a positive contribution dᵀΔθ_i ≥ ε||Δθ_i||, with slack variables penalized by C. The result is d* = Δθ_⊥ + Σλ_iΔθ_i. Through the KKT conditions, only conflicting clients get λ_i > 0, which the authors call a "selective mechanism". CALM can be used standalone (with FedPAC's classifier combination) or plugged into FedRep, FedALA, FedCP and FedPAC.

**Resultados.** - Motivating observation (Fig. 1): accuracy of FedRep, FedALA, FedCP and FedPAC falls as the base model goes from a 4-layer CNN to ResNet4/10/18/34 on CIFAR10. The FedRep conflict rate rises with depth on three datasets. The authors call this "deep model degradation".
- Table 1, Flowers102: FedCALM gets 75.57 / 80.24 / 75.80 / 71.14% with ResNet4/10/18/34, beating the next-best method by 9.88 / 3.16 / 1.59 / 4.57 points. On CIFAR100 the margins are +5.31 / 0.52 / 2.51 / 3.67. With the shallow CNN, FedCALM is worse than FedPAC (63.11 vs 68.32 on Flowers102; 54.68 vs 61.65 on CIFAR100).
- Adding CALM helps every baseline: with the CNN, +0.26 to +3.54 (Table 2); with ResNet10, up to +9.01 (FedCP on Flowers102, 70.80 to 79.81) and +6.41 in the pathological setting (Table 4).
- Scalability (Table 3, ResNet10): best in all N ∈ {20, 60} × P ∈ {30, 60}% cells, e.g. CIFAR100 N=60 gives 76.21 / 75.06 vs FedPAC 69.46 / 70.96.
- Conflict-rate dynamics (Fig. 4): for all methods the conflict rate dips early, then rises sharply as higher-level features form, then plateaus. FedCALM has the lowest rate with N=60. With N=20 it deliberately tolerates some conflict.
- Overhead (Table 5): negligible extra compute.
- 3 seeds, means only, no variance or significance tests. Setup: 20 clients, 500 rounds, E=1, batch size 10, SGD lr 0.005.

**Limitações.** - Classification only, on natural-image benchmarks (CIFAR10/100, CINIC10, Flowers102), with synthetic label skew (Dirichlet β=0.1, pathological). There is no domain/feature shift, no cross-modality setting, no multi-task, no segmentation and no medical data.
- The conflict rate behind the central depth claim (Fig. 1b, Fig. 4) is defined only in the appendix.
- The theory is a first-order Taylor expansion that assumes Δθ_j = −α∇L_j, i.e. one gradient step. Local training actually uses multiple steps, and heads are trained before the body.
- Eq. 4 attributes loss increase only to the cross terms. Under mutually orthogonal updates the gap is 0, so "insufficient average update magnitude" is reduced to a by-product of conflict. A small step caused by averaging near-orthogonal updates (dilution) is not flagged as a problem by their measure.
- Eq. 5 uses uniform 1/N weights while FedAvg uses p_i, and the QP output d* = Δθ_⊥ + Σλ_iΔθ_i can have a larger norm than plain FedAvg. Part of the gain may therefore come from an effectively larger server step. There is no control against server-learning-rate or extrapolation baselines such as FedExP, and no ablation separating stage 1 from stage 2.
- Per-layer QPs plus pairwise projections cost O(N²) per layer, which is fine for 20-60 clients.
- The claimed reduction in communication overhead is weakly justified.
- FedCALM underperforms with shallow models. The "deep model degradation" explanation is asserted from correlation, not tested causally.

**Relevância para o projeto.** This paper is very relevant on two counts: the formal definition, and the step-dilution interpretation.
(1) Their Eq. 4 quantity, G = ||Σ w_iΔ_i||² − Σ w_i²||Δ_i||² = Σ_{i≠j} w_i w_j Δ_i·Δ_j, is the squared-norm counterpart of the project's cancellation ratio CR = 1 − ||Σ w_iΔ_i|| / Σ w_i||Δ_i||. G = 0 exactly when the weighted cross terms vanish, e.g. for mutually orthogonal updates. In that case CR equals the project's orthogonal_reference, 1 − sqrt(Σ w_i²||Δ_i||²) / Σ w_i||Δ_i||. So the sign of G is the sign of (orthogonal_reference − CR): "excess cancellation over the orthogonal reference" in the project is exactly FedCALM's "server aggregation conflict" (G < 0). This gives a citable formal anchor for reading CR against orthogonal_reference, and for reporting G (or a normalized G) per block alongside CR. Their layer-wise decomposition (Eq. 3) also matches the project's per-block cosine logging.
(2) FedCALM names "insufficient average update magnitude" as one source of loss increase. Their analysis ties it to conflict, though. The project's finding (little inter-dataset excess cancellation but a ~10x smaller aggregated step) is the case their Eq. 4 treats as harmless (G ≈ 0, near-orthogonal high-dimensional updates). This is a gap the project can articulate: magnitude loss from averaging near-orthogonal updates (step dilution) is distinct from directional conflict, and conflict-centric methods like FedCALM do not diagnose it. Because d* can be larger than the FedAvg step, FedCALM's gains may partly be a dilution remedy, so a server-LR or FedExP-style control is needed when comparing.
(3) The setting matches closely. Their objective (Eq. 1) is FedRep/FedPer (shared body, personal heads), and they argue deeper shared bodies increase conflict, which is relevant to how far up the project's shared trunk goes (E6 wide sharing).
(4) The paper's evidence comes from label-skew classification on natural images. Its conclusions do not transfer automatically to the project's cross-domain (ultrasound / dermoscopy / MRI / X-ray), multi-task seg+cls FedPer. Treat it as a definitional and methodological reference and a candidate aggregation baseline (CALM as a plug-in), not as evidence about medical cross-domain FL.
